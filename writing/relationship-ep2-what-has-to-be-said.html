23:14. Forty minutes ago you wrote that you missed them. There's no green dot beside it, no "last seen", nothing. X is merciful and cruel at once - it tells you absolutely nothing. The chat looks exactly the same whether they read your message and put the phone down, or whether the phone is lying in the hallway while they're in the shower.

You simply don't know. They're working, they fell asleep, they're cooking pasta - all equally likely, and none of it written anywhere.

That's the price. Not the distance, not the time zones, not even the absence of skin. The price is that the other person only exists while they're typing. In between, they're a guess. And nothing helps against guesswork except sentences that make it unnecessary.

Sad, Angry, Tired, Hurt, Turned On

The last part was about meeting someone. This one is about what comes after, and it's harder, because it doesn't stop.

Sad, angry, tired, hurt, turned on: five completely different states that look identical online. Namely, like short answers. Or like none.

My own mistake was a different one and far less dramatic: I didn't always tell people everything. Sometimes out of laziness. Sometimes because the other person was in a good mood and I didn't want to spoil it. Sometimes because I didn't want to deal with it myself. Three very understandable reasons, one identical effect.

That's changed now. Not because I've become a better person, but because at some point I saw what it costs.

If you only send the good parts, you're letting someone share in one half. And loving someone doesn't mean loving them in the good times, it means loving all their sides - the tired one, the miserable one, the boring one. Online, transparency isn't a character trait, it's the precondition. Without it the other person can't participate in your life, they can only be given a summary of it afterwards.

So this doesn't land wrong: transparency doesn't mean disclosing everything. You owe nobody a complete account. There are things that belong to you alone - old business, other people's secrets, thoughts that are nobody's concern - and they're allowed to stay yours.

What's meant is narrower: whatever concerns the two of you belongs out in the open between you. What you feel. How you're doing in this relationship. What's currently on your mind heavily enough that it's bleeding into the two of you - even if you keep the reason to yourself.

The distinction matters, because confusing the two gets expensive. Mistake transparency for full disclosure and sooner or later you arrive at accountability, and that isn't closeness any more, that's bookkeeping.

Saying It Replaces the Body

The compensation for the missing body isn't more text. It's more direct text.

Offline you can imply, because everything else runs alongside. Online you have to say it. Not more beautifully, not more poetically - just explicitly.

"I'm short today, that's exhaustion and not you."
"I'm angry right now, give me an hour and I'll talk."
"I'm reading, I just don't have words."
"I'm going to sleep, not going away."

At first it feels awkward, roughly like subtitling your own life. After two weeks it feels like safety. The sentence "that's exhaustion and not you" has prevented more arguments in my relationships than any apology afterwards ever did.

And yes, it takes some of the mystery out. Good. Mystery is cheap online, you've got plenty. What's scarce is certainty.

The Dialect Between You

You do have signals, they're just not innate. Message length. Pace. Whether someone uses full stops. Whether it's "okay" or "ok" or "k". Whether the emojis have vanished. Whether someone writes in whole paragraphs because they're thinking, or in fragments because they're shaking.

The catch: it's a dialect, not a standard. Your three dots at the end of a sentence mean you're mulling something over. For them they mean they've got nothing more to say. Two people can misunderstand each other for weeks using the exact same characters and both feel they were perfectly clear.

So you talk about it once. Genuinely once, explicitly, like adults: what does it mean when you just write "hm"? How do I tell when you've actually checked out? What does it mean when you answer my long message with one line?

That isn't a romantic conversation. It's one of the most useful you'll ever have.

Two Fixed Points a Day

With seven or eight hours of time difference there's no shared evening. One of you goes to bed while the other is on their lunch break. There's no hour that belongs to you both, and anyone waiting for that to sort itself out will be waiting six months.

What helps is ritual. Two messages that always come: good morning and good night. Not as content, as structure. They don't depend on whether the day was good or whether anyone has something to report.

And in between, the small gestures that sound ridiculous and work anyway. Keeping each other company at bedtime. "I'm brushing my teeth." "I'm in, it's freezing." "Turn the light off." Small talk with a job to do: you still go to bed alone, but you don't go there unaccompanied. Your head registers the difference even if the bed doesn't.

The ritual has a second use that nobody talks about: because it's fixed, you can see when it's missing. An absent "good night" is information; an absent chat is not. That isn't surveillance, that's a smoke detector.

Relationship research has a number for this that I'm very fond of. John Gottman spent years watching couples respond to each other's small attempts at contact - the quick "look at this", the sigh from the next room, the glance. The couples still together six years later had engaged with roughly 86 percent of them. The divorced ones, 33. And engaging doesn't mean enthusiasm, it means looking up and saying "mm". The skill is in the noticing, not the answering.

But it has to stay a gesture and must never become a duty to attend. If the "good night" starts feeling like a form to fill in, shrink it rather than scrapping it. A heart will do. It isn't about the text, it's about someone closing off their day and you being there for it.

Don't Write What Fits

Which brings us to the flip side, and it doesn't only concern rituals, it concerns everything here.

Text has a property no face has: you can be entirely correct in it without feeling a thing. You've got time. You can assemble the appropriate sentence, the one that belongs at this point, and nobody can tell you were thinking about dinner while you wrote it. Offline that's harder, your face gives you up inside two seconds. Online you can be perfect and absent simultaneously for months.

And they notice. Not this week, maybe not even this month. But eventually they read a warm sentence and feel nothing at all, and neither of you knows why.

So, the small check before hitting send: am I feeling this, or is this just the correct move?

The more interesting part is the other direction. Let the feelings in, in the moment they arrive. If you're grinning like an idiot while reading, write exactly that. If you go warm, say so. If you've got butterflies, the correct response is not to politely administer them until they settle - because then they're gone, and what you send later is a report about a feeling instead of the feeling.

Take each other along into your emotional weather. Not just the big things, the tiny ones: that a song reminded you of them. That you stood next to the pasta in the supermarket for two minutes thinking about last night. That their message turned your entire Tuesday around. That's what closeness is built from online, and it has a shelf life of about an hour.

And it goes far beyond the physical. Desire is one channel among many, and honestly the easier one. The harder one is giving somebody access to your inner weather - including the days when it's simply grey in there.

That costs something, obviously. You make yourself visible without knowing in advance whether it'll be returned. But the alternative is a chat full of correct sentences, with two people sitting in it wondering why it feels like admin.

The Whole Relationship, Not the Comfortable Half

Talking about good feelings is easy. They please the other person, they warm the chat up, they cost nothing. Sharing bad feelings is different - it feels like an imposition, like bringing the mood down, like handing someone work they never ordered.

And that's exactly where the mistake sits.

"I'm feeling neglected by you right now." "Something's on my mind and I don't want to talk about it, but I wanted you to know it's there." Those are sentences people don't send, because they might confront the other person. But that confrontation is sometimes precisely what's needed. Not because conflict is good, but because someone who only knows the tidied-up version of you doesn't actually know you.

And that isn't my opinion, it's now quite well measured. People who systematically swallow their feelings pay for it - in studies it predicted falling satisfaction and more thoughts of leaving three months later, with a lack of authenticity as the mechanism behind it. The genuinely unpleasant part: the other side often doesn't consciously register the inauthenticity. They just like you a bit less and don't know why. So you're not protecting anyone. You're only making yourself harder to love.

And harder to help, which is almost more expensive. Some problems are like toothache: they don't go away just because nobody's looking. A few hours of quiet, a few days, with luck a few weeks - and then they're back, reliably at the worst possible moment. Teeth have never once rewarded anyone for ignoring them.

If you never say something hurts, you don't get help. You get quiet, and it's on loan.

Sometimes it's also enough for someone to just listen. Not solve, not comment, not comfort. Just be there. But for that they have to know they're needed.

Online there's a tendency to reduce relationships to one or two aspects. You write daily, you laugh a lot, you have good sex - and at some point you're sitting there thinking: something's missing, and I don't know what. That feeling isn't a sign that the relationship is wrong. It's a sign that it was built incompletely. That the uncomfortable parts were left outside because they don't feel good to send.

If you never say something hurts, you don't get help. You get quiet, and it's on loan.

Reducing a relationship to its best doesn't make it better. It makes it shallower. And eventually you notice it in exactly that vague feeling: it's lovely here, but it isn't deep.

Maybe a different image helps. A house needs load-bearing walls, and you can't tell from the finished building how many of them actually carry weight. You see rooms, windows, a nice kitchen. If too few of them carry, it still stands for a while. It stands so long that everyone forgets anything was ever missing. Until it doesn't, and nobody can say in advance when.

You can't predict the moment. You can only build beforehand.

And so this doesn't sound like an insurance policy: a well-built house protects you from nothing. There are arguments inside it too, and sometimes someone moves out of it too. Good relationships end as well. They just end over something real - and not because two people spent years politely living past each other until neither could say what actually happened.

Back to the uncomfortable parts, because there's a limit here that needs thinking about.

The fact that difficult feelings belong in a relationship does not mean you should go looking for people who bring an unusually large quantity of them. That's a different matter entirely and rarely a good one. But you don't select people from their file, so sooner or later you end up with someone who carries a lot.

Help or Run

It rarely happens at the start. At the start everyone is charming.

It happens on a Tuesday in the third or eighth week. The tone shifts, someone drops their guard, and suddenly there's something on the table bigger than anything you've had between you so far. It can start harmlessly - self-doubt, the usual "I don't know what you see in me". It can also be very much darker. Some people carry something around with them closer in scale to a black hole, and it drags at them, and from now on it drags a little at you too.

And there you are, in love, with a keyboard, holding two impulses at once. One wants to fix it. The other wants out.

The first thing worth knowing: this isn't an ambush. Someone has just shown you the load-bearing walls. Online it happens earlier than offline, because there's no intermediate stage here - no body language, no cautious testing, no showing half of something. There's silence or there's text. So it usually arrives all at once.

You are not therapy and you won't become therapy. That's the mistake warm-hearted people make reliably, myself included. You read up on it, you have ideas, you want to help, and eventually you're having conversations other people studied six years for. It goes well for a while and then, as a rule, badly - for both of you. What you can be is a person. That's a remarkable amount, but it's a different thing.

Don't be the only support. If you notice you're the only person somebody talks to about the hard stuff, that isn't a compliment, it's an alarm. It's fragile for them - their entire net is now hanging off an internet connection - and it crushes you. The sentence that holds both together isn't "I'm here for you, always". It's: "I'm staying, and I want you to have more than just me." Professional help isn't a brush-off here, it's the difference between someone who likes you and someone who trained for this.

Be honest about what you can do. "I can be there in the evenings, not during the day." "I can listen, but I can't carry this today." That sounds harsh and it's the kinder option, because a promise you can't keep is worse than one you never made.

And if it gets genuinely dark, if someone writes about thoughts of hurting themselves, the amateur hour ends here. Take it seriously, every time, including the fifth time. Don't promise confidentiality before you know what it's about - that's a promise you may have to break. Say you're frightened, if you're frightened. And work towards someone near them knowing about it, someone who can open a door. You can't do that from here, however much you want to.

People sitting in a dark hole don't drag others down on purpose. They sometimes do it anyway. That isn't blame, that's gravity.

And then the actual question: stay or go?

Both are allowed. Genuinely. Nobody is obliged to be pulled into something they don't have the strength for, and that decision doesn't make you a bad person. It makes you someone who knows how much they've got.

What isn't allowed is the third option: the slow disappearance. First answering less often, then more briefly, then not at all. That's the most comfortable solution for you and the cruellest for someone whose core conviction is already that everyone leaves in the end. You confirm the exact sentence that's destroying them, and what you save yourself is one uncomfortable conversation.

If you go, then go and say so. One sentence, honest, no diagnosis and no accusation: "I can't do this, and that's about me, not about something being wrong with you." It hurts, and it is orders of magnitude better than silence.

And if you stay: look after yourself. Not as selfishness, as a precondition. Someone who can't breathe themselves can't carry anyone - they just become a second person who isn't doing well.

Comfort Without Being There

At some point someone writes that their father is in hospital, and you're sitting on your bed eight hundred kilometres away with nothing but a keyboard.

The first impulse is to fix it. Don't. Fixing is the language of people who feel helpless, and on the receiving end it sounds like: please deal with this so we can carry on talking.

What works instead is simpler than you'd think.

Ask what's needed. "Do you want me to listen, or should I distract you, or should I think it through with you?" Three options, one second of work, and for the first time you're not missing.

Show presence through time instead of touch. Offline you sit down next to someone. Online that means: "I'm here for the next hour, you don't have to write anything." An open chat where nobody has to perform is the text-based form of holding hands.

Say what you would do. Not as a substitute, as an image. "I'd put a coffee down in front of you and sit behind you right now." It sounds sentimental and it's still translation work: it brings a gesture into a medium that has no gestures.

And check in again the next day. That's the part almost everybody forgets. Offline you see at the breakfast table how things are going. Online you see nothing unless you ask. A "so, how was the night?" in the morning weighs more than three paragraphs of sympathy the evening before.

What doesn't work: comfort that's actually reassurance for yourself. "Tell me you're okay" isn't care, it's an invoice.

Grief Lasts Longer Than Sympathy

With real grief - a death, a diagnosis, a life splitting in two - something happens that you can observe very precisely online. In the first week everyone's there. By the sixth, nobody is. Not out of coldness, but because sympathy has a short half-life and other people's lives keep moving.

And that's where one of the few genuine advantages of an online relationship sits. You can be there at three in the morning. You don't depend on visiting hours, you don't have to get in a car, and if you're in different time zones then your perfectly ordinary lunch break is their worst hour.

Don't ask "how are you" every day. That forces a summary, and the summary always comes out the same. Ask small and specific: how was the morning? Have you eaten today? Have you been outside? Small questions can be answered without having to pull yourself together first.

Write the dates down. The day they died. The birthday of the person who's missing. The day of the funeral. And then get in touch on that date without anyone having to remind you. To them it feels like you carried part of the weight - which you did.

Allow banality. Grieving people don't want to talk about grief constantly. Sometimes they want to talk about a stupid TV show, because that's the only half hour where it stops. Anyone who makes every conversation solemn takes that half hour away from them.

You don't have to grieve along. It isn't your loss and you don't have to pretend. "I don't know this, I can't imagine it, I'm staying anyway" is entirely enough.

And Then?

Everything up to here comes down to one sentence, and it's an unremarkable one: the other person only knows what you tell them.

Not what you feel. Not what you imply. Not what's obvious. Not what anyone sitting in the same room would see. Only what you wrote.

That sounds like a burden and it's actually rather good news. Because it also means: it's up to you. You aren't dependent on being guessed correctly. You can make sure at any moment that they know where you are - with a sentence that costs twelve seconds.

So go into your chat and write how you're actually doing. Not how it reads well. How it is.

Tonight. One sentence.
