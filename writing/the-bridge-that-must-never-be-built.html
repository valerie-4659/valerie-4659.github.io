Why your persona is your safe space - and how to protect it

One photo too many

The other day someone proudly told me she had sent a real photo of herself to a persona she gets along with really well. Just like that, because it felt right in the moment. My first reaction, to be honest: "Are you out of your mind?" What I actually said was something more diplomatic. I thought it anyway, though. In capital letters.

Why so harsh? Because with that one photo she had done something that can never be undone. She had tied her persona to her real face. And a face is the shortest path to a real name. Everything she had ever written as a persona was now attached to her. Not just for today, but forever.

Not because the person on the other side has to be evil. Maybe they're a wonderful human being. Maybe you like them, maybe you even fall in love with them someday. But people change, relationships change, and at some point there may be a fight. And then there's that photo. A reverse image search, a little help from an AI, a few hits on Google - and suddenly someone who is deeply hurt right now has your real name. Plus every chat log in which you felt so safe behind your persona. Exactly the place where you dare more, say more, and really let loose during online sex.

That's what this article is about. Not about distrusting everyone. But about protecting the space that gives us so much freedom. Paranoia is exhausting and suits no one. A healthy dose of caution, on the other hand, goes with almost any outfit.

Persona and identity - two worlds that never touch

My persona stands entirely on its own. It has my character, yes - but nobody can identify me through that. "Valerie is a nice person" applies to millions of people. "Valerie lives somewhere in Germany" too. "Valerie works in an office" - congratulations, that's a few million more.

The most important rule, then: Nothing that truly identifies you may ever be connected to your persona. I call that the bridge. Once that bridge stands, the persona is compromised. It no longer protects you, it's wide open like a barn door, because it is now linked to your real identity. And the nasty thing about bridges like this: You can build them, but you can never tear them down again. It's the only construction project in the world that is guaranteed to finish on time - and then stays standing forever.

A first name can be okay if you really like someone. A last name never. A hometown never. An employer never. And not your favorite bakery around the corner either, no matter how good their cinnamon rolls are.

The mosaic effect - when harmless puzzle pieces form a face

Now it gets tricky, in that quiet way you only notice when it's too late. Because the bridge doesn't have to consist of one big detail. It can be built from many tiny stones that each seem completely harmless on their own. In the security world this is called the mosaic effect.

"Lives near Leipzig" - harmless. "Works at a bank" - harmless. "Has a dog" - harmless. "Usually online between 9 and 11 pm" - harmless. "Goes to yoga on Thursdays" - harmless. But all together? Millions of people suddenly shrink to a few hundred. And with just one more detail, maybe to exactly one. She lives near Leipzig, has a dog called Biscuit, and is ever so slightly embarrassed about her yoga pants.

This isn't theory. Back in 2000, privacy researcher Latanya Sweeney showed that three pieces of information are enough to uniquely identify 87 percent of the US population: ZIP code, date of birth and gender. Three. That's it. Fewer than you need to order a pizza. And how often do you casually mention that your birthday is next week? Some people even celebrate it publicly with their persona, with congratulations from all their followers - and now the date is out there for anyone to see.

Your pets may become famous - you may not

I know, we're all proud of our furballs, feathered friends and scaly darlings. The animals know it too, by the way - the cats at least pretend not to care for exactly that reason. And yes, show your little ones! But show only your little ones. Not the view out of the window behind them, not the package with the address label on the couch, not the reflection in the TV, not the distinctive church spire on the horizon. People with too much free time (and AIs with even more computing time) can figure out an astonishing amount from things like that.

How fast this works these days was shown by a viral trend in April 2025: people had ChatGPT guess where perfectly ordinary photos had been taken. A menu, a building facade, a view out of a window - and the AI was often surprisingly close, sometimes down to the exact bar. Without any metadata, just from what was visible in the picture. It gets things wrong too, sure, and with exactly the same confidence it has when it's right. But anyone trying to find you doesn't need a perfect hit rate. A good hunch is enough to start with. Because it's not about finding the right front door straight away. It's about narrowing things down. "Somewhere in Germany" becomes "this city", the city becomes "this neighborhood". And every narrowing opens new angles of attack: Whoever knows the neighborhood can ask quite innocently about the bakery, the park, the farmers' market. Suddenly all the other puzzle pieces fit much better, and many possibilities very quickly become very few.

Metadata - the invisible package ins

What many people don't know: A photo you take with your phone often contains so-called metadata. Camera model, date, time - and quite often the GPS coordinates too. Accurate to a few meters. Your phone is like an overeager butler in this respect: It writes everything down, unasked, and tells anyone who asks politely. If I get a photo that was taken in an apartment and the location is embedded in it, I know where someone lives. And that someone is the person I'm chatting with right now.

Some platforms strip this data on upload, but I wouldn't rely on it. X, for example, removes location, camera data and timestamps from the publicly visible image when you post through the app or website. With uploads through third-party tools that isn't guaranteed, though, and X itself still gets to see the original with all its data. WhatsApp and Telegram also remove the location when you send a regular photo. But send the same picture "as a document" or "as a file" and everything goes along, GPS included. Signal cleans up photos by default. With email attachments, cloud links and smaller platforms, on the other hand, everything often stays in. So: remove metadata yourself beforehand. There are apps for that, and on most phones you can simply turn off location tagging for the camera. Or the pragmatic way: take a screenshot of the photo and send that. The screenshot doesn't carry the camera metadata or the location. Just crop out the status bar with the time and mobile carrier first.

One image, two worlds

And another classic: The same pet photo shows up on your persona account - and on your Instagram, where you hang out under your real name with your real friends. A reverse image search with Google Lens or similar tools links those two worlds in seconds. Faster than the animal in the photo can look offended. That leads to a simple rule: Whatever was once in your real world, on Instagram or in your WhatsApp status, stays there. Your persona only gets new photos. But even those only help so much if your darling is one of a kind - a tomcat with half an ear, a dog with a heart-shaped spot on its nose. Today an AI recognizes not just the photo, but the animal. So if you really want to play it safe, show your pet either on your real account or on your persona. But never on both.

AI - magnifying glass and invisibility cloak at once

We live in times when AI can do an incredible amount. And unfortunately that's true for both sides. A knife cuts bread just as well as fingers, and it doesn't ask first.

I can use an AI to find out whether the person I'm talking to might be a romance scammer. Put in a bit of information, "research this" - and the AI uses a reverse image search to discover that the pretty profile picture actually belongs to a model from Brazil. Or it recognizes a pattern that has already shown up in hundreds of scam cases.

But the other side can use an AI just as well. To find you. To assemble your puzzle pieces into a mosaic. To uncover what you never wanted to reveal.

Your writing style is a fingerprint

Something hardly anyone thinks about: People can also be recognized by how they write. Favorite words, typical typos, the way you place commas or sprinkle emojis, how long your sentences are - all of this is almost as individual as a fingerprint. Experts call it stylometry. The most famous example: In 2013 a crime novel came out by a certain Robert Galbraith. After an anonymous tip, a newspaper had linguist Patrick Juola analyze the text - the most common words, typical word pairs, word lengths, recurring letter sequences. The trail led to J.K. Rowling, who confirmed shortly afterwards: busted. If that works on one of the most famous authors in the world who deliberately wanted to hide, it works on us too. If your persona writes exactly like your work emails, that's a bridge too.

But here AI can also become an invisibility cloak. Modern translation tools no longer translate word for word, they rephrase. When I write in German and have it translated into English, my little quirks are largely gone afterwards. A nice side effect: Especially in emotionally charged moments, the AI acts as a buffer between your first impulse and the message you actually send. That defuses many a fight before it even starts.

You don't have to do this all the time - a bit of personality gets lost, and that deserves to live too. But with people you don't know yet, I'd recommend it at first: Write in your native language, have it translated, send.

AI as a second pair of eyes

On top of that: An AI can look at a conversation and spot patterns you overlook because you're emotionally caught up in it. Anyone who is in love, worried or flattered reads messages differently. The AI has no butterflies in its stomach. It only sees the text - and that's exactly its strength here.

Typical things it may notice:

  • The life story comes far too early. Illness, loss, loneliness in the very first messages - a classic lever for pity.
  • Love bombing. A lot of affection, very fast. "I've never met anyone like you" after three days.
  • The wish to leave the platform. Suddenly the conversation should move to email, Telegram or WhatsApp, where there's no report button and no moderation.
  • Dodging anything that could be verified. No video call, never any time, the camera is supposedly broken.
  • Contradictions. The job sounded different last week, so did the hometown, and the age doesn't quite fit the stories.
  • Money creeps into the conversation. First just a hint, a bill here, a problem with the bank there.
  • Urgency. Everything has to happen right now, and thinking it over is practically betrayal.

None of these signs is proof on its own. But when several pile up, it's worth taking a closer look. And an AI soberly adds them up while you're still making excuses for every single message.

Important: Black out everything that identifies you or the other person beforehand - real names, handles, places, phone numbers. Otherwise, while protecting yourself from one bridge, you build the next. And the AI doesn't replace your own judgment. It's a second pair of eyes, not the judge.

A little tip: Put the conversation in as neutrally as possible and ask "What stands out to you here?" - not "Isn't this a scammer?". Because AIs, too, sometimes tend to agree with you when you've already packed the answer into the question.

Social engineering - how to hack your way into hearts

Anyone who deals with security sooner or later ends up at social engineering. And it quickly becomes clear: Most attacks don't need any technology. All they need is a heart that wants to help. Hearts are the only security system in the world that can be cracked with sad puppy eyes.

That's not just my gut feeling. In Verizon's 2025 Data Breach Investigations Report, one of the most important annual analyses of real security incidents, a human was involved in 60 percent of all breaches - through a mistake, a click or skillful manipulation. Germany's Federal Office for Information Security (BSI) soberly describes social engineering as exploiting "helpfulness, trust, fear or respect for authority". And the nasty part: The victims act in good faith. They believe they're doing the right thing.

The tools for this are surprisingly few. Psychologist Robert Cialdini described them back in 1984 in his classic "Influence": reciprocity, commitment, social proof, liking, authority and scarcity. Add pity and pressure. Almost every scam is a mix of these - only the costume changes. So let's take a look at the costumes. It's a bit like a carnival, except that at the end someone else has your wallet.

The life story in message number three

Real people usually tell their hard stories late. In passing. Often only when you ask, because you notice something is off. I know someone with a serious illness, and I only found out after a long time, when I once asked why she so often seemed down.

People with other intentions do it the other way around. The big life story comes right at the start: "I'm not perfect, nobody loves me because of my illness." The story then isn't proof of trust, it's a tool. Whoever offers that much pity that early usually wants something in return.

Faked neediness

A particularly elegant tactic is simulated helplessness. Someone writes late at night: "I'm feeling really bad right now, I don't know who else to talk to." Or someone constantly has little problems a simple web search would solve - and still asks you for help. And everyone jumps. The helpers feel needed and good, the other person gets attention and, along the way, learns who reacts how fast and who questions nothing.

Basically these are little tests. And whoever already plays along with the harmless stuff can often be steered with the bigger things later. Sometimes a simple plausibility check is worth it: Can this story even be true? Someone who's really in distress rarely types long messages into a group chat with perfect calm.

The thumbscrews

The pattern is astonishingly similar for toxic people and scammers. First they make themselves small, slip into the victim role, so that you feel the urge to protect them. Then the person notices exactly when you've taken the bait - when you feel responsible, worry, want to help. And that's exactly when the thumbscrews get tightened.

With toxic people it's about control. The illness or limitation becomes a shield: Say "Don't talk to me like that" just once, and right away you get "I told you I have this, you obviously didn't listen. Don't you love me anymore?" And suddenly you're the one apologizing.

With scammers it's usually about money in the end. "My mother is seriously ill, it's Friday, I can't pay for the medication, and if she doesn't get it..." And off goes the instant transfer. The fact that it's Friday is no coincidence, by the way: Banks and doctors are hard to reach, and nobody has time to check anything. Scammers' emergencies have a remarkable instinct for weekends.

That's why every security training teaches a sentence so important you can't tell yourself often enough: Artificial pressure is an alarm signal. Real emergencies exist, of course - when a person is in danger, every minute counts. But then the emergency number helps, not a transfer to someone you only know from a chat. As soon as pressure and money come together, it's time to pause first. No matter how much it breaks your heart.

How big this business is shows in numbers from the US, where the Federal Trade Commission (FTC) collects fraud reports centrally: In the first nine months of 2025 alone, 55,604 romance scam cases were reported there, 22 percent more than in the same period the year before, with more than 1.16 billion dollars in losses combined. And those are only the people who dared to report it.

Reciprocity - the trap with the gift

Psychology has the principle of reciprocity - Cialdini's first tool: Whoever receives something feels obliged to give something back. Social engineers exploit exactly that. They tell you something seemingly very personal about their childhood, their job, their heartache. And you answer with something real about yourself. Only their information may be completely made up and therefore worthless - and yours is another piece of the mosaic.

Love bombing - too good to be true

After three days you're supposedly soulmates. Constant compliments, constant attention, you feel more seen than you have in ages. That's liking used as a tool. Pros read your profile and your posts beforehand and then mirror you: the same favorite band, the same scars, the same quirky humor. In psychology this is called mirroring. The unconscious version, where we automatically pick up the posture and language of the person in front of us, is called the chameleon effect. And it works because we like people who are similar to us. In a flirt this happens quite naturally. With pros, it's a method. Whoever tells you exactly what you want to hear may simply have done good research. Real people have edges you occasionally bump into. At some point they disagree with you, and that's a pretty good sign.

Give an inch - first a little, then everything

First a selfie with your face covered. Then one with your hand in the picture, because of that tattoo that sounds so cute. Then another one, and "but you already did earlier...". That's commitment: Whoever has said yes once wants to stay consistent and says yes more easily the next time. Every single step is small. Together they make a bridge - and with intimate pictures, often the foundation for sextortion. A good trick against it: Don't ask yourself whether this one step is okay. Ask yourself whether you'd be fine with where the staircase ends.

"Prove to me that you're real"

Sounds like safety, but is often the opposite. "Send me a photo holding a note with your name and today's date." Or: "Just verify yourself quickly on this site", and the site then wants your ID, phone number or straight away your credit card. That hands over exactly the bridge this whole article warns about - with a date stamp. A persona doesn't have to prove to anyone who it really is. That's the whole point. And whoever doesn't accept that has already told you a lot about themselves.

The code that came by mistake

A classic that police keep warning about: You get a text with a WhatsApp code. Shortly afterwards someone you're chatting with writes: "Sorry, I accidentally sent you my code, could you forward it to me?" In fact this person is trying to register your number on their device right now. Forward the code and you give away your account - along with all your contacts, who will be messaged next. The rule is very simple: Never pass on codes, to anyone. And two-step verification in your WhatsApp settings should be on anyway.

Uniform, oil rig, lab coat - borrowed authority

Whoever commands respect gets questioned less. That's why scammers love wearing uniforms. It feels like there are more lonely generals on the internet than in all the world's armies combined. According to the US Federal Trade Commission, a military deployment abroad is the most popular excuse for why someone can't meet. Closely followed by working on an oil rig somewhere at sea. Where apparently there's never enough signal for a video call, but always enough for a request for money. The handy thing about these roles, from the perpetrators' point of view: They explain everything at once. Why there's no video call, why the connection is so bad, and why money is suddenly needed for a flight home, a fee or a package held at customs.

Just the two of us against the world

"Don't tell anyone about us, they wouldn't understand." Or more subtly: little jabs at your friends, who supposedly don't appreciate you the way she does. The goal is isolation. Because your friends are exactly the people who would do the plausibility check you can no longer do yourself right now. Whoever wants to be your only advisor usually has a reason for it.

The trading uncle

Probably the most expensive variant starts quite harmlessly with romance. After a few weeks the other person casually mentions how well their crypto investments are going. The uncle is an analyst (strangely, the uncle is never a janitor), there's this app, and there are screenshots of profits too. You start small, the app shows lovely gains, you put in more. And when you want to withdraw your money, suddenly taxes or fees are due. The profits were never real. It's called "pig butchering": first fatten, then slaughter. According to the FBI's Internet Crime Report, crypto investment fraud was the costliest type of fraud of all in 2025, with 7.2 billion dollars in losses in the US alone.

Everyone's doing it - and only today

Two tools that love to show up together. Social proof: "Everyone else in the group has already posted their photo", "Look how many people are already investing with me." And scarcity: "My account is getting banned tomorrow, quick, message me on Telegram", "The offer is only valid until tonight." Both are meant to achieve the same thing: that you stop thinking and just go along. And that brings us back to artificial pressure.

An example of how this plays out

Imagine you've been chatting with someone for a few weeks. Everything is nice, you have a lot in common, there are compliments, things are getting close.

Then, completely casually, a few harmless questions come up. "What was the name of your first pet?" "What street did you grow up on?" "What was your grandma's signature dish?" Sometimes wrapped as a fun game: "Your drag name is your first pet plus the street you grew up on!" Everyone laughs, everyone plays along.

And now the reveal: These are exactly the typical security questions used to reset passwords. One more detail about the job, one about the area - and an account can be taken over or the real person found. No single question was suspicious. Only all of them together.

Turning the tables

The nice thing is: You can use this technique yourself. If someone tells you about their dog, casually ask what its name is. Let's say, Rex. Most people happily share that kind of thing, it doesn't lead anywhere after all.

A few weeks later you simply ask: "How's Rex doing, by the way?" A real person is happy and tells you about the dog. But scammers often work in teams, and every few days a different person sits at the keyboard. Or one person handles dozens of victims at once. And notes aren't always kept carefully. If what comes back is "Rex, who?" - then you know. Nobody forgets their own dog. The name of one of forty victims, on the other hand, easily.

The team thing is no exaggeration, by the way. The United Nations speaks of hundreds of industrial-scale scam centers, mainly in Southeast Asia, making just under 40 billion dollars in profit a year. More than 200,000 people are estimated to have been trafficked into them and forced to run scam chats. So the charming person on the other side is sometimes not a person at all, but a workstation.

Important: Don't bring it up. Keep it to yourself. The other side doesn't need to know they've been found out. For you, it's a reason to look very closely from now on.

When relationships turn

Companies have a rule that sounds harsh at first: When someone is let go, all their access is revoked immediately. Even for the people who were always the last to turn off the lights at night and the first to turn them on in the morning. Not because anyone distrusts them. But because people in an emotional state of emergency do things they would never otherwise do. A few clicks out of anger and frustration, and accounts are deleted. That happens faster than you'd think.

It's the same with relationships. A breakup is an emotional state of emergency. It's enough that someone is hurt. It's enough that someone believes you cheated. That can set off a dynamic that can no longer be slowed down or controlled. And the stupid things people do then can't be undone in the end.

Imagine your real name suddenly appears online next to your persona. And next to it, the chat logs. If that lands with your employer, it can cost you your job depending on the content - even if everything you did was completely legal. Because the picture others have of you gets distorted in one stroke. And sometimes things come to light that you wanted to decide entirely on your own whom to tell and when. An unwanted outing, for example. Oops.

Sextortion

The most vicious form of this has its own name: sextortion. Someone has intimate pictures, videos or chats of you and blackmails you with them. "Pay, or I'll send this to your family, your boss, your friends." Sometimes an ex is behind it, sometimes a professional gang that has been working toward exactly this. And sometimes a single real photo is enough to turn a harmless flirt into extortion.

German police describe the typical pattern like this: Contact starts on a social network or dating platform, then quickly moves to a video chat. There you're talked into undressing, and everything is secretly recorded. Often there are organized gangs behind it operating from abroad. There are no nationwide statistics in Germany, but a survey of the state criminal police offices already found well over 2,000 recorded cases for 2022. The real number is probably much higher, because so many victims are ashamed. Remember: A video chat is never as private as it feels.

Invisible risks - deleted doesn't mean gone

Many people feel safe because they delete messages after a few minutes. Unfortunately that's a false sense of security. The internet has a memory like an elephant, just with considerably less tact.

Whatever was once on the other person's screen can be saved. By screenshot, by a second phone taking a picture - or very elegantly by browser plugin. Chat history on X, for example, isn't exactly convenient. But with AI, almost anyone can now build a small Chrome plugin in no time that logs every conversation in the background and makes it nicely searchable. The AI even helps cover various scenarios along the way. You delete, the other side smiles. And you don't even know it.

And even if all your chat partners are lovely: Platforms can be hacked too. What happens if one day masses of chat logs end up online? That's not a thought experiment - in 2015 Ashley Madison, a platform for affairs, was hacked. Data from around 37 million user accounts landed online. Extortion, broken marriages and lost jobs followed. And as late as 2017, two years later, victims received new threatening emails demanding Bitcoin. A leak never expires.

That gives me my second rule of thumb, the one that holds everything together: Always plan as if everything will become public one day. And never write anything that would ruin you if your real name stood next to it. Then your persona is your protection - and not your only safety line.

Technical protection - the walls around your safe space

As long as you stay within one platform, a lot is still easy to control. It often gets dangerous when you start leaving the platform: "Here, just email me." So that this never turns into a bridge, a few ground rules. They're not particularly exciting, but neither are castle walls. At least not until someone is standing in front of them:

  • A separate email address for your persona. One that has nothing to do with you and doesn't appear anywhere else. My persona accounts all run through their own address, everything lives in isolation. If something ever gets cracked open, it won't lead to me.
  • A separate phone number or none at all. Many platforms want a number for verification. Your private one is the worst choice for that.
  • Turn off contact syncing. Otherwise the app might suggest you to people from your real address book - and vice versa. One of the most common unnoticed bridges there is.
  • Don't reuse old usernames. The funny nickname you've been using in forums for ten years can lead straight to your real profile with a simple search.
  • Separate browser profiles or devices. No shared cookies, no accidental liking with the wrong account.
  • Careful with payment methods. Anyone thinking about getting paid for pictures, texts or anything else should know: There's practically no payment method that truly anonymizes you. With many services, your real name ends up on the other person's receipt.
  • Careful with links from strangers. One click can reveal your IP address and with it roughly where you live. A VPN helps - that's already a somewhat higher security level, but a good idea for anyone who takes this seriously.
  • Keep an eye on your online times. When you're active reveals your time zone and your daily routine. That's a puzzle piece too.
  • Password manager and two-factor authentication. So that one hacked account doesn't open up all the others. And never answer security questions truthfully - make something up and store it in your password manager. Then the drag name game from above is worthless too.
  • Remove metadata. Before you share any photo. Always.

Persona and really getting to know someone - two different rooms

I'm often asked whether we couldn't meet up sometime, since we're nearby anyway. My answer is clear: No. We're in a chat with personas here, and that's how I want to keep it.

This isn't only about safety. A persona is my ideal image, with my character. It's a playground for all the thoughts I simply want to try out. Once I've seen the real person behind a persona, I can't unsee it. Then I no longer see the persona, but the person - and sometimes the two fit together about as well as day and night. That can destroy the whole magic, for both sides.

And one more thing: Whoever sends a real photo unasked puts the other side under pressure. Suddenly they feel obliged to deliver something too, even though they don't want to. Often the chat goes quiet afterwards. Not out of malice, but because you've put someone in a position they can't gracefully get out of.

Whoever really wants to get to know someone goes to a real getting-to-know-you chat. There you're open about who you are - but also more filtered. You don't barge in with everything at once, you build trust step by step. The things you tell as a persona in the very first hour come up there much later, if at all. There's some embellishing there too, sure, but most people stay close to the truth, because they know a meeting would expose them otherwise. How exactly the crossing between these worlds can work - I'll write a separate article about that soon.

And if it has already happened?

Sometimes the bridge has already been built. Then the most important thing is: don't panic and don't stay alone with it.

  • Don't pay. Whoever pays once almost always gets blackmailed again. Paying ends nothing, it only shows that it works.
  • Secure evidence. Screenshots of chats, profiles, demands, with date and time. Even if it hurts to look at them. And don't delete anything before the police have seen it.
  • Cut contact and report. Block and report on the platform, and in case of extortion, go to the police too.
  • Get intimate images blocked. Through StopNCII.org you can make sure intimate images are recognized and blocked preemptively on many major platforms - without the images ever leaving your device. Only a digital fingerprint, a so-called hash, is calculated, and participating platforms compare new uploads against it. It can't erase things from the entire internet, but it prevents a lot.
  • Talk to someone. A friend, a counseling service, anyone you trust. In Germany, for example, HateAid advises victims of digital violence for free and in some cases even covers legal costs.

And what if you just have a weird feeling? Even just a hint of doubt? Then ask, for example an AI. It's often more objective than a friend who might just say "You're crazy" or, on the contrary, immediately assume the worst. Doubt doesn't mean you're right. Doubt only means something is rumbling somewhere in your gut. And that rumbling deserves to be taken seriously. Gut feelings have saved people whose heads had long since been talked into it.

Falling for it has nothing to do with intelligence

Finally, something that really matters to me: Precisely the smart people - or the ones who consider themselves especially smart - are often the most vulnerable. Because whoever believes this could never happen to them checks less. And whoever has already invested a lot is reluctant, out of pure vanity, to admit they're making a mistake right now.

That's why so many who fell for it are ashamed and talk to no one about it. And in the end, that only helps the perpetrators. Falling for it has nothing to do with stupidity. It has to do with someone very deliberately targeting your heart. Talking about it is no disgrace - it's the first step to taking back control.

A few final words

Folks, no matter how nice someone seems to you: Protect your personas. They're your shield and your safe space, where you can do whatever you want. Don't break through the wall of that space just because you found the other person so nice right now.

Don't believe that the number of followers or the age of an account is a mark of quality. Both can be bought, faked or taken over. Look instead at how someone treats others. Pay attention to how they talk to you. Just stay alert. And keep your real identity separate from your persona.

That doesn't mean everyone out there is evil. I'd even claim it's only a small percentage who mean you harm. But that small percentage can do immense damage if they want to.

There's no such thing as one hundred percent safety. When in doubt, listen to your gut - and not to your genitals or your heart. Try to look at things soberly, and always ask yourself: What happens to me in the worst case?

My persona is my safe space. A place where I'm allowed to be free, playful, brave, a little bit crazy. And I won't give up that place for anyone. No matter how much I like someone. Stay curious, stay warm-hearted - and always stay a tiny bit skeptical. 🖤
