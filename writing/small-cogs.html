I was fourteen when the question first got me properly. Not the way you ask at eight where the dog has gone, and settle for "dog heaven" because you are tired. Properly.

I lay awake at night, staring into the dark above me, and thought: what is all this for.

The first answer I found was the biological one, and it was awful. We are here to reproduce. Everything else is trimming. Evolution sifts, the ones that fit pass it on, and over millions of years something better comes out of it. That is not meaning, that is a procedure. A very slow, very bloody algorithm with no intention behind it that works anyway, which does not make it any more likeable.

And then it occurred to me that we have been cheating at this procedure systematically for a few hundred years. We practise medicine. We keep alive exactly the people the algorithm would have sorted out, we fit heart valves and hand out insulin.

I have asthma. In the eighteenth century I would have been a very short story, with a modest chapter about my childhood and none of what came afterwards. Today I have an inhaler in my bag and write articles about the meaning of life. If reproduction and selection really were the meaning, then humanity would be the only species that consistently sabotages its own purpose and hands out Nobel prizes for it.

So the biological answer was gone. And that is where it started.

The Case Against You

I am going to do this properly and without padding, because otherwise the comfort at the end is worth nothing.

Do you remember your great-grandfather? Not his name. A story he told. His opinion about anything. The way he laughed. Most of us do not get far here, and that is only the fourth generation. Four. Roughly a hundred years, and the man has vanished completely apart from a name and possibly a photograph in which he is sitting uncomfortably. Four generations is the shelf life of an entire human being.

The whole biography, boiled down to one paragraph: we arrive in a life we did not choose, in a family we did not choose, in a country and a century nobody consulted us about either. We grow up and build something of our own. Those who have children raise them and then watch them leave, because that was the entire point of the exercise, which does not make it easier. We get older. The body starts clocking off one part at a time, first the knees, then the back, then the things one does not discuss in company. And in the end we sit there and wait for death to close the procedure. What remains is a memory in a few heads, and it is alarmingly fragile. Once those heads go too, there is nothing left.

The neuroscientist David Eagleman once sorted this out neatly: you die three times. Once when the body stops. Once when it is buried. And once when your name is spoken somewhere in the world for the last time. The third is the one that counts, and that is precisely the one you do not get to witness.

Some people get further than others. They become great leaders, in the majority of cases also great arseholes who shove the world towards the abyss with admirable energy. Some become scientists and find something that moves all of us forward a little. Some become stars and accompany our lives for a few years, usually without knowing it. Their footprint is big enough to press itself into the collective memory for a long time, and the children of children learn their names at school.

But that is all it is: a name on a timeline. When you memorise a date about Charlemagne in year nine, you feel nothing. There is no person there any more. There is a data point with a beard.

As a child I assumed the universe simply went on forever. A naive idea, and physics broke me of it thoroughly. The universe is finite. It has an appointment, the date is just set very generously. Nobody knows exactly what the end looks like, but that there is one is the best calculation we currently have, and the calculation is good. At some point every star has burned its fuel, and no new ones form, because there is nothing left to make them from. Then black holes remain, merging and slowly radiating away over stretches of time our number words were not built for. Or the expansion keeps accelerating until it pulls apart galaxies, stars, planets and finally atoms. Or it simply goes cold. Not cold like January, but cold all the way down: no differences in temperature any more, so no heat anywhere that could flow anywhere else, so no movement that still does anything. No electron with anywhere left to go. And then it stays that way.

Whichever version. In the end it is dark, quiet and very tidy. Nothing remains. Not "not much". Nothing.

Everything That Had to Go Right for You to Read This

We live in the flowering period of the universe, in a spiral galaxy, in one of the outer arms, which is to say the suburbs, where less is going on, which in this case means where less explodes. Our sun is not too big and not too small. Earth sits at the right distance. It has a magnetic field that shields us from the solar wind, and it only has that because it has a decent iron core, and it only has that because four and a half billion years ago a protoplanet hit it at exactly the right angle. A metre off, and I would not be writing this article. The debris became the moon, and the moon was needed to keep the axis from wobbling and to slow the rotation down. It holds us straight like someone steadying a wardrobe during a house move while somebody else looks for the screws. Without it, life here would be about as comfortable as on Jupiter. Water arrived later as ice on comets, because before that it would simply have boiled off into space. And then molecules, for reasons we still cannot pin down cleanly, started to live. That became the code, the code became single cells, and after a very, very long while the single cells became us.

I am not listing this to make us smaller. I am listing it because every single one of those steps could have gone wrong and not one of them did. What came out of it exists, as far as we can currently see, exactly once. And that is where the pain sat for me: it is not just a shame that everything passes. It is a shame that this in particular passes.

Why We Ask at All

That we think about it at all has to do with our neurons, but not as simply as I assumed for a long time. It is not down to sheer quantity. An African elephant has around 257 billion neurons, three times as many as our 86 billion. But almost 98 percent of them sit in its cerebellum, where they presumably run the trunk, a tool that makes any human hand look clumsy. That leaves 5.6 billion for the cerebral cortex. We have 16 billion there. So it does not come down to how many there are, but to where they sit and how they are wired together.

And what they do there really is a kind of hallucination. The neuroscientist Anil Seth calls perception a controlled hallucination. The brain does not receive the world, it guesses it. It generates continuous predictions about what might have caused the signals coming in, and what we experience is essentially that prediction. The senses only supply the correction. Hallucination, because it comes from the inside. Controlled, because reality keeps interrupting. When someone hallucinates in the medical sense, it is the same process, except the prediction has lost its grip on the world.

Consciousness, then, is the result of that hallucination. A model of the world good enough to survive in, and eventually good enough to include itself in the model. From that point on the model sits there and asks why it is doing this.

Whether that is the whole explanation, nobody knows. Why any of it feels like anything from the inside, instead of just running along in the dark, is still unsolved, and in the literature it is called the hard problem. So the cleverest people in the field are standing in front of the same wall I stood in front of at fourteen, just with better equipment.

The question drove me into a depression. Not dramatically, no thunderclap, more like water seeping away into sand. I cannot make my peace with "that is just how it is". I want to know why, I want to know where all this is going, and I am not somebody who finds peace in a god. I am a factual person. I need something that holds when you put both feet on it.

What I noticed much later: I had been asking the wrong question for decades.

The Flaw in the Question

I asked about meaning while quietly assuming it had to be inside me. Inside my lifespan, inside my eighty years, self-contained and singular, like an object you carry around in your handbag and occasionally go looking for. I treated my life as a closed system.

It is not one. It has doors. Everywhere, and most of them are open.

Viktor Frankl, neurologist, psychiatrist and survivor of four camps, called this the Copernican turn of the question. We ask: what do I expect from life? That is the wrong direction. The right way round is: what does life expect from me, here, in this particular situation I am sitting in? For him, meaning is not something you find like a key under the sofa. Meaning is what comes into being when you answer. Through an act, through a person you love, and in the worst case through the attitude you take towards suffering that cannot be changed. Frankl did not develop this at a desk. He tested it under conditions that would have broken any theory that was not true.

Camus arrived at the same place by an entirely different route. A meaningless universe, he says, is not an invitation to despair but to defiance. Sisyphus, rolling his stone up the hill knowing full well it will come back down, is not the saddest man in history. He is the freest, because nobody has to promise him anything to keep him going.

Both are saying the same thing at heart. Meaning does not get delivered. It gets made. And it does not get made inside you, it gets made between you and everyone else.

What You Can Actually Check

So I went and looked at whether this is more than a pretty phrase you tell yourself in the evening. It is.

Let us start right at the beginning, with the first signature in human history. On clay tablets from Uruk, around 3300 before our era, there is a name next to a barley account: Kushim. Not a king, not a prophet, not a general. An accountant. Scholars still argue about whether this was really a person or just a job title, but even then the finding holds: the first name humanity kept of itself belongs to somebody who counted supplies. And without counted supplies there would have been no city in which somebody, a few generations later, had the idea of using the same script to write down something that was not a barley inventory.

Then there is the family tree. In 2004, Rohde, Olson and Chang published a calculation in Nature of how far back you have to go before the human family tree converges, and the result is fairly uncomfortable. The most recent common ancestor of everyone alive today lived, depending on the model, a few thousand years ago. Go back somewhat further and you reach a point where every single person alive then who still has descendants today is an ancestor of all of us. Of every single one, no exceptions, across every continent, every skin colour and every very loudly held opinion about who really belongs here. It is a model and not an excavation report, the numbers wobble, the direction does not. Your great-grandfather's stories are gone. He himself is still here, distributed across people who never heard of him.

And your behaviour reaches further than you can see. Christakis and Fowler analysed data from the Framingham Heart Study, which has followed the same families since 1948, and found that states such as happiness, smoking or obesity spread through a social network across roughly three degrees. The friend of your friend is statistically a little happier if you are, and the two of you have never met. They found the same pattern in cooperation games: people treated generously are more generous towards third parties, who in turn are more generous towards fourth parties. The studies are methodologically contested, like almost everything interesting. But they are an attempt to measure something anyone already knows who has ever shared an office with somebody in a bad mood.

What convinced me most, though, was a distinction I had not made before. In 2013 Roy Baumeister and colleagues followed nearly four hundred people for a month and cleanly separated what makes a life happy from what makes it meaningful. They are not the same thing. Happiness depends on getting what you need. Meaning depends on giving. People who mostly took reported more happiness and less meaning. People who mostly gave, the other way round. Those who look after others have a harder time of it and find it more significant anyway, which any family carer would sign off on immediately, usually sitting down, because she is tired.

I have never been entirely comfortable with the word happiness, though, at least not the version you can measure with a questionnaire. Years ago I read a line somewhere that I have not been able to shake since, roughly: happiness is not some kind fairy who turns up if you have been good enough. Happiness is the ability to cope with your own life, particularly when it is going badly.

I would add one thing. Happiness is also the ability to find something positive behind the bad, and there is always something. My childhood was bad, which is set out at greater length elsewhere and does not need spreading out again here. I am still the person I am today because of that childhood and not in spite of it. That justifies none of it and makes none of it all right, and I would not wish it on anyone. But what came out of it I am allowed to put in the credit column, and I do.

And it holds all the way to the end. The Harvard Study of Adult Development has been running since 1938 and has followed Harvard students and boys from poor Boston neighbourhoods across their entire lives, and by now their children too. Over eighty years of research, millions in funding, hundreds of questionnaires, blood work, brain scans, and the most stable finding is this: not money, not fame, not your cholesterol at fifty predicts how healthy and content you are at eighty, but the quality of your relationships. You could also have just asked a grandmother, but she would not have supplied any significance values.

Lastly, because I find it charming: Erik Erikson called the seventh stage of human development generativity versus stagnation, roughly between forty and sixty-five. It is the point at which people start wanting to bring forth something that outlasts them. Not necessarily children. Also students, texts, workshops, gardens, trees. I am in my early forties and currently writing an article about the meaning of life. Erikson would probably have nodded and written something in his notebook.

What Everyone Else Worked Out Independently

I do not believe in any god. I do believe, however, that every intelligent life form invents one on the threshold of its first society, for two thoroughly practical reasons. The first is not knowing. Nobody can stand a hole in the explanation for long, so something gets put into it that is larger than yourself. The second is order. Rules are considerably easier to enforce when they do not come from the neighbour, who owns two goats himself, but from somebody sitting a great deal higher up who sees everything.

I am not alone with that hunch. The Big Gods hypothesis says essentially this: that punishing, morally interested gods evolved culturally because they make cooperation between strangers possible, and without cooperation between strangers there are no cities. Whether the gods came first or only arrived once societies were already large is heatedly disputed. A large data analysis in 2019 found the opposite order, was retracted by its own authors after criticism of how they handled missing data, recalculated, and arrived at the same result again. The side finding is the part I like best: for that first step towards the large society, shared rituals seem to have mattered more than the content of the belief. Not what everybody believed, but that everybody did the same thing.

What I find remarkable regardless is where cultures ended up that had never heard of each other. The Talmud has the line that whoever saves a single life saves an entire world. Not a proportional share, not a bit of a world. An entire one. The southern African formula of ubuntu says a person is a person through other people: I am because we are. Buddhism calls it dependent origination, the observation that nothing exists out of itself, only out of what holds it. In Mexico the third and final death is the moment when nobody thinks of you any more, which is why there is a rather large annual festival aimed at preventing exactly that. China and Japan do the same thing with ancestor veneration, only with fewer sugar skulls. And the Hávamál, the Old Norse collection of sayings, states in essence that cattle die and kinsmen die and you die too, but that one thing never dies: the verdict on the dead. So the Vikings, rarely praised for their tenderness, concluded that what remains of a person is what they did to others.

Six cultures, no common denominator beyond the fact that everyone involved was human, and the same answer at the end. That is not proof of a god, and I am not using it as one. But when six people independently guess the same number, you take another look at the number.

Chains That Actually Happened

Imagine you read this. You find it good enough to keep. Years later you tell it to your grandson, who is in a bad way, so bad that he no longer knows why he should get up in the morning, and something in it gives him a reason. He gets up. He studies. Twenty years after that he builds a research lab where a woman works on Alzheimer's, and her work is the reason somebody in 2098 recognises their mother until the last day.

That sounds contrived. It is the normal case, we just never see it from the outside.

Norman Borlaug failed his university entrance exam. He only got in because there happened to be a new programme for people like him. There he heard a lecture by the plant pathologist Elvin Stakman about fungi that destroy grain, and chose that field afterwards. Borlaug went on to co-develop the wheat varieties with which Mexico, India and Pakistan averted their famines, and estimates of how many people did not starve because of it run as high as a billion. Whoever set up that admissions programme never found out. He probably filled in a form and looked forward to his lunch break.

Anne Sullivan grew up in an almshouse in Massachusetts, half blind, without schooling, without any prospects. When an inspection committee walked through the building, she literally put herself in the path of one of the men and asked to be allowed to go to school. He made it happen. She got into a school for the blind and later became Helen Keller's teacher. A man pauses a moment longer during a routine inspection instead of walking on, and two generations later an entire world thinks differently about disability.

It also works as absence. In 1962 Vasili Arkhipov is sitting in a Soviet submarine off Cuba, out of radio contact, with a nuclear torpedo on board and two officers in favour of using it. He is not, and because his consent is required, nothing happens. In 1983 Stanislav Petrov sees American missiles launching on his screen and does not pass the alert on, because the number strikes him as too small for a real attack. It was a sensor fault. Two men, two nights, one button not pressed. Neither was much celebrated for it in his lifetime, one of them got in trouble with his superiors instead. You can have your effect by not doing something.

And then there are the ones whose names we do not even have. The stonemasons who worked on cathedrals that took two hundred years to build. Not one of them saw the finished building. They cut their stone, set it in place, complained about the foreman and died, and the stone still holds up something they could not have imagined.

Big things do not come out of nowhere. They grow. Ideas grow in heads and spread across generations, and every head along the way is necessary, including the one that did nothing more than pass them on.

This Works Both Ways, Unfortunately

I would be dishonest if I stopped here, and then the rest would just be decoration.

The same mechanism carries the other thing too. The teacher who tells a child they will never amount to anything has started a chain just the same, only one in which people get smaller instead of larger. The sentence somebody said in passing thirty years ago and forgot the same evening, which is still there when you look at yourself in the mirror in the morning. I know exactly what I am talking about, but that is another article.

This is not a reason to despair, it is the reason it matters at all what you do. If your actions had no effect beyond yourself, it would be completely irrelevant how you behave. That is precisely nihilism, and it is comfortable, because it keeps you out of everything. Having an effect is less comfortable, because then you are responsible. But it is also what makes you important. The two belong together, and you cannot take one and hand the other in at the cloakroom.

Yes, But It All Ends Anyway

Here comes the objection. For years it came to me at night. It is legitimate and it is wrong anyway, for two reasons.

First, transience does not refute meaning, or nothing would ever have meant anything. A concert is over when it is over. A hug lasts eight seconds. The afternoon somebody listened to you when you badly needed it is long past and was still the reason you got through that month. We treat meaning as a substance that has to be durable in order to be real. It is not. Pain does not have to be eternal to hurt either, and oddly nobody argues about that.

Second, and this is the point that actually calmed me down: I had been using the wrong time scale. The universe has unimaginable stretches ahead of it, but it is habitable in only a ridiculously narrow window of them, and we are in the middle of that window. Not before, not after. Now. Everything human beings will ever feel, decide or pass on happens in this one strip, and the strip is running right now. That is not a consolation prize, that is a window, and you have a place in it whether you like it or not.

When the Voyager probe took one last look back at Earth, Carl Sagan said in essence: everything you have ever loved, everyone you have ever heard of, every human being there ever was, on a speck of dust in a sunbeam. Most people read that as a humiliation. Sagan meant it differently, because the conclusion he drew was not that nothing counts. The conclusion was to treat each other more kindly, since we have nothing but each other and nowhere else to go.

You

So, you. Yes, you, reading this right now.

You are important. Your children are important, if you have any. Your parents are important, your family, your friends, your colleagues, the acquaintances whose names escape you at the moment. The woman at the checkout you see every Thursday and whose surname you were never told. All of them.

And the ones who are no longer there. The people you once loved and do not love now, at least not like that. A relationship ending does not make it not have happened. What you taught each other you both carry onwards, into two lives that have long had nothing to do with one another, and at some point one of you says something to a complete stranger that they can only say because the other person existed. If you parted on good terms, even the love stays. It just no longer points anywhere.

And if that sounds too easy: this is not a nicety, it is the summary of everything above it.

The catch is only that you cannot see your own effect. You stand in the middle of the chain and only ever see your own link. Nobody gets a status report on their own aftershock. Hardly anyone ever finds out what became of the person they once comforted, and if they do, it is nearly always too late for the one who should have heard it. That is why so many people feel small and insignificant when they are not. Not because they have no effect, but because effect is invisible from the inside.

We look at ourselves far too often as individual people. But we are part of a humanity and part of a human history, and in that, every single one of us is necessary. Not equally large. Necessary.

It is true, you are a small cog in a very large machine. But a machine does not care how big a cog is. It cares about one thing only, which is whether it turns.

Which is why there is no insight at the end, only something to do.

Think of a person who changed something in your life. A teacher, a neighbour, somebody from the internet, somebody you have not spoken to in twelve years. It does not have to have been anything big. Usually it was one sentence, which they have long forgotten and which stuck to you like a burr.

But a machine does not care how big a cog is. It cares about one thing only, which is whether it turns.

And then tell them. Today. A message will do, three lines will do, it does not have to be nicely put. Because they do not know. They never found out that they had an effect, because nobody ever told them.

Send the message. The chain takes care of the rest.
