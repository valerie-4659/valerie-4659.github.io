Sam and Robin

Sam: Hey, everything okay with you?
Robin: Yeah sure, just a lot going on today.
Sam: Okay.
Sam: There's always a lot going on with you...
Robin: What do you mean by that?
Sam: Nothing. I just kind of feel like I'm not that important right now.
Robin: I told you there's a lot going on today...
Sam: Fine. Do your thing.

A perfectly ordinary chat. No swearing, no accusation, nothing you could hold against anyone later. And still, at the end, two people sit in front of their phones feeling rotten.

Who did something wrong here? Maybe nobody, really. But somewhere between "Okay." and "Do your thing", something went wrong, and it wasn't the content.

I've been Sam in chats like this more often than I'd like to admit. I never noticed.

The Question

Melodie and I were talking about how I want to change. Somewhere in that conversation we landed on everything messages say without saying it out loud. Implicit communication. Nonviolent communication.

At some point I asked her: "Do I really communicate that aggressively?"

The moment I hit send, I had a feeling the answer wouldn't be "No, of course not." Anyone who asks a question like that usually half knows the answer already.

Her answer was kind and precise, and that's exactly why it landed: I tend to pin my feelings on the other person. Sentences like "You make me feel". Indirect blame. Not often, she said. But often enough that she'd noticed.

I knew I can get clumsy when things get emotional. I didn't know that my language changes direction while it happens. That somewhere along the way, "I feel awful" turns into "it's your fault I feel awful", without me ever seeing the turn-off.

This is the fourth part of my series on communication in text. The first three were about how feelings survive in letters, how we think while we write, and what culture does to a sentence. This one is more personal. It's about the moment when you yourself are the sentence that lands wrong.

And let me say this right away: this won't be a text about language police. Nobody should have to run every message through an inner customs check three times after reading this. It's about something much smaller. Noticing.

Everything a Sentence Says

That messages say more than their words is nothing new. The Hamburg psychologist Friedemann Schulz von Thun described more than forty years ago that every message has four sides. A factual layer: what is this about? A self-revelation: what am I giving away about myself? A relationship layer: what do I think of you, where do we stand? And an appeal: what should the other person do?

The nasty part: we always broadcast on all four channels, even when we only mean one. And whoever receives listens with four ears, but often picks just one of them. Usually the one that's sorest right now. Four ears sounds like a very attentive rabbit, and that's exactly how it feels in moments like these: wide awake, jumpy, and ready to vanish down the burrow at any second.

Let's take Sam's "There's always a lot going on with you" apart. On the factual level it says: Robin is busy. As self-revelation: I miss you right now. As a relationship statement: I'm clearly not that important to you. And as an appeal: make more time for me. Sam probably meant the self-revelation most of all. Robin mostly hears the relationship statement. And just like that, two people are talking about two different sentences that happen to be made of the same letters.

Then there's an insight from Paul Watzlawick that every chat could hang over the bed: one cannot not communicate. A message that doesn't arrive is a message too. A "Read" with no reply speaks volumes, usually exactly the volumes we least want to read at that moment.

Which leaves the full stop. Yes, the punctuation mark. Researchers at Binghamton University had people rate short replies like "Okay" or "Sure", sometimes with a period, sometimes without. As text messages, the replies with a period came across as less sincere. On a handwritten note, the period made no difference. So in chats, the humblest punctuation mark in the world has had a remarkable career, from the end of a sentence to a frown.

Sam's "Okay." was never just an okay. It was an okay with a raised eyebrow.

Why It Tips Over

When things are calm, I'm good with words. I write entire article series about it. When it hurts, I write the way I reverse park. I turn the music down, hold my breath, and at the end the car is still crooked.

That's not a character flaw, that's biology with bad timing. Couples researcher John Gottman calls the state "flooding". Under stress your pulse goes up, and in his lab the line sat at roughly a hundred beats per minute. Above that, you stop really hearing the other person, no matter how hard you try. Your body then needs about twenty minutes for the stress hormones to wear off.

Twenty minutes. In a chat, that's an eternity. In twenty minutes I've written four messages and regretted two of them.

On top of that comes the text. Face to face, my face would have cushioned the sentence. My voice would have trembled, and the other person would have seen: someone's hurt here, not angry. In a chat there's only the sentence, naked and with its arms crossed.

Justin Kruger and Nicholas Epley studied how well people get their tone across in writing. Over the phone, the intended tone was read correctly in about three out of four cases, in writing only 56 percent of the time. That's barely better than a coin toss. And yet almost everyone was sure they'd been understood. The reason is simple: we read our own messages in our own voice inside our heads. We hear the vulnerability because we feel it. All that arrives is the letters.

And then there are people like me. For many people with ADHD, difficult emotional regulation comes as part of the package, even if it barely shows up in the official criteria. In one study of adults, more than half of those with ADHD reported regulation problems stronger than almost everyone in the comparison group. That doesn't mean we can't wait twenty minutes. It means the wave comes faster and higher. And that the hand holding the phone is sometimes quicker than the part of me that says "wait". That part is a kindly old gentleman with a walking stick. The hand is a greyhound.

Nobody Owns a Palantír

I don't own a palantír. If I do, it's somewhere under the laundry pile and I've never used it. Still, in emotional moments I often act as if the person on the other end had one. As if that person should simply know what's going on inside me.

That's exactly what Sam expected. Sam felt alone, never wrote it, and was still disappointed that Robin didn't notice.

Psychology has a name for this: the illusion of transparency. Thomas Gilovich, Kenneth Savitsky and Victoria Medvec showed that people systematically overestimate how well others can read their inner states. We think our feelings leak out. They do, but far less than we believe. Feelings are surprisingly homebodies. We imagine them standing out on the balcony, waving. In truth they're sitting inside in the armchair with a good book, and nobody on the street suspects a thing. And that was in the lab, face to face. In a chat nothing leaks out at all, except what we actually type.

The headteacher trap.

When we notice we weren't understood, we want to explain. Which is fair enough. It's just that explaining likes to tip over, especially when it already hurts.

"As I already wrote..."

"Once more, slowly."

"I honestly thought that was clear."

Suddenly someone is standing at the blackboard, writing something up for the third time that any child should understand. And the other person sits in the back row and feels like that child. That's a judgment too, just with chalk instead of an accusation.

The other direction is passive surrender: "Fine." "Doesn't matter." "Just forget it." It sounds like retreat, but it's often a slap in reverse gear. What it means is: you didn't get it again, and I'm giving up on explaining it to you.

Facts get read too.

"But it says so right there!" is one of the saddest sentences in any chat. Yes, it says so right there. And it still didn't land.

Facts are in the text, and they can be misread too. Especially when things get emotional, nobody reads carefully anymore. We skim, get caught on the one half-sentence that hurts, and fill the rest with our own interpretation. That rarely has anything to do with bad intentions, it's flooding again. When your inner alarm is going off, you take in new information worse, written information included.

So when someone "missed" something, it usually wasn't ignoring. It was a nervous system that was busy with something else.

And that goes for perception in general, not just reading. Perception isn't a window, it's more like a pair of glasses, and feelings are very eager opticians. When we're sad or tense, we read anything ambiguous as negative. A short "Okay." becomes coldness, a late reply becomes punishment, a joke becomes a jab. Kruger and Epley, by the way, didn't only show that senders overestimate their tone. Recipients, too, were far too sure they'd read the tone correctly.

A lot of what we experience as hurt was never meant that way. That doesn't make the pain any less real. But before replying, it's worth asking myself one small question: was that said, or was that what I heard?

Four Steps, One Sentence

So what do we do? Psychologist Marshall B. Rosenberg developed a model for this, Nonviolent Communication. It has four parts: observation, feeling, need, request. On paper it sounds like you have to file every feeling in quadruplicate. In practice it's more of a habit you slowly pick up, like putting the milk back in the fridge.

Let's take a classic. Someone who matters to me hasn't replied since yesterday. My first draft, unfiltered, straight from the gut:

"You really make me feel like I don't matter to you."

Everything is in there except me. The sentence is entirely about the other person: what they do, what they think, what they're doing to me. Whoever reads it has exactly two options. Defend themselves or feel guilty. Neither leads to me.

Now the four steps.

Observation. What actually happened, the way a camera would record it? "I haven't heard from you since yesterday." Not "You never get in touch", that's already an interpretation. And watch out for "always" and "never". They're almost never true, and the other person is guaranteed to find the one exception.

Feeling. What's going on inside me? "I'm restless and a little sad." Rosenberg's core idea: what the other person does is the trigger of my feeling, not its cause. The cause lies with me, and that's not a question of guilt, it's an address.

Need. Why does this hit me? "I need the reassurance that we're okay." This is often where it gets honest for the first time, with myself too.

Request. What do I actually wish for? "Could you drop me a quick message when you have a moment? A heart is enough." A request is allowed to get a no. If it isn't, it's a demand in an evening gown.

Together it sounds like this:

"I haven't heard from you since yesterday and I'm a bit restless. I just need the reassurance that we're okay. Could you drop me a quick message when you have a moment? A heart is enough. ❤️"

A request is allowed to get a no. If it isn't, it's a demand in an evening gown.

Longer, yes. But this sentence leaves the other person a third option the first one didn't: simply to answer.

The Trap After "I Feel"

When I asked Melodie how else to put it without it sounding like pressure, my idea was simple: "You make me feel" becomes "I feel". Away from you, towards me. Melodie thought that was exactly right.

And it is, as a first step. It just depends on what comes next. And that's where there's a rug edge you've known for years and still trip over every single time.

Rosenberg says: when "I feel" is followed by "that", "like" or "as if", it's no longer a feeling. It's a thought that has thrown on a feeling costume.

"I feel like you're ignoring me."

The test is easy: replace "I feel" with "I think". "I think you're ignoring me." Fits perfectly. So it was never a feeling, just an assumption about the other person, wrapped a little more kindly. The blame is still there. It's just wearing slippers now.

Even trickier are the words that look like feelings and aren't. Rosenberg calls them pseudo-feelings: ignored, rejected, abandoned, misunderstood, manipulated, used, betrayed. They feel like feelings, they hurt like feelings. But each of them describes what the other person is supposedly doing to me. "I feel ignored" really means: you're ignoring me.

Underneath lie the real feelings, and they're usually much quieter:

  • Behind "ignored" there's often: lonely, sad, insecure.
  • Behind "abandoned": hurt, scared, exhausted.
  • Behind "misunderstood": frustrated, helpless, alone.

The quiet words are harder to say. They don't point at anyone, only at me. That's exactly why they land.

Rejected

There's one word on that list I need to look at more closely, because it lives at my place: rejected.

I have RSD. If you know my writing, you know what that means. For everyone else, in one sentence: rejection, real or only suspected, hits me with full force.

The tricky part: expecting rejection is often worse than the rejection itself. A missing reply isn't rejection yet. But in my head, rejection has already taken off its coat, sat down and asked for a cup of tea.

Now look at the four steps again. With RSD, the interpretation comes before the observation. Before I've even registered what happened, I already know what it means. That's why the first step is the most important and the hardest for me: what actually happened? A message hasn't been answered. That's all. The rest is a story, and I'm a very diligent author.

The second step even helps me directly. In 2007, Matthew Lieberman showed in a brain scanner that naming a feeling dampens the activity of the amygdala, the part of the brain that sounds the alarm. "I'm sad" isn't just a kinder sentence for the other person. It's a small brake for my own nervous system. A pseudo-feeling like "rejected" doesn't brake. It pours fuel on the fire.

What all this doesn't mean: that my pain is wrong. It's real, it hurts, and I don't have to swallow it. It also doesn't mean I sign my loved ones up as permanent reassurance providers who confirm every ten minutes that they're still there. A request is allowed to get a no, mine too. Only the address changes. Instead of "You're hurting me" I say "Something hurts". And suddenly the other person can help instead of having to defend themselves.

The Beautiful Sentences Too

"You make me feel loved."

Who wouldn't want to read that? I certainly would. And still, it's worth a second look, because the sentence is built exactly like its unfriendly twin "You make me feel like I don't matter to you". In both cases, the other person is sitting at the control panel of my feelings. Once the right button gets pressed, once the wrong one. But the panel is always on the other side.

Said once, a sentence like that is a gift. As a pattern, it becomes a job. "You make me happy." "I'd be lost without you." "You're the only one who understands me." All lovingly meant. But whoever hears this often eventually also hears the sentence that's never said out loud: and if you ever don't, you'll make me unhappy. On the evening the other person is tired, has a headache or simply needs some space, the declaration of love turns into a job description they failed to meet.

Rosenberg goes one step further here, and it's a surprising one. He counts praise and compliments as alienating communication too. His reasoning: a compliment like "You're great" is a judgment, just a positive one. Whoever judges sits on the judge's bench, even if the verdict is kind. And you learn almost nothing about the person giving the praise.

Honestly, I find that a little strict. I don't want to take anyone's beautiful sentences away, and I'll keep telling people they're great. But his alternative is so lovely that it deserves a place here. For him, appreciation has three parts: what the other person concretely did, which need of mine it met, and how I feel about it.

So instead of "You make me feel loved", maybe:

"That you remembered my appointment yesterday and asked how it went made me so happy. I need someone to think along with my everyday life. I felt warm inside the whole evening. ❤️"

The second sentence isn't a job. It's a thank you for something that already happened. Nobody has to deliver anything for it except to be happy about it. And the other person now knows exactly what reaches me, which is worth far more than a general "You're great" that you can't do anything with except blush.

On the Other Side of the Chat

So far this has been about Sam. But often enough, we're Robin. A "You make me feel" comes in, and the pulse goes up before the sentence is even finished.

The reflex is defence. "I told you there's a lot going on today." Factually that's true, and it still doesn't help, because Sam was never talking about the calendar. Rosenberg suggests something else: look for the feeling and the need behind the accusation. Translate the sentence before answering it.

"There's always a lot going on with you" then becomes: Sam feels alone right now and needs a sign. And the reply could look like this:

"Sounds like you're a bit alone with your day right now. I'm really swamped today, but I'm thinking of you and I'll get back to you once things calm down. ❤️"

The lovely thing about Rosenberg's approach: the guess doesn't have to be right. Even a wrong guess helps the other person find their own feeling more precisely. "Nah, not alone, more insecure" is already a much better conversation than "What's that supposed to mean?".

By the way, Robin did one thing exactly right in the chat above: asked. "What do you mean by that?" is an invitation. Sam just turned it down with "Nothing".

John Gottman has a term for this that I really like: bids, small offers of connection. Pointing out a pretty bird, a question about someone's day, a "Hey, everything okay with you?". Gottman observed newlywed couples and checked back six years later. The couples still together had turned towards such small offers 86 percent of the time. For the couples who had split up, it was only 33 percent.

In a chat, almost every message is an offer like that. Sam's very first message was one. And turning towards doesn't have to be big. A heart under a message is already an answer that says: I see you.

There's still a boundary that belongs here, though. Translating doesn't mean taking on the blame. I can understand what someone needs and still say: I can't manage that today. Understanding isn't a signature under the accusation. And anyone with RSD is allowed to sort themselves out first as the recipient too, before replying. Twenty minutes, remember? 😉

Still, Robin isn't entirely innocent in this chat. "Yeah sure, just a lot going on today" is pretty curt. And "I told you there's a lot going on today..." carries its own three dots, which roughly mean: weren't you listening? Whoever replies short and cool under stress broadcasts on all four channels just like Sam. A brusque answer is a message too, and sometimes it's the reason the next one arrives with fists already raised.

Communication always has two sides, and neither is perfect. In chats like this there's rarely a perpetrator's side and a victim's side. Usually there are two people who are both at once. Sam hurts and is hurt. So is Robin. Anyone reading this text only to better spot how other people write has read half of it.

No Language Police

Nonviolent Communication can be used as a weapon too. "That wasn't very nonviolent communication" is a judgment itself, just with a technical term attached. And anyone who presses every message through the four steps first will eventually sound like a customer service hotline telling you, very sympathetically, that your parcel is sadly lost.

I also want to be honest about the research. Reviews of Nonviolent Communication find positive effects, more empathy, better handling of conflict. But there are few studies, and many of them aren't methodologically strong. The model is a good tool rather than a proven law of nature.

That's not what this is about for me anyway. I promised at the beginning and I'm happy to say it again: nobody should spend hours wondering whether and how to say something. Whoever stops writing altogether out of sheer phrasing has only moved the problem. A small warning light that flashes briefly at "You make me feel" is enough.

What really helps is almost always the same thing: taking the pressure off. A few things that help me without turning me into a rules lawyer:

  • Read it out loud, neutrally. Kruger and Epley found that people judge better how their message will land if they read it aloud in a neutral voice first. That way you hear the sentence the way it arrives on the other side, without your own mood as subtitles.
  • Write the tone in. If the tone doesn't travel along, you can pack it. "That sounds harsher than I mean it" or a smiley in the right place takes the edge off a message.
  • Ask instead of proving. "I think that came across differently than I meant it. How did you read it?" This question gets asked far too rarely. Instead we keep explaining and explaining, and at some point a bitter thought settles in: you never understand me anyway. Or worse: you don't even want to understand me. When often nobody simply asked what actually arrived.
  • Misunderstanding before intent. Before I assume the other person wanted to hurt me, I assume something landed wrong. That's true far more often.
  • Announce the pause, don't vanish. "I'm too worked up right now, I'll get back to you in half an hour." Important: talk about yourself. Never "You're too worked up right now".
  • One message instead of five. Five messages in a row while the other person is still typing aren't a conversation, they're a downpour. And nobody has to reply right away. A pause isn't an answer, it's a pause.
  • Repair instead of perfection. I'll keep getting it wrong. The sentence "I phrased that badly, let me try again" is worth at least as much as any perfect phrasing. Maybe more, because it shows you're listening to yourself.

And above all: nobody has to win a chat like this. If one person ends up right and both end up sad, both have lost.

Delete the First Three Words

I promised Melodie I'd make a note of this. This is the note. It got a little longer, that tends to happen with my notes.

If you take away only one thing, let it be this: the next time you type "You make me feel", stop for a second. Delete the first three words and see what's left. Usually it's a feeling that was there all along and just didn't have the courage to stand at the front on its own.

I'm practising this from now on. I'll forget, catch myself, and try again.
