This text is for everyone who has ever read a message three times to make sure it was meant to be mean. For everyone with RSD, and for everyone without it who still sometimes turns a full stop into a declaration of war. For everyone who really did get hurt and doesn't know whether to hit back. And for everyone who would rather say "it's fine" than say what hurts.

It's about three things: how to tell whether a sentence was mean or just crooked. What to do when it really was mean. And why silence isn't a solution, even when it feels polite.

I'm not writing this as someone who's mastered it. I'm writing it as someone who's practising. With varying success.

I'm a very good detective. Give me a sentence and I'll find the proof in it that someone doesn't like me. I don't need a magnifying glass, a trench coat or an assistant with a moustache. A full stop in the wrong place is enough. A word in quotation marks. A reply that arrives twenty minutes too late.

I've solved hundreds of cases in my career. I was just very rarely right.

That's the uncomfortable part of the story, and I'm putting it first, because otherwise the rest sounds like a self-help book. This text is about what to do when a sentence hurts. About telling apart whether someone was mean or just clumsy. And about what happens when you never say it at all. I've played all three roles: the one who got hit, the one who was misunderstood, and the one who kept quiet. Sometimes in a single evening.

I've solved hundreds of cases in my career. I was just very rarely right.

"ok."

With a full stop. No heart, no emoji, not even an "okay" spelled out. I stared at it for three hours and worked out what I'd done wrong. There was a list. It was long.

The next morning: "Sorry, was on the bus, battery at 2 percent."

It was a full stop. It was only ever a full stop.

A Wardrobe Full of Armour

Written sentences arrive without a face. No smile, no eye-roll, no voice that lifts a little at the end. They stand naked in your inbox, and we dress them in whatever happens to be in the wardrobe. On good days, that's a jumper. On bad days, it's armour, the kind with spikes.

I've written about this before in my texts on communication, and it's still true. But some people have more armour in their wardrobe than others. I'm one of them. RSD, rejection sensitivity, the thing that likes to ride along with ADHD, doesn't turn a cool sentence into a slight. It turns it into a verdict. Not "that was a bit unfriendly", but "this person doesn't like me, never has, and is telling everyone right now". From zero to courtroom in a single breath, and the defendant is always me.

And as if that weren't enough, the people on the other side come from every corner of the world. What sounds brief and matter-of-fact here can come across as cold somewhere else, and what counts as polite there can sound long-winded here. Some people write in their second or third language and reach for the word that happens to be at hand, not the one they meant. Some think along completely different lines, because they grew up differently or their brain is wired differently. A sentence that lands on me like a slap might, on the other side, simply be a sentence.

The nasty thing about that verdict is that it doesn't feel like a guess. It feels like knowledge. And that's exactly the moment you lash out. Not because you're cruel, but because you believe you're defending yourself.

I want to say two things about this, and both are true at once. The feeling is real. And it's still not proof.

A word in quotation marks. I'd written it the way you do in German when a word has two meanings. A wink, a double meaning, nothing more.

On the other side it landed like a slap. For a whole day, a story built itself there, a story in which I wanted to hurt.

I knew nothing about it. I did laundry that day.

Sorting

Before you react, you have to sort. That sounds like office work, and that's exactly what it is. A mailbox with two compartments: misunderstanding and intent. The only problem is that RSD automatically throws everything into the second one and then locks the door.

A few questions help me sort. None of them is proof, but together they make a picture.

Is it the first time? A single crooked sentence is usually just that, a crooked sentence. Meanness rarely comes alone. It has siblings, and they look alike.

Did it come straight to me? Misunderstandings happen in conversation, one on one, mid-sentence. Meanness often needs an audience. A post, a comment, a remark in the group where everyone knows who's meant and nobody can say it.

Does the other side gain anything? Someone who phrases something clumsily gains nothing from it. Someone who's being mean does: attention, agreement, a place that used to belong to someone else.

Would I read it the same way if it came from my best friend? That's the most honest question, and the most uncomfortable. If the answer is no, the problem might not be in the sentence, but in what I already believed about the sender.

And if I still don't know which compartment it belongs in? Then it goes in the first. Until proven otherwise.

That's not naivety. A misunderstanding you treat like meanness becomes meanness. Whoever hits back where no one ever hit has thrown the first punch.

That's why I now assume, as a rule, that nobody means any harm. Not because the world is so nice, but because doubt has a tone of its own. Whoever doubts quickly reads bad intent into things, no matter how well they were meant. And then you're no longer replying to the sentence that's actually there, but to the one you added in your head.

"That hurt me. I think it's a misunderstanding. Please tell me it is one."

That was the message after the day with the word in quotation marks. I explained. It was one. Done.

A misunderstanding that died in an afternoon, because someone asked instead of judging. Unspectacular. It didn't need a post.

That message is the best tool I know, and it has three parts. First the feeling: that hurt me. Then the assumption that holds the door open: I think it's a misunderstanding. And then the request that lets the other person walk through that door. Nobody has to defend themselves, nobody has to win. There's just one question and the chance of a good answer.

And the question goes to the sender. Not to everyone else. That's the big temptation when a sentence hurts: screenshot, over into another chat, "How do you read this?" It feels like reassurance. In truth, it's the start of a story in which the one person who actually knows doesn't get a say. A friend can only tell you how they read the sentence. What was meant, only the person who wrote it knows. Ask five others first, and you end up with five opinions, one rumour and still no answer. And rumours have the unfortunate habit of running faster than apologies.

You don't need movie courage for this. You just need enough distance between reading and replying to take the armour off again. For me, that's sometimes ten minutes. Sometimes a night. The message doesn't get worse for it.

When It's Intentional

Sometimes, though, it isn't a misunderstanding. Then it belongs in the second compartment, and rightly so.

A group chat, Sunday evening. Someone writes: "Some people should ask themselves why everyone always leaves in the end." No name. Two hearts underneath, a third after three minutes.

Nobody asked who was meant. That was the answer.

A well-built piece of meanness has no exit. Fight back and you prove you were meant. Stay silent and it stays up. So what then?

Less than you'd think, and that's the good news.

Don't reply. Every reply feeds the story, and stories nobody feeds get very thin eventually. It feels like losing. It isn't. It's just quieter than winning.

Tell your own version, but not to the audience. Directly, calmly, without a folder of evidence. To the few people you care about who need to know. Everyone else is free to believe what they like. That sounds harsh, but it's surprisingly restful.

Draw a line. No counterattack, no counter-post. Just the quiet sentence to yourself: I'm not building a bridge to this person anymore. A door you close without slamming it.

And one more thing, because I had to learn it myself. Even real meanness isn't a verdict on you. It says something about the person who built it: about their need for an audience, for being right, for control. About you, it only says that you were in the way. RSD will tell you something else. RSD lies at this point, and with great conviction.

"It's Fine"

Now the part I find hardest, because in it I'm not only the one who got hit.

Long after, in the middle of a completely different conversation: "You pushed me aside back then."

I didn't know. I'd never known. Back then someone had smiled, or what passes for it in a chat, and written "all good".

It wasn't all good. It had been lying in a drawer the whole time, and I didn't have the key.

There's a way of dealing with hurt that looks so polite you mistake it for strength: brushing it aside. "It's fine." "Not a big deal." "Forget it." You don't want a fight, you don't want to seem oversensitive, you want to protect the peace. All understandable.

Only what you brush aside doesn't disappear. It moves into a drawer, and there it piles up. Every new little thing joins it, neatly folded. At some point the drawer is full, and then everything comes out at once, usually on a day when it doesn't fit at all, and usually as an accusation. The other side stands there and doesn't understand where the storm came from, because they never saw the weather.

That's what's actually unfair about brushing things aside, however well meant: it takes away the other person's chance. The chance to apologise. The chance to explain. The chance to do better next time. You can't fix a door you don't know is sticking.

And yes, I know this from the other side too. I've brushed things aside as well. Not with "it's fine", but with silence. When something hurt, I'd slowly pull back, so it would hurt less if it ended. I even wrote that down once, as an explanation of how I work, and thought it was honesty. It was just a very tidy way of saying nothing. The other side only noticed when I was already halfway gone.

Brushing aside and hitting back look like opposites. They're siblings. In both cases, the hurt doesn't land where it belongs. One time it goes outward as an attack, the other time inward as silence. And both times, the other person never gets the chance to make it right.

What helps? Saying it. Small, early, without accusation. "That just now stung a little. Nothing big, I just didn't want to swallow it." That's all. A sentence that asks for nothing but listening.

And if the right moment has passed? Then the wrong one will do. "I should have said this back then, I didn't, and it's still sitting crooked in me." Said late isn't as good as said early. But it's infinitely better than never. And much better than someday in a fight, as ammunition.

What It Costs Everyone Else

This part belongs here too, and it's uncomfortable.

Living with RSD means needing reassurance. That's not weakness, it's a need, roughly the way someone with cold hands needs gloves. But reassurance has a price, and other people pay it. Anyone who has to follow up on every cool message, who wants every "ok." explained, who needs confirmation after every silence, turns the people around them into an on-call service. And people on call get tired. Not because they like you less, but because even love needs to clock off sometime.

And then there's something nasty. If I assume bad intent behind every sentence, at some point people stop telling me anything at all. They start weighing their words before they talk to me. And then I get exactly the coolness I was afraid of. Only this time I built it myself, brick by brick, with great attention to detail.

So I have to learn to calm myself down first, before I need others to calm me down. Not always, not perfectly, but more often. What helps me:

Time between reading and replying. A night, if possible. Armour comes off more easily in the morning.

The best-friend question. Would I read it like this if it came from someone I trust?

Two columns. On the left, what I believe. On the right, what I know. The left column is usually long. The right is often a single line.

Ask honestly, not as a trap. "Is everything okay between us?" is a question. "Is everything okay between us, because you're texting so weirdly?" is an accusation with a question mark.

Half past two in the morning. The message is finished. It's long, it's well written, and it would hurt. Every word lands. My thumb is already on the arrow.

I delete it. Character by character, so it takes longer.

Then I type one line, just one. With a question mark at the end.

One Question

That's really all this text wants to say, and it could have been printed on a calendar. But calendar quotes only help if you know when you need them. Usually you only remember them once the message has already gone.

So, for the next time a sentence hurts: take off the armour before you reply. Sort before you judge. And if you don't know which compartment it belongs in, ask. Directly, without accusation, with a door in it: That hurt me. I think it's a misunderstanding. Please tell me it is one.

And if something of yours is still lying in a drawer, neatly folded and long gone yellow: take it out. Say it. Even if it's late. Especially then.

Most of my cases could have been solved with a single question. Ask yours. ❤️
