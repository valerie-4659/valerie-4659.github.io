You wrote two hours ago. Nothing special, something about your day, and at the end you added that one word you thought about for a second before sending.

Since then: nothing.

And in those two hours your head has produced more text than you have all week. It has taken the sentence apart. It has built three explanations, two of them genuinely unpleasant. It went into the timeline once, briefly, to see whether anything had happened there, and found something it won't be forgetting now.

None of this happened. It isn't written anywhere. It's there anyway, and in about forty minutes you'll either send a message you'll regret tomorrow or say nothing at all, which isn't better.

Welcome to the difficult part.

When It Blows Up

Arguing online has one property that separates it from every argument at a kitchen table: it doesn't stop on its own.

Offline an argument ends because somebody stands up, because dinner's ready, because eventually you're too tired to stay angry. You share a room, and the room forces you back towards each other. Online there is no room. There are two people sitting alone in their flats, staring at a field where nothing is happening.

First check whether it's even a conflict. The majority of online arguments are two people fighting a misreading. Before you defend yourself, ask: "how did that land with you?" If the answer describes something you never meant, the argument is already over, you just don't know it yet.

Account for the language itself. If one or both of you is writing in a second language, a good share of your arguments happen not between you but between two dictionaries. Under strain your vocabulary shrinks to whatever's nearest, and that's rarely the friendliest word. Literally translated sentences almost always sound harsher than intended - German reads like an order in English even when nobody was ordering anything. At precisely the moment you need to be most exact, you're speaking in the language at least one of you is least exact in. So: short sentences, no idioms, no irony. Irony is always the first thing to fall over the language border.

A pause needs an end time. The single most important move there is. "I need an hour, I'll be back at eleven" is care. The same thing without a time is a threat, however it was meant. A pause with no appointment attached is, online, indistinguishable from being left.

Use the one superpower of this medium. You can delete the paragraph before it goes out. You can't do that at a kitchen table. Write down everything that shoots through your head, at full sharpness - then select it, delete it, and write the sentence you actually mean.

And if you know you tend to overshoot: use an AI. Unromantic, but practical. Write what you want to say at full volume, with everything in it, and have it rephrased. What comes back is the same statement without the barbs that would only have been collateral damage. You give up nothing: the accusation stays, the boundary stays, the anger stays. All that's gone is the one clause you'd be apologising for tomorrow. If you boil over fast, you now have something standing between the impulse and the send button for the first time. The same goes for translation: a paragraph that went through the machine and is accurate beats a homemade one that smoulders.

Don't quote. You can go and find the exact wording of something from three weeks ago and lay it out like evidence. It even works. It just doesn't end anything - all it demonstrates is that every word they write to you can eventually be used against them. After that they write more carefully. And careful is the opposite of close.

Not in front of an audience. Where you argue doesn't matter - DM, Discord, Signal, carrier pigeon, as long as it's only the two of you reading. What doesn't work is arguing with spectators: the pointed remark somewhere other people are sitting, the meaningful post in the timeline, the friend who gets the summary that evening and picks a side from then on. The moment third parties are watching, you're no longer arguing about the thing, you're arguing about the figure you cut while doing it. And people defend that considerably longer than necessary.

If it starts going in circles, change the format. Text amplifies bad feelings and filters good ones. Anger arrives at a hundred percent, warmth at maybe thirty. If the same round is running for the third time, a better paragraph won't help. Sleep on it and start again tomorrow rather than going a fourth lap tonight.

And the reconciliation has to be said out loud. Offline a hug does it and everyone knows where they stand. Online there is no sign for it unless somebody makes one. "As far as I'm concerned this is finished, I'm not carrying it any further." Without that sentence one of you will haul it around for weeks, waiting for an aftershock that never comes.

Jealousy, or: the Problem With the Open Curtain

Offline you see about five percent of your partner's social life. You know there are colleagues, you've heard about a friend, there's a regular pub night. You never see how somebody laughs there.

Online you see everything. Every heart under every picture. Every reply, every exchange, every small kindness at two in the morning. You have access to a volume of data nobody twenty years ago had about their partner, and your brain was not built for that volume.

And the catch isn't the quantity. The catch is that you see the behaviour and not the meaning. A heart looks identical whether it's politeness, networking, habit or actual interest. You get the gesture without the context, and your imagination falls into the gap, and at night it's rarely generous.

Out of this comes the quiet version, which is far more common than an open argument: the scrolling. You say nothing, you only watch. You build a file. After three weeks you know who liked what and when, and you feel not one bit better for it, considerably worse in fact, and you still haven't gained a single piece of information that means anything.

The way out is the same as everywhere in this piece, just less comfortable: say it. But say the right thing.

Jealousy is almost never about the third person. It's about you. The sentence underneath it usually reads: I'm replaceable. I'm not enough. I'd be the one dropped if things got tight. And if you translate that into "why are you messaging her at two in the morning", then from now on you're negotiating about times of day instead of about what's actually going on.

So take the harder version instead: "I saw that and I felt small. I know there's probably nothing in it. I still didn't want to sit with it on my own."

That isn't an accusation, it isn't a demand, and it's remarkably hard to say. It does, however, work.

And for the other side: don't punish honesty. If somebody tells you they had a lovely conversation with someone and you react wounded, you have just very efficiently taught them to tell you nothing next time. Afterwards you feel safer and are less safe. Both at once.

For everyone living poly, all of the above applies, just more often. Poly doesn't mean jealousy doesn't occur - that's a misunderstanding that stubbornly persists, and one I believed myself once. Poly means handling it differently. That handling consists of agreements, of honesty, and of naming the feeling instead of hiding it as a character flaw. Agreements that were never spoken out loud work exactly as badly, incidentally, as everything else that was never spoken out loud.

One more point, and it stings: some jealousy is information. If it doesn't stop, if it isn't attached to an occasion but runs permanently in the background, then it isn't about the third person any more. Then it's about the shape of this relationship, or about something you brought with you that's older than they are. Both can be looked at. Just not while the feeling is still disguised as a question about times of day.

The Empty Field

Fear of abandonment isn't worse online than off. It just has an uninterrupted food supply here.

Offline you know somebody is in the bathroom. You hear the door. You see the car in the drive. There are a hundred small proofs that the world is still fine, and not one of them had to be said out loud.

Online there's a chat where nothing has happened for two hours. And silence carries no meaning, which is the vicious part - it means precisely nothing, and it looks identical for "asleep" and for "done with you". A head inclined towards abandonment fear fills that emptiness in about eleven seconds, and never with the friendly option.

What helps is unspectacular.

Know your normal. Not as surveillance, as calibration. What does an ordinary Tuesday look like for you two? When does somebody usually not write? If you know that nothing ever arrives between three and seven anyway, your head can't build a story out of it. The rituals from Part 2 do exactly this job.

Say it small instead of acting it out big. "My head is doing nonsense right now, tell me quickly that everything's fine" is a sentence that costs the other person ten seconds and saves you two hours. That's a good deal, and it isn't weakness, it's maintenance.

And don't test. That's the reflex that wrecks things. Sending a cool "ok." to see what happens. Withdrawing to see whether anyone follows. Not answering for a while so they can see how it feels for once. It's the same move as in Part 1, only later in the relationship and with higher stakes. It answers not a single question. It just manufactures real distance in order to test imaginary distance.

And then the honest part, which I'd rather not write: this fear cannot be soothed from outside.

Reassurance has a half-life of about an hour. If you need it daily, your partner becomes a supplier, and suppliers get tired eventually. Not because they love you less, but because nobody can deliver a proof that lasts long enough. "I'm not going anywhere" is used up again by tomorrow.

That doesn't mean you have to carry it alone. It only means your partner is the wrong place for the repair. They're the right place to know about it.

When Your Own Head Works Against You

There are days when you read everything wrong, and you even know it.

A neutral sentence arrives as coldness. A delay arrives as rejection. A full stop where there usually isn't one becomes evidence. And the empty field is roughly the worst possible stimulus you can offer a head that finds rejection everywhere anyway.

I know this from the inside. Rejection dysphoria belongs to me, the way ADHD does, probably autism, and a solid helping of abandonment fear. That isn't jewellery I've hung on myself, it's simply the equipment I walk around with.

And because plenty of people know the term without knowing what it actually does, it's worth a few more paragraphs.

What it is. Rejection sensitive dysphoria appears in no diagnostic manual. It's a term out of the ADHD corner, a description rather than an official label. What it describes is an extreme sensitivity to anything that smells of rejection - criticism, being turned down, a sentence that's too short, a face that feels wrong for a fraction of a second.

What it isn't: sensitivity in the sense of "takes everything to heart". That sounds like a character trait you could get a grip on with a bit of backbone, and that is not remotely what this is.

How it feels. It arrives faster than any thought. Between the stimulus and the feeling there is no assessment, no interpretation, no "hm, what might she have meant". It's simply there, physically, in the chest, in the stomach, and it doesn't feel like a guess. It feels like a fact. As though you had just received a message reading, word for word: I don't like you any more.

Your reason turns up about twenty seconds later and points out, entirely correctly, that the person is probably just in the shower. This changes precisely nothing. You can know with total certainty that it's nonsense and still feel it completely. Knowing doesn't defuse it.

And it isn't that you invent the rejection out of nothing. You read real signals - a shorter answer, a longer gap, a missing emoji. Those are genuine observations. It's just that the apparatus turning them into meaning is calibrated far too sensitively, and it resolves every ambiguity in the same direction every time.

Why you then behave oddly. Because an alarm like that has to go somewhere, and there are essentially three routes.

The first is withdrawal. You close up, go monosyllabic, disappear for a few hours. From outside it looks like sulking or disinterest. From inside it's an attempt to get to safety before the blow lands.

The second is attack. You go in first, accuse them of exactly what you're afraid of, sometimes fairly sharply. It's the reflex to strike rather than be struck, and it's as destructive as it sounds.

The third is the most inconspicuous and, long term, the most expensive: over-adaptation. You suddenly become very nice, very available, very low-maintenance. You apologise for things there's nothing to apologise for. You make yourself so small and so pleasant that nobody could possibly have a reason to leave. That works for a while and costs you everything else.

What all three have in common: from outside they look like a wild overreaction to nothing. And that's even true, if you measure the events. It just isn't true if you measure the experience.

And afterwards comes the shame. That's the part hardly anyone talks about. When the storm has passed and you see what you just staged over an unread message, it's enormously embarrassing. So next time you hide it, and because you hide it nobody can help, and because nobody helps it's worse the time after that. A very neatly constructed trap.

Online this whole thing is in its stress test. Text produces ambiguity constantly, delays here aren't an oversight but the architecture, and the empty field is the perfect projection surface - nothing is written there, so anything fits.

What helps isn't getting rid of the feeling. That isn't happening, certainly not tonight.

For the affected side: name the state instead of acting out of it. "I'm in the hole right now. I know this isn't reality. I need twenty minutes." That says everything that needs saying, and you've bought yourself twenty minutes in which you break nothing. At this point the difference between a bad evening and an argument is exactly one sentence.

And say it in advance, not mid-episode. Anyone who knows their head works like this should explain it while things are calm - not as an excuse, as an operating manual. A partner who knows what's happening reacts differently from one who only sees a sudden coldness.

For the other side: short and clear beats long and soothing. The impulse is to write a lot. Long explanations, many reassurances, everything very soft. That helps in the moment and, over time, trains an expectation nobody can meet. A brief "no, all fine, I was out shopping" often works better than three paragraphs, because it keeps the thing small instead of making it large.

And don't take it personally when you briefly become the enemy in these moments. You aren't the target. You just happen to be the person it's currently attaching itself to.

"I Don't Want to Disappoint You"

It's half eleven. Somebody made time for you, maybe stayed up specially, and you notice there's nothing left in the tank. So you write: "Sorry, I'm completely exhausted, I can't any more."

And then you attach the sentence I've written far too often myself: "I don't want to disappoint you."

The remarkable thing is that the exhaustion is usually no problem at all for them. They may even have expected it, because it's simply your bedtime and people do sleep. There was nothing to forgive until you apologised for it.

So the apology doesn't repair anything. It creates something.

Because it doesn't come alone. It brings a task with it: tell me it's okay. Please don't be disappointed. Reassure me before I go to sleep. A plain fact - I'm tired - has just become a small emotional operation that somebody else now has to carry out. That isn't ill will. It's still pressure.

And then comes the part that gets genuinely expensive. They don't want you feeling guilty. They like you, they can see what it does to you, and they draw the logical conclusion: from now on they avoid situations where this could happen. They stop staying up specially. They stop asking whether you've got time. They move things around so as not to put you in a position where you'd have to decline.

From outside that looks like fading interest. It's actually consideration. And the result is that you get less of the exact thing you wanted.

Where it comes from is usually clear enough. You care about other people, you feel their disappointment faster than your own, and somewhere in the background the old conviction is running that you're an imposition when you don't function. For me that comes in the same package as everything else in this chapter.

What helps is two sentences, both shorter than what you'd otherwise write.

State the fact, no attachments. "I'm done, I'm going to sleep. This was lovely." Finished. No apology, no chain of justifications, no plea not to take it personally.

It helps to be clear about what an apology actually claims: that you did something wrong. Being tired isn't wrong. Apologise for having a body and you teach the other person that this body is a problem. Nobody wants that, and everybody does it.

And let their disappointment stand, if there is any. They're allowed to be disappointed. Disappointment isn't an accusation and isn't an emergency, it's the price of them caring about you, and it passes on its own. What doesn't pass on its own is a person who has learned that their disappointment sends you into a panic. They stop showing it - and now you both have one fewer feeling that's permitted to exist between you.

For the other side, the counterpart: don't make a ritual of it. A brief "all good, sleep well" beats three paragraphs of reassurance. Reward the apology with a big soothing performance every time and you guarantee it comes back.

Arguments, jealousy, abandonment fear and bad days all have one thing in common: they're all made of something somebody thought and didn't say.

That's the bad news, because it means you're the one who's up. And it's the good news, for the same reason.

So: there's something you're carrying around right now. A small slight from last week, something you saw and didn't want to raise, a feeling you're slightly ashamed of.

Write it down. Not as an accusation, as a weather report.

Tonight.
