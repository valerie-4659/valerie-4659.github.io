Part 2: On the ones who give themselves away

Whatever you want

"What do you want to do tonight?"
"Whatever you want."

The sentence comes out so fast that it's already out there before I've even thought about it. And then it turns into a lovely evening, really, exactly the way the other side wanted it. Only later, when it's quiet, do I notice that I don't even know what I actually wanted. I didn't forget. I just never checked.

I know this sentence in a hundred variations. "It's fine." "Doesn't matter." "No problem, I can do it later." Every single one of them is harmless, friendly, lovingly meant. But added up, they make a life in which I'm always there and rarely show up.

This text is for those who always step back. For those who automatically pass on the best piece. And for those who wake up one day and realise they've given a lot and are left standing empty-handed.

Why it feels like love

The tricky thing about stepping back is: it feels good. At least at first.

Giving makes you happy. When I make someone laugh, when I shape an evening so the other person lights up, when I feel needed, that's real joy. There's nothing wrong with that. And often it really is love that's stepping back. You want the other person to be okay, and your own need suddenly seems so small next to that.

But sometimes there's something else behind it, quieter and older. Anyone who learned early on that you're liked as long as you don't cause any trouble eventually stops causing trouble at all. Anyone who's often been told they're "too much" tries to be as little as possible. And anyone who's afraid of being abandoned starts believing at some point that you're only allowed to stay as long as you're useful.

Then stepping back isn't just love anymore. Then it's insurance. Every day you pay a small premium out of yourself, hoping that when disaster strikes, you won't be left standing alone.

How strongly this shows up is different for everyone. Some people swallow a wish now and then when it suits and barely notice. Others arrange their whole life around the people close to them and at some point no longer know where the others end and they begin. For some it only shows up in one particular relationship, for others everywhere, in love, in friendships, at work. It depends on what you've been through, how much fear is involved, and also on who you're dealing with. A little stepping back is part of every relationship; that's consideration. The stronger it gets, the more important it is to take a look in time.

How you become invisible

Nobody becomes invisible overnight. It happens in small steps, and each one looks harmless.

A wish you swallow because it isn't a good moment. A plan you postpone so it suits the other side better. A hurt you smile your way past because you don't want an argument. An "I'm fine" when you're not, because the other person already has enough on their plate. A little less room every time.

The nasty part is: the others get used to it. Not out of malice, but because people get used to anything that works reliably. You become a permanent part of the scenery. Stable, uncomplicated, always there. And that's exactly why, at some point, nobody asks anymore what you actually want. Why would they? You never wanted anything.

From the outside, it looks like a harmonious relationship. From the inside, it feels like you're slowly being erased. Not by the others, but by yourself.

When giving becomes a burden

There's another side that givers rarely think about: giving can put the other person under pressure. Someone who's constantly showered with time, attention, love, small and big gestures eventually feels obliged to give something back. And when that isn't possible to the same degree, a guilty conscience creeps in. The gift turns into a bill that nobody ever issued and that's still lying on the table.

I often asked: "Is it too much?" The answer was always no. Until, at some point, other sentences came. "You don't have to do so much, I'm not forcing you." "Make yourself important too, not always just me." Back then they sounded to me like care and like withdrawal at the same time. Today I think they were both. And maybe they were also a quiet attempt to balance a scale that had been tipped for a long time.

Because we givers often feed on other people's happiness. If we're honest, we're a little dependent on it, out of fear of giving too little. We want the other person to feel that we really would do anything and that they lack nothing. Only then something important is sometimes missing after all: the room to give something back without always lagging behind. And sooner or later, that imbalance comes due.

Why you end up empty-handed

How it happens is usually the same story. At some point the moment comes when things get tight. Too little time, too little energy, too many people who need something. Something has to give, and where do you cut back? Where it makes the least noise. With the person who never asked for anything. Who always understood. Who'll surely understand this too.

That's the bitter arithmetic of stepping back: if you take up no space, you leave no gap when you go. Not because you were loved less, but because you were so good at not standing out that your absence doesn't stand out either.

And on the inside it isn't any better. You've given away so much of yourself that there's hardly anything left to hold on to. The wishes you ignored for so long don't suddenly come back just because there's room now. You stand there and don't even know what to wish for.

The most painful part I only understood late. Nobody took anything from me. I gave it away, piece by piece, willingly and smiling, with the firm feeling that this was love. It was love, too. Just not for myself.

What can help

I don't want to talk anyone out of giving. If you like to give, keep giving. It's just about not losing yourself along the way.

Notice the reflex. The "whatever you want" comes faster than any thought. You don't have to switch it off right away. It's enough to pause briefly and ask yourself: and what do I actually want? Sometimes the answer really is "I don't mind". But sometimes there's something there that simply never got asked.

That's the bitter arithmetic of stepping back: if you take up no space, you leave no gap when you go.

Say small wishes out loud. Not the big ones straight away. "I'd love to watch a film tonight." "Could you send me a quick message tomorrow morning?" If you haven't wanted anything for a long time, you have to practise wishing again, like a muscle that hasn't been used in ages.

Put yourself on the list. We all have an inner list of people we look after. Many of us aren't on it ourselves. Add yourself. Not at the very top, if that feels wrong. But at all.

Give from fullness, not from fear. A good test: would I still do this if I knew for sure the other person wasn't going to leave? If yes, it's a gift. If no, it's an insurance premium, and you're allowed to cancel it.

Take stock now and then. When did I last receive something I didn't have to earn? If the answer is a long time ago, that's not a reason for guilt, but a reason for a conversation.

Endure a conflict. A "no", a "that wasn't okay for me", an "I need this too". At first it feels like betraying the harmony. But a relationship that can't handle a single wish of yours was never as stable as it looked.

None of this is meant as a reckoning along the lines of "I invested so much and got nothing back". I like giving. It brings me joy, and I have the means to do it. I'll probably always be the one who gives a little more, who wants to spoil a little more, out of gratitude too. But I want to learn to measure it out better, so I don't overwhelm the person I love.

The Biggest Slice

You aren’t loved for disappearing. You’re loved because you’re there, with everything that comes with it, including the wishes, the rough edges and the occasional “no”. Anyone who only loves the version of you that never wants anything is really only loving the space you keep free.

Work on your love without it turning into work. Give without giving yourself away. It isn’t a skill you learn once, but one you practise a little every day.

I’m starting small. The next time someone asks what I want to do tonight, I won’t answer “whatever you want”. I’ll think for a moment, just one breath, and say: “Pizza. Extra cheese. And I want the biggest slice.” 🍕
