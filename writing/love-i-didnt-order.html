The Five-Course Menu

Imagine you're sitting in a little restaurant. You fancy a glass of wine, maybe a bit of bread, and the nice waiter with the charming smile. You order. The waiter nods, disappears into the kitchen - and comes back with a five-course menu, candles, a violinist and a ring on the little dessert plate.

You wanted a glass of wine.

Now you're sitting there, facing a table full of plates you never ordered and a waiter looking at you expectantly. Saying no feels rude. Saying yes feels wrong. So you poke around the first course a little, say "delicious" and hope nobody notices that all you really want is to go home.

This week a friend told me about exactly this kind of table. She likes the playful side of things, the laughter, a bit of teasing, a bit of cheek. That's all she ever ordered. And yet people keep turning up at her door with trays, full of big words and pet names, as if a wink had signed a contract. What bothers her most isn't other people's feelings. It's that nobody asked her first.

She got me thinking. About both sides of that table.

The Waiter Means Well

Before anyone condemns the waiter: he usually means well. He didn't set out to push anyone. He's taken a shine to someone, a little or a lot, and he wants to show it.

So a crush lands on the table. A few days later an "I love you", casually, with a heart attached. Then a pet name that feels so lovely to type. To him, that's affection, not a demand.

What comes back is a heart. A friendly thank you. Warm, but every time with a small detour right at the spot where things get serious.

He doesn't notice. Or he doesn't want to notice, because noticing would hurt. So he serves another course.

Nobody is the villain in this story. One keeps serving because he feels something. The other keeps poking at her plate because she doesn't want to be rude. And that's exactly where it gets interesting.

Why Online, a Glass of Wine Turns Into a Menu So Fast

Online we have no bodies. No smile that says "I'm only playing". No shrug, no raised eyebrow, no tone of voice. We have letters, emojis and pictures. And in that vacuum our minds do what minds love doing most: they fill the gaps. With whatever we're hoping for.

A cheeky kiss emoji in the morning becomes "she's thinking of me". A generated picture in stockings and suspenders becomes "she wants me". Three days of good-morning messages become "we've got something". When maybe it was just a kiss emoji. Some people hand those out like confetti, and confetti isn't a promise.

On top of that, flirting is almost the only currency online that feels like closeness. Anyone who's lonely, who's just come out of a breakup, who hasn't been seen in a long time, holds those coins up to the light and sees gold where there's only brass. Not out of stupidity. Out of hunger.

And then there's "I love you". In English, "love" is a pretty relaxed word - you say "love you" to your best friend on the phone. German has another step in between: between "hab dich lieb" and "ich liebe dich" lies an entire continent. Write online in English and you lose that step, and suddenly affection sounds like a proposal. "Friend" works the same way: before a friend comes an acquaintance, and whether one becomes the other is decided by time, not by an entry on a friends list.

Two years ago I wouldn't have sent anyone so much as a heart after three weeks. These days hearts fly low, and often not because we feel so much, but because we think the other person expects it. And yet a heart can simply be warmth, a small sign of connection. That's lovely, as long as it doesn't demand anything back.

The problem isn't the word. The problem is that "I love you" is a question, even when it looks like a statement. It waits for an echo. And whoever can't give that echo suddenly stands empty-handed in front of a full table.

What It Does to the Other Side

We talk a lot about how much it hurts not to be loved back. I've written enough about that myself. We talk less about what it feels like to be loved when you can't love back. Or don't want to. Or simply have no room for it right now.

Because it doesn't feel like a gift. It feels like a bill.

The person on the other side now has to do something. Reply without hurting. Turn it down without losing the friendship. Keep flirting without raising false hopes. It's a tightrope walk across a freshly waxed floor, and usually you walk it alone.

So you get careful. Your replies get shorter. You answer with a heart instead of a sentence, because you don't know which sentence would be the right one. And that very caution is what the person in love reads as cooling off. They serve more - more hearts, more words, more questions. The other gets even more careful. A wonderfully efficient machine for reliably moving two people away from each other.

And at some point the meanest thought of all arrives: What am I actually doing wrong that this keeps happening? Am I too nice? Too cheeky? Too open? Am I inviting it without noticing?

No. If ten people bring you a menu you never ordered, it's not because of your appetite. Sometimes the problem isn't the person, it's the surroundings. That took me a long time to learn myself.

How to Tell You're Serving Too Much

The nasty part: from the waiter's side, it's hard to see. You're so busy arranging everything nicely. A few signs to watch for:

  • Warmth comes back, but no answer to what you actually said. Your confession gets a heart, not a word.
  • You write paragraphs, she writes lines. Not once, but always.
  • She's happy to flirt along, but the moment it gets serious, she takes the easy exit. A joke, a change of subject, a brief thank you.
  • You're almost always the one who opens the day.
  • You wait for her messages more than you look forward to them.

None of this is a no. But it isn't a yes either. It's a person sitting politely at the table who doesn't know how to say they only wanted a glass of wine.

If ten people bring you a menu you never ordered, it's not because of your appetite.

We all know this from real restaurants. The food was so-so, then the server comes over: "Did you enjoy your meal?" - "Oh yes, absolutely! Could we get the bill, please? By card, thanks." Online, the "oh yes, absolutely" is a heart. And the "bill, please" is the reply that gets a little shorter every day.

Clearing the Plates

What helps is surprisingly unspectacular. Asking. Before you serve. "What is this for you?" isn't an unromantic question, it's the most respectful thing you can say while flirting. It puts the menu in the other person's hands instead of setting a plate in front of them.

And sometimes it also helps to simply put the truth on the table instead of wrapping it in little hearts. "Hey, I want to tell you something. I have strong feelings for you. You don't have to return them, and I don't expect you to. I just want you to know, so maybe my behaviour makes a bit more sense." That isn't a proposal. It's a menu with the prices printed on it. The other person no longer has to guess what's being served and can decide in peace what they'd like.

And when you notice you've already served too much: clear the plates. No drama. No reproach, no hurt silence, no "fine, then". Just say: I think I was a bit much. I'll take that off the table, and I still like you. That's not a defeat. That's hospitality.

The same goes for the other side, just the other way round. An honest "this is a bit much for me right now" is kinder than three weeks of evasive little hearts. It hurts for a moment. But it doesn't leave anyone searching for gold in the dark.

Be honest with each other when something's there. Clear terms sound unromantic at first, but they build a frame, and inside a frame you can move freely. Without one you fumble in the dark, tense up, don't dare anything and feel uneasy without quite knowing why. Sure, the truth hurts. But so does a plaster coming off, and nobody leaves one on for three weeks just because it stings.

And often something happens afterwards that nobody saw coming. Once the pressure's off, the conversation really begins. Suddenly there's room for things that never fit between all those hearts before. The teasing can stay. It just doesn't have to lead anywhere anymore.

Sometimes you simply get to know a person without wanting to marry them straight away.

So take a look at your chats today. Is there someone you've set a whole menu in front of without being asked? Then write to her. Not "sorry, sorry, sorry", just: "I think I've served you more than you ordered. What would you actually like?" And then listen to what she orders 🍷
