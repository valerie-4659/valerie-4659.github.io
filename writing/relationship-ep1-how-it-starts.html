It's just past eleven, and you've written the same message four times now. The first one was too long. The second sounded like a come-on. The third was so noncommittal it could have come from a bot. What's left in the field is "Hey", and you're wondering whether the exclamation mark is too much.

And on the other side there might be someone who's been doing exactly the same thing with your profile for three weeks.

I have a string of failed online relationships behind me. Not failed because of distance, not because of time zones, not because we didn't like each other. They failed because my partners wanted the uncomplicated part. The quick one, the light one, the one that feels good. Talking about feelings - no. Too risky. Too much work. Might get complicated. You don't want to be a burden.

And then it just drains away. Not with a bang, the way water disappears into sand. First one person writes less often. Then both. Then one day you have one more name in your contacts, someone you had a thing with once, and you're not entirely sure what happened.

I know what happened now. Nobody ever said what they felt.

What follows is my attempt to write that down. Not as a rulebook - as the things I've learned, some of them painfully, some of them very beautifully. I'm with AnimeHussy, and we run our relationship almost entirely on text. What I can tell you without it being anyone's business: our love is something you can feel. It keeps growing. And that isn't a trick, it's simply that we're both open and honest with each other, even when it's uncomfortable.

None of this is an instruction manual. I don't have a method, I have experience - most of it expensive, some of it lovely. Some of it will fit you, some of it won't, and part of it will probably strike you as flat wrong. People aren't kits, and nobody has the same problems I do.

Take what's useful and leave the rest.

Let's start at the beginning.

What Runs in the Background Offline, and Simply Isn't There Online

Offline you have crutches, and they're very good ones.

When you're sitting across from someone, half the communication reaches you without any effort on your part. You see that their smile doesn't quite reach their eyes. You hear that their voice is a shade too bright. You register something in the set of their shoulders before you've consciously formed a thought about it. And if you say the wrong thing, you see it in their face immediately and can catch it in the same second.

Those are compensation mechanisms. They catch your mistakes before they become mistakes. Offline you can communicate surprisingly badly for a surprisingly long time without the relationship breaking, because so much other information is running alongside and filling the gaps.

Online nothing runs alongside. There's no face, no tone of voice, no hand on your arm, no shared presence in a room. There are letters. That's it. The other person knows exactly what you've written and not one millimetre more.

Which is why things have to be said out loud online that offline simply happen. Not because online relationships are more complicated, but because there's no safety net. People who communicate well online are communicating well on purpose. There's no other option.

The nice side effect: whatever you learn here, you take with you everywhere. But that's a side effect, not a promise. This is about online.

Just Write to Them

There's a situation that's statistically far more common than anyone assumes: two people find each other interesting. They might both be introverts - that's the textbook case, but it isn't required. Shyness doesn't respect categories. People who give presentations at work without a tremor sit in front of an empty message field at night and go very quiet. Both wait for the other to start. Both read the other's not-starting as disinterest. Both move on and think, shame, really.

Scale that up to a platform with a few million people on it. That isn't a tragedy, it's an epidemic.

The problem isn't cowardice. The problem is that you're thinking too much. It's a mistake that comes out of thought itself - you run the scene through your head until it's grown larger than it ever was. You imagine your message arriving, and in your imagination it lands under a spotlight, in the middle of a silent hall where everyone is watching it be judged. In reality it lands in a notification list, between a crypto bot and someone who liked a photo of a cat.

The worst realistic outcome isn't humiliation. It's nothing. No reply comes, you never find out why, and three days later you've forgotten. That's the floor you're risking. It's fairly soft.

The fear behind it always has the same three voices, and all three have answers.

What will they think of me? - Nothing. Honestly. People think remarkably little about you, and that's the most liberating news in this entire piece.

I'm probably bothering them. - You're bothering someone who voluntarily puts things on the internet so that other people will look at them. That's roughly like apologising for buying something in a shop.

It could be embarrassing. - It could. Maximum damage: no reply. That costs you precisely nothing beyond two minutes and a small sting that'll be gone by Thursday.

So what makes the difference isn't courage, it's what you write.

A lone "Hi" isn't a message, it's a request for labour. You're handing the entire job of inventing a conversation to a stranger who owes you nothing. Some people answer anyway. Most don't, and quite rightly.

A pure compliment on the work is better, but only just. "Your pictures are great" is something anyone posting pictures gets thirty times a day. It's pleasant, it costs nothing, it goes nowhere. Afterwards you're both standing there, it's their move again, and they still have no idea who you are.

Same goes for "you're so pretty". Kindly meant and still a dead end: after the "thanks" the conversation is over, and you both know it.

What works is remarkably simple: show that you actually looked, and attach something of yourself.

"That picture you posted yesterday reminded me of something and I ended up crying. Not because I'm sad - it was a good memory. Thank you for that. I've been following you for a while and I wanted to tell you."

That's not a come-on, it's not a work order, and it's genuinely hard not to answer. It contains something only you could have written, and it gives them a direction to write back in.

It doesn't have to be tears. The principle is just this: don't say what the picture is, say what it did to you. Anyone who knows the craft can give a technical compliment. What you felt is the one thing nobody else can supply.

And then the other trap, the one that catches thoughtful people: don't write an essay. If your first message is the length of a job application, that isn't a sign of interest, it's a sign of pressure. They read it and feel immediately that an expectation is already in the room, one they never ordered.

Three sentences. Really. Three sentences will do for anything.

For the Shy Ones: This Is Your Home Ground

I think this gets lost in all the nerves. You're online. This is the place where shyness is almost consequence-free.

Offline you have two seconds to say something, and everyone watches you go red, and then comes the pause where time stops and you'd like to sink into the floor. Here you have twenty minutes if you need them. You can think. You can rewrite. You can delete three times and start over, and the only person on this planet who will ever know is you.

The person on the other end sees the finished thing. Not the four attempts, not your hands, not your heart, which is currently very loud. Just three calm sentences on a screen, written by someone who evidently knows what they want.

That isn't deception. It's simply a property of the medium, and it happens to work in your favour.

"But what do I say after hello?"

That's where most people get stuck, and the answer is unspectacular: nothing new. You don't have to invent a second topic. You continue the one that's already there.

They replied to your observation? Then ask about the part you actually want to know. Why they did it that way. Whether it was deliberate. What they were thinking. The thread is already in front of you, you just have to not drop it.

And one move that almost always helps: end your message in a way that can be answered. No obligatory question, no forced question mark, but leave a door instead of putting up a wall. "I love this" is a wall. "I love this, I can never get that kind of light myself" is a door - someone can walk through it if they want to.

The Art Isn't Being Interesting. The Art Is Being Curious.

This is the relief introverts need at this point, because extroverts have the advantage when it comes to selling themselves and always will. Not so with the other half. Curiosity is quiet work, and introverts are often rather good at it, because they listen more than they talk anyway.

Ask about what actually interests you about them. Not what one asks. The difference is instantly visible in text, even if nobody can name it.

But please don't kick the door in. Some people confuse interest with speed and ask about the worst childhood memory in the third message. That isn't depth, that's a raid.

You get to know people in waves. Layer by layer.

Anyone who's seen Shrek already has the image - the donkey explaining to the ogre that ogres are like onions, because they have layers. The comparison is better than an animated comedy deserves. Right at the centre sits the core, and around it lie layer after layer: fears, experiences, irony, sarcasm, defensiveness, that unfortunate business from four years ago that doesn't get discussed. Psychologists have been using the same onion since the seventies for precisely this phenomenon, just with less charm.

Each layer gives you a little more. But they don't fall away by themselves. You have to challenge them a bit.

That sounds more violent than it is. Concretely: ask one question more than politeness requires. Take the sarcastic answer seriously for a moment instead of laughing along and moving on. Don't stop at the first evasion - but do stop at the second.

The evasions themselves are the most interesting part of the whole business.

Some people let you remarkably close remarkably fast and still stay on the surface. Two days in you seem to be right in the middle of things, and two months later you realise you still know nothing. Every further attempt bounces off - off sarcasm, off irony, off a suddenly very silly tone that reliably turns any serious subject into confetti.

And then there's the version that makes itself small. "I don't know what you see in me." "I'm honestly pretty boring." That sounds like modesty and is still a layer - one that hands you the work and simultaneously makes sure nobody looks any closer.

In my experience there are two rather different intentions behind this, and you only learn to tell them apart over time. Some people are pushing you away because it's getting too close, and they mean it exactly as it looks: please go. Others are building hurdles to see whether anyone clears them - because so far nobody has stayed, and they've slowly stopped expecting it.

You can't tell from any single sentence. It only shows in what happens after you've stayed, warmly, a while longer. Someone who genuinely wants to close up will close up. Someone who was testing gets a shade softer.

And here's the line you don't cross. Layers aren't a lock to be picked. If someone holds one shut, that's an answer too, and you respect it. Maybe it opens in four months. Maybe never, and then that's how it is.

How many layers someone has varies enormously. Some have a great many, because they learned to deflect everything rather than look weak. Some have few and are surprisingly close after two weeks. And very occasionally you fall straight through all of them without anyone doing anything, because you happen to be the right person at the right time.

That's a gift, not a skill. You can't manufacture it, you can't force it, and you shouldn't make it the standard. Anyone waiting for it to always feel like that will be waiting a long time. The normal case is layer by layer, with patience, and the normal case is completely fine.

"But I'm not interesting."

That's the sentence that prevents most good conversations, and it rests on a thinking error.

Being interesting isn't a property you either have or don't, like freckles. It's something that happens between two people or doesn't. Nobody is interesting alone in an empty room. The most fascinating people I know all consider themselves fairly unremarkable, and the ones rock-solidly convinced of their own fascination become exhausting after about forty minutes.

On top of that: the part you think is boring is the material. Your job, your commute, your volunteering, your broken washing machine, your absurd specialist knowledge about something nobody cares about - that's what closeness gets built out of. Not the highlights. Everyone already knows your highlights from your profile.

And here's the trap that springs at exactly this point: people who think they're uninteresting start performing interesting. They build the polished version, the print-ready anecdote, the self that comes across well. That works for a surprisingly long time and still goes nowhere, because in the end the other person falls for someone who doesn't exist. More on that later - it's the central mistake in online relationships and it deserves a chapter of its own.

For now this is enough: you don't have to be interesting. You have to be there and answer honestly. The rest follows.

One last thing, and it's an uncomfortable one: don't finish building the whole relationship in your head before you send the first message.

I know this one well. You watch someone for a few weeks, you read everything, you've long since formed a picture of who this person is - and then you write, and the answer doesn't match the person you assembled. And then you're disappointed by someone you haven't even met.

That isn't a personal defect, it's how the medium is built. It's actually well documented: too little information plus plenty of time reliably produces an idealised picture of the other person, and the same idealisation that makes online relationships so intense also makes them fragile. You fall for a gap you filled in yourself.

The person in your head is your invention. The one behind them is more interesting, but you have to meet them first.

And if no reply comes: that's not a verdict on you. People have children, shift work, depression, three hundred unread messages and sometimes just a bad month. You don't know the reason and you won't find out. Don't take it personally, it's almost never meant personally. 🙂

And If You're the One Pushing People Away

There's the other side of this, and it's less comfortable, because it's harder to spot in yourself.

Someone writes you something warm, something open, someone makes exactly the move this chapter has been asking for - and you answer in one syllable. Or not at all. Or you do answer, but with that slight coolness, that tiny distance, which lands on the other end like a door closing two centimetres.

And that isn't what you wanted. You wanted to meet someone. You wanted someone to be there again.

That's not arrogance. It's a protective reflex, and it's older and faster than any intention. The arithmetic runs roughly: if I don't let anyone close, nobody can leave me. If I don't hope, I won't be disappointed. If I go first, at least the other person doesn't.

The logic is clean and the result is entirely wrong. It produces precisely the loneliness it was supposed to prevent - just on a schedule and self-administered. You trade the risk of being left for the certainty of staying alone, and then you call it caution.

The forms are varied and all well camouflaged. Answering late on purpose so as not to seem too keen. Sarcasm as armour, always a shade too sharp. Small tests of whether the other person sticks around, which nobody recognises as tests because they look like a bad mood. Talking down your own interest before someone else does. Or the opposite: driving hard into a wall once, to see whether anyone stays.

And the vicious part is that it works. Interested people can reliably be driven off if you deflect them long enough. Afterwards there's your evidence: see, they always leave in the end. It's just evidence you forged yourself.

Anyone carrying a decent load of abandonment anxiety probably recognises the pattern, and if rejection sensitivity is in the mix the reflex runs faster than the thought. There's more to say about that than fits here.

What fits here is the way out, and it's smaller than you'd think.

You don't have to get rid of the fear. Honestly, you're not managing that in the next two weeks, and trying usually makes it worse. You only have to say it out loud.

One sentence will do: "I'm hard to reach right now, and it isn't you." Or: "I notice I shut down when someone gets close too fast. I'm working on it, please be patient with me."

That turns armour into information. And that's the decisive difference: a cool reply with no explanation reads as disinterest, and the other person draws the only sensible conclusion and leaves. A cool reply with an explanation reads as a human being having a hard time. People stay for that.

And because the reflex is faster than you are: it isn't about preventing it. It's about noticing afterwards and coming back. "Sorry, that came out wrong, I had a moment there" two hours later is not a defeat. That's the actual skill. Nobody reacts well every time. The people it works out for aren't the ones without reflexes - they're the ones who come back.

The First Few Rounds, and How to Get Out of Them

Let's say it worked. They answered, you answered, something is running.

What's running is small talk, and small talk is entirely fine. It's the handshake of conversation: brief, friendly, there to establish whether you'd like to keep talking. You can't skip it and you shouldn't try.

What you shouldn't do is move in.

That's where most contacts die, and they die of politeness. Two people exchange friendly nothings for weeks. Both find it pleasant. Both would like more. Neither dares, because everything is so nice and you don't want to break it. Six weeks later you have a chat that reads like a customer service transcript and a vague feeling that this ought to be going somewhere.

Getting out takes two moves, and both are unspectacular.

One: ask about the day, not the work.

Everything a person posts publicly is the presentable version. The pictures, the writing, the projects - that's the shop window. It gets interesting exactly one level below, and that level is mostly boring, which is the point.

Not "what are you working on" but "how was your day". Not "how did you do that" but "did you actually get to eat today". There's almost nothing more intimate than being asked about the banal. Someone who asks about your work is interested in what you produce. Someone who asks about your Tuesday is interested in you.

Two: go first.

That's the more important one. Questions on their own produce an interrogation, not closeness. If you want to know how their day was, tell them how yours was first. Honestly, including the part where you got annoyed at the machine that took your money and produced no coffee.

Someone who asks about your work is interested in what you produce. Someone who asks about your Tuesday is interested in you.

Closeness happens in steps, and in each step one person moves forward a little and waits to see whether the other follows. If they follow, it was the right step. If not, you wait a round and try again later. Anyone who only ever waits for the other person's step ends up standing around very politely for a very long time.

The same goes for pace. Some people answer in seconds, some in hours, and neither says anything about interest. If you start timing their replies and adjusting yours so as not to seem needy, you're no longer building a relationship, you're doing signal processing. Write when you have something to say. That's the whole rule.

From "I Like Your Stuff" to an Actual Conversation

At some point there's a transition, and you usually only notice it afterwards.

It looks like this: someone tells you something they didn't have to tell you. Nothing dramatic - that they were afraid of the cellar as a child. That they didn't get the promotion and it's still gnawing. That they sometimes wonder whether this is all there is.

And then one of two things happens. Either something comes back - not necessarily a solution, often just an "oh, I know that one" with a story of their own attached - and now you're a floor lower and you talk differently from here on. Or you get a friendly, helpless "oh no, I'm sorry", the subject changes, and you stay where you were.

Both are results. The second one is information too, just less pleasant.

What characterises these moments is that they're almost always small. You wait for the big revelation, the significant occasion, the right time for serious conversations. There isn't one. There's only an ordinary Wednesday on which someone says more than they had to, and the other person decides what happens with it.

The second accelerator is simpler still: make something together.

Posting next to each other isn't a relationship, it's coexistence. But even one small shared thing changes everything: they write a text, you make a picture for it. You build a character together. You watch the same film simultaneously and comment in the chat, including the bit where you both cringe.

And this isn't just pleasant, it's demonstrably effective. Relationship research calls it self-expansion, and the finding is fairly robust: couples who do new and exciting things together experience the relationship as better - and noticeably more so than couples who only do pleasant things together. Pleasant isn't enough. It has to be new. Building something together beats watching something together, and that isn't my opinion, that's a measurement. 😄

As a side effect you produce something online otherwise lacks: shared history. Something you can point at in four months. Offline you have places you've been together. Online you only have the things you've made together, which is a good reason to make quite a lot of them.

And the Moment It's Obvious There's More

It arrives earlier than either of you will admit.

You notice it in small things. That you spend the day collecting things to tell them. That your phone buzzes differently when it's them, which it technically doesn't. That you catch yourself grinning and are glad nobody can see.

And then you circle each other. Both of you know. Neither says anything. Both wait for the other to start, and we're right back where this chapter began, only with higher stakes.

Nothing helps here except the uncomfortable option: say it.

Not as a proposal, not as a condition, not with an expectation attached. Just as information about the current state of things. "I've noticed I look forward to your messages more than I probably should, and I wanted you to know."

That's a sentence that can go either way. But the third possibility - that you both wait for months until it drains away because nobody opened their mouth - isn't better. It's just slower.

And honestly: I've said that sentence too rarely and lived the third version too often. It isn't painless. It's just pain spread thin.

So, concretely, for today: there's someone whose stuff you've been looking at for weeks. You've wondered what they think, what they'd think of you, whether it would come across as odd.

Write them three sentences. One observation only you could have made, and something small of your own attached.

Worst case, nothing happens. And nothing is happening right now anyway.
