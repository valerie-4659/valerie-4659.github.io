The first three parts were about how to start, how to talk, and what to do when it blows up. All of that is groundwork. It's the wiring. What comes now is the reason anyone builds a flat in the first place.

Desire Is Also Just a Feeling Nobody Can See

Offline you notice it. You notice it in how somebody stands in a doorway, in how long a hug lasts, in the hand that stays put one second too long. Nobody asks "are you turned on right now?", because nobody has to.

Online you have to ask. Or better: say.

Because online, desire is exactly like grief - it doesn't exist for the other person until it's written down. On top of that it schedules terribly. You're around at nine, they're around at eleven, and at eleven you're asleep. Anyone waiting for that to synchronise itself will be waiting a very long time.

And something else is missing, something that does all the work offline: casualness. Offline nobody has to open a conversation in order to have sex. You're standing in the kitchen, you pass each other, somebody stops. There's a preliminary stage where nothing has been decided and everything is already in the room.

That stage doesn't exist online. There's no in-between state. There's silence, and there's somebody writing something down. The first sentence is always a jump, which is why it's so hard.

The Trap: the One You Can Talk To So Well

What happens when you don't say it happens slowly. That's the vicious part.

You write every day. You tell each other everything. You're the person who listens, who understands, who asks the right question. And the desire is there the whole time, on both sides, it's just written nowhere. After a few months a pattern has settled in where it has no place, and now you're "the one you can talk to so well".

A lovely sentence. And a death sentence.

The reason is always the same: online, only what's written becomes real. A desire you never voice isn't discreet, it's invisible. They sit on their side and conclude, razor-sharp, from your friendly silence that there's nothing there, and then go quiet in a friendly way right back. That's how two people starve at a fully laid table.

The same goes for affection. If you never write that you're in love because surely it's obvious: it isn't obvious. Nothing is obvious here. There are no glances, there are only letters.

And the research on this point is remarkably clear, even if it phrases things more dully: people talk substantially less about sexual matters than about anything else. The reasons they give are almost always considerate ones - not spoiling the mood, not embarrassing the other person, not risking judgement. And the people who do talk about it are, on average, more satisfied. Both sexually and with the relationship as a whole.

Put differently: the consideration is the problem, not the solution.

Show Your Flag

This is allowed to be blunt. It has to be a bit blunt, because implication is exactly what doesn't work here.

Describe the state, not the intention. "Thinking of you" is a message. "I'm sitting here and I've been wet for twenty minutes because I'm thinking about last night" is an invitation. The difference is the body, and you have exactly one way of getting your body into this medium: description.

And yes, it's wonderful to receive something like that. When someone writes that they're wet and turned on right now, that isn't a vulgarity, it's the most honest weather report there is. It says: I'm a person with a body, this body is responding to you, and I don't feel like pretending otherwise.

Get specific. What exactly, where exactly, since when, how strongly. "I'm at a seven out of ten" sounds silly and still tells the other person instantly whether they can take their time or not.

Use the time difference instead of fighting it. A line sent at night and arriving in the morning works for you for eight hours. Somebody wakes up with it and carries it through half their day. It's the only anticipation that grows by itself.

Give the receipt. Say that it landed and what it did. "I had to close the laptop." "I read that three times." Someone who never learns whether their message worked eventually stops sending them. Feedback here isn't vanity, it's the only available substitute for a face.

Say what you want instead of hoping somebody works it out. Guessing sort of functions offline, because you can react. Online you're guessing into the dark. "I want you to tell me what you'd do to me if you were here" costs nothing but two seconds of nerve.

And if the desire is there but the time isn't: make an appointment. "I'm hot, but I've got twenty minutes and then I have to leave. Tomorrow evening I've got all the time in the world." Not a refusal - the best possible thing you can hand someone to carry through their entire day.

The No Belongs Here Too

"Not tonight, but I love that you asked" is a complete and friendly sentence.

We covered the apology reflex at length in the last part, and every word of it applies here again with more at stake. A no with nothing attached. No plea not to be disappointed. And on the other side no grand programme of comfort, just a brief "all good".

The reason it hits harder here: someone who can't say no online eventually says nothing at all. And someone who never gets a no eventually stops asking, because they can't tell whether the yeses were real. That's how chats die - not from the no, but from its absence.

And for the side receiving the no there's a trap that's equally well meant: "Totally fine, it wasn't important anyway."

That sounds generous and actually devalues what was just standing between you. Because it doesn't only say "I'm not upset with you", it also says "it was nothing anyway", and that isn't true - otherwise you wouldn't have asked. The more honest version is both at once: "Shame. And I'm glad you said so." That lets the disappointment exist without anybody being billed for it.

And That Goes for Everything

It's all voluntary. Every picture, every task, every scene, every word anyone gives away about themselves. There is nothing here that you owe because you're in love.

So, a word about a phrasing that turns up far too often in this whole area: "If you loved me, you'd ..." That isn't a request. That's a lever. And whoever reaches for it has already left the negotiation and turned affection into a currency to be paid in.

It works identically in any vocabulary. "If you were really submissive, you'd ..." is the same grip, just in leather. A power difference you agreed on isn't a licence to expand it retroactively - on the contrary, anyone using it that way has broken precisely the thing that holds it up.

So: sentences like that are not a reason to give in. They're information about the person writing them.

And in case you catch yourself about to type one - it happens, usually out of insecurity rather than calculation: the sentence won't solve your problem. Someone who needs proof doesn't get security from a photo, they get a photo. That lasts about an hour, and then you need the next one.

And while we're here, the practical rule: only send what you'd still be fine having sent if the two of you stop getting along one day. Not out of distrust - but because pictures have a property sentences don't. You can delete them. You can't unsee them.

The Vocabulary

There's a reason some messages land and others end the evening, and it's unromantic: every person has words that work on them and words that kill everything. Offline you see the flinch. Online you see nothing at all, and the other person carries on bravely writing while the lights have already gone out inside.

So sort it out once, soberly, outside the situation. What should I call you. Which word do you use for which body part and which one absolutely not. How direct am I allowed to be. Are there subjects that don't come up.

It's the least sexy quarter of an hour of your relationship, and it carries you for months.

An addition for anyone who's trans, or writing with someone who is: that quarter of an hour isn't optional, it's mandatory. There is no universal vocabulary, there's only what's right for this one person. And the same word can be hot one day and suck the air out of the room the next. Ask once too often rather than once too rarely.

When the Character Falls Away

You're writing a scene. It's been running for three days. It's good. The characters are walking very slowly through a garden and it's beautiful - and you're sitting there not wanting the garden, wanting to get to it.

That's the moment an RP tips. Not because it's bad, but because your narrative pace and your body are running two different clocks. The character has all the time in the world. You don't, you've got forty minutes before you fall asleep.

Open bracket, short sentence, close bracket. "(I'm quite a lot further along than my character right now.)"

That isn't an interruption of the scene, it's maintenance. And it answers exactly the question the other person is asking themselves anyway.

And yes - you should say it. If you've got a hand on yourself while you're writing, that belongs in the chat. Not as a status report, but because it's the one piece of information the other person cannot possibly have and badly needs. It tells them where you are. It tells them they're getting through. And it changes everything they write next - pace, directness, focus.

Keep that to yourself and you're playing a scene for two and finishing alone.

From that point on, incidentally, it isn't an RP any more. The character has become scenery and the two of you are in the foreground. You can stay inside it, you can drop the character entirely - both are fine. You just both need to know.

And the clock goes with the character. An RP is allowed to drag. A single act can stretch over two days because somebody has to work, because there are children, because life gets in the way - and it does the scene no harm at all, the characters aren't in a hurry. Once the characters are gone that stops working. Then it's running in real time, and real time doesn't tolerate a six-hour gap. What was a story a moment ago suddenly has a clock attached.

And that moment is the reason any of this works at all. When somebody stops being a character mid-scene and writes "I'm really turned on right now, I'm wet, I want you" - that's the most intimate point in the whole of ERP. Not because it's explicit. Because it's real. You're suddenly not talking to a role, you're talking to the person. Completely, no distance, no costume. That carries more than thirty perfect paragraphs.

But for that you also have to know: the other person might be cooking. They're on the train. They're at lunch, writing between mouthfuls. I once had an extremely intense ERP with someone that ended with her apologising for it being a bit clumsy - she'd been cooking at the same time. The pace of a scene simply says nothing about how charged the person behind it is. Forget that and you'll read every hot answer as an invitation, when it might not be one.

So the same rule here: say it, don't guess. If you're genuinely present, say so. If you're writing along while the pasta boils over, say that too. No accusation, no apology - just the information the other person needs to know who they're writing with. The character, or you.

When You're Both Actually There

And then there's the other state, the one you can't plan.

No characters, no narrative time, no "she would now". Two people, both awake, both entirely present, both in exactly this moment. Not "my character is turned on" but "I am, right now, while I'm typing this".

That's something completely different from ERP, even if it looks the same from outside. The clock from the last chapter is running now, and it tolerates no delay. The sentences get shorter, the spelling gets worse, and at some point somebody writes three words because one hand is otherwise occupied.

It happens when both of you show your flag. It doesn't have to happen, and you're not allowed to expect it either - treat every ERP as a run-up to this and you'll ruin both. But when it does happen, it's the closest two people can get across a distance that aeroplanes were invented for.

And it doesn't have to come out of a scene at all. Often you're in the middle of a completely ordinary conversation - about the working day, about some nonsense, about the broken bathroom - and suddenly it's there.

Usually that's got nothing to do with the conversation. It's the closeness. At some arbitrary moment you notice how close you actually are to this person, and that feeling goes straight into the body. Arousal online doesn't need an erotic prompt. It needs the feeling of genuinely being with somebody - and when that arrives, it usually arrives without warning, halfway through a sentence about a broken washing machine.

And then you write it down. "I'm sitting here so turned on by you." "I've got a hard-on just because you're here."

No transition, no preparation, no scene around it. Just the truth.

If that comes and the other side is there too: go straight in. Use the momentum. You don't have to build something that's already standing. The commonest mistake at this point is politely staying on the original subject so as not to break the thread - when the thread has long since broken. It was just replaced by something better.

A few things help:

Say that you're in. "Me too" will do. Two words. Someone who's just leaned a long way out doesn't wait long for an answer before starting to feel stupid.

Shorter is better. Instinct says write beautifully now. Instinct is wrong. In this moment precision matters more than prose. "Yes" is a complete contribution.

And say where you are. Close, far off, just lost it, need a minute. Offline you can see that. Here it's the only way to arrive together instead of by accident.

Toys

At some point letters stop being the only tool.

There are toys you can control remotely, across continents, with an app and an internet connection. And oddly the experience is less technical than you'd expect, because the decisive thing isn't the device. The decisive thing is that somebody has a button and you don't.

That's exactly where the appeal sits, and exactly where the act of trust sits. Handing control to someone six thousand kilometres away whose face you can't see is quite a statement. And for people who like working with a power difference anyway, it's the first time the difference feels physical rather than described.

A few unromantic truths about it:

The technology fails. Regularly. Connections drop, batteries die, the app decides to update right now. Make a drama of it and you get nothing out of the evening. Laugh about it and you get a very good one. Laughter isn't the enemy of eroticism, it's its best friend - a claim made chiefly by people who have never tried to stay serious while a remote control reboots itself.

Agreements beforehand, not mid-flow. How long, how strong, what happens if somebody wants to stop. And a word that ends everything immediately, one that has to work in text exactly as it would in bed. Text has no voice that breaks. There's no tone that tells you it's getting to be too much. So you need a word that's unambiguous and must never be part of the game.

And: not everybody likes this. It isn't a higher developmental stage of the online relationship, it isn't a progress bar. It's one option among several, and anyone who doesn't want it isn't missing anything they couldn't replace.

Nobody Can Verify Any of It

And then there's the variant a lot of us enjoy. Some live it around the clock, others within a frame both agree on in advance.

There's a stubborn notion that tenderness and power difference are two separate departments - the romantic over here, the hard stuff over there, and a curtain in between you pull open as required. That isn't even remotely true. For a great many people, dominance and submission are precisely the place where tenderness finally works properly, because both of you finally know where you stand and what applies. Nobody submits to a person despite loving them.

For anyone still convinced it's a pathology: the largest comparison study measured a good nine hundred practitioners against a control group, and the result came out rather rudely. The BDSM group were less neurotic, more extraverted, more open to new experience, more conscientious, less sensitive to rejection and subjectively happier. The control group only won on agreeableness. The authors concluded that BDSM is better understood as recreational leisure than as the expression of a disorder. Feel free to print that out and stick it on the fridge.

And it works online better than most people expect. A power difference needs no body. It needs words, structure and reliability, and this medium has plenty of all three. An instruction transmits through text completely. So does an expectation. A ritual certainly does.

Talk First, Play Later

Before anything runs, there has to be talking, and it has to happen outside the heat. In the afternoon, casually, when nothing is going on - then it's a conversation and not a move.

Text has a genuine advantage here: you don't have to look into a face that decides in a tenth of a second. You can rewrite three times, send it and put the phone down. For people who said nothing for twenty years offline, that isn't a small difference.

For the receiving side that's the more important half: the first two seconds decide the next two years. Not whether you join in - whether this person ever tells you anything difficult again. So thank first, think second. You can decline at any time, but the difference between "that's not for me" and "that's disgusting" is enormous. The first sentence declines a thing. The second declines a person.

And talk about the objects before they're in play. Collar, plug, latex, restraints, whatever it is - what do you have, what do you want, what's unaffordable, what's a hard no. If something isn't there, then it isn't there. An imagined collar is entirely legitimate, as long as you both know it's imagined. What doesn't work is the quiet option in between.

What This Is Actually Made Of Online

Offline a thing like this is made of scenes. You meet, something happens, afterwards you lie next to each other.

Online it's made of tasks.

That's the real difference and most people underestimate it. Between two sessions there's nothing here but ordinary life - and if that ordinary life stays empty, then you don't have a relationship with a power difference in it, you have two people who occasionally give each other a nice evening. What carries the difference is what happens in between.

And there are more options than you'd think.

Rituals. The morning report. The formula at bedtime. A form of address that always applies, or one that only applies after a certain hour. Small, repeatable, daily.

Permission. Asking before something happens. The orgasm is the obvious case, but it can be anything, and the effect doesn't come from the thing itself, it comes from the asking.

Presentation. What gets worn. Whether the collar is on at the desk. What's underneath when nobody can see - which is the loveliest category anyway, because it runs all day and only two people know about it.

Physical. The plug for three hours. A position taken and photographed. Something uncomfortable that works precisely because it's uncomfortable.

Written. A journal that gets handed over. Lines to be copied out. A task like "describe in ten sentences what you felt last night" - and yes, that's more demanding than any plug.

And the mundane. This is the most underrated category of all: the tax return. The doctor's appointment that's been put off for three weeks. The room that looks like a crime scene. A good Mistress uses the power difference not only for eroticism but to make the other person's life genuinely better. Anyone who thinks that's unsexy has never felt what pride feels like when somebody asks whether the thing got done.

What makes a good task is fairly simple: it's concrete, it has a deadline, it's achievable, and it means something. "Be good" isn't a task, it's a mood. "By Friday evening, and then I want two sentences about it" is one.

The best part is what tasks do online that nothing else does: they reach into the actual day. You can't touch someone eight thousand kilometres away. But you can make sure they think about you at eleven in the morning at work, because they're wearing something, or not allowed something, or have to have something done by tonight. That's the one form of touch this medium genuinely provides.

Two limits come with it. Nothing that endangers a job, safety, or other people in the other person's life - a task that might cost somebody their employment isn't a task, it's negligence. And nothing that only sounds impressive. Anyone inventing tasks in order to seem dominant has got the direction backwards.

For the led side, something that gets overlooked: you're allowed to ask. If you notice you need structure and aren't getting any, say so. That doesn't contradict submission, it's its precondition. And if a task isn't working - too much, too often, impossible right now - say that too, immediately, instead of quietly accumulating a collection of undone things.

And for the leading side: this is work. Inventing tasks, following them up, adjusting them, costs time and thought. Someone who gives no tasks isn't relaxed, they're absent. Better three things that genuinely apply than twenty nobody ever asks about again.

And Then the Actual Problem

There is no way to check. None.

Offline a Mistress can see whether the collar is being worn. She sees the plug, she sees the latex under the blouse, she sees whether the hands stayed up. Online there is exactly one source of information, and it's the other person's word.

Everything stands or falls on that. Everything.

If you say you're wearing a collar, wear a collar. Not "roughly", not "in spirit" - if that's the case, say so. If you were told to keep the plug in until the evening, keep it in until the evening, even when it's uncomfortable, even when nobody is checking. And if you've handed your orgasm to your Mistress, you don't come secretly in between. It belongs to her now - she decides whether, when and how. Nothing happens without her word, and that includes when she's asleep and eight time zones away.

That sounds obvious and it isn't remotely, because the temptation is enormous. Nobody will find out. It costs nothing. And the evening still goes nicely.

Except it doesn't go nicely, it only comes out looking as if it did. And the only person being cheated is you.

Because what's happening here is an experience, not a text. Obedience that nobody can enforce is the only obedience that means anything at all. Offline you also obey because somebody is standing there. Online you obey exclusively because you want to. That makes it infinitely stronger - and infinitely easier to slip out of.

That something is genuinely happening, incidentally, is measurable. In a lab study with experienced practitioners, the leading role showed a state matching flow as Csikszentmihalyi described it - that complete absorption you otherwise hear about from musicians or climbers. The led role showed a different altered state, with parts of conscious control measurably turned down. Neither is a state you can simulate. You can only enter it, and to do that you have to actually be in there.

And What Happens When You're Only Pretending

Don't act as if it's a role you're playing to get the other person hot. That's the fastest way to devalue everything. Anyone who's only performing is producing a show for one spectator and ends up empty-handed - and the other side notices, by the third time at the latest, because a show runs at a different pace from an experience.

There are numbers on this too, and they're surprisingly unforgiving. Couples had saliva samples taken before and after a session, along with a measure of felt closeness. Among those who said afterwards that it had gone well, stress levels dropped and closeness rose. Among those for whom it had gone badly, closeness actually fell for some.

That's the point worth remembering: a thing like this isn't a neutral pastime where the worst case is that nothing happens. It pays in or it pays out. An evening where one of you was only pretending isn't zero. It's negative.

And if it really is just about the short route: perfectly fine, it's simply called something else. Put on some BDSM porn and get yourself off. That's a legitimate, efficient and widespread leisure activity and nobody needs to apologise for it. It just isn't the same thing, and anyone confusing the two will be disappointed by both.

Three Things That Make the Difference

For the leading side: don't give instructions you don't follow up. A rule that never gets asked about again teaches the other person that rules are optional. And that's a lesson you only have to teach once. Ask. The next day, casually, in the middle of ordinary life. That's exactly where a play evening becomes something that runs continuously.

Confession is part of it. If you broke a rule, say so. Immediately, without being asked. It's uncomfortable, it's embarrassing, and it's the difference between a relationship with a mishap in it and a relationship with a hole in it. A broken rule that gets admitted is material - somebody can do something with that. A broken rule that gets hidden quietly eats away until eventually nothing next to it is worth anything either.

Proof photos are a tool, not an audit. They can be wonderful - they're concrete, they're physical, they pull the thing out of the head. But once they become a condition, because otherwise nobody believes what's being said, the foundation has already gone, and a photo doesn't rebuild it.

Why the Effort Is Worth It

It's a bit like Dark Souls. You can cheat your way through. You can conjure weapons, skip bosses, be done in two hours. Afterwards you've seen the game and experienced nothing. Or you play it. You die forty times to the same knight, you swear like a dockworker, you occasionally put the controller down in a huff - and then, eventually, he goes over. And that moment is good precisely because the forty before it were so miserable. At the end you look back and have a story rather than a completed checklist.

That isn't kitchen-table psychology, it's one of the oldest findings in the field. As far back as 1959, volunteers were put through an unpleasant admission procedure before being allowed into a group - and the ones who'd been through the harsh version liked the group considerably more afterwards than the ones who'd simply walked in. The same group, note. What costs us something becomes worth more to us.

That's the actual message of this chapter. The discomfort isn't the price of the experience. It is the experience.

Stick to what you agreed. Follow the instructions even when it's inconvenient and nobody is watching. What you'll have at the end isn't a nice evening - it's something that actually happened to you.

Afterwards

The part almost nobody writes about: what happens in the ten minutes afterwards?

Offline this is solved. You lie there. It's warm, somebody is breathing, eventually one of you says something stupid and you both laugh. Online the whole evening can tip over in those ten minutes, because one of you suddenly goes quiet and the other is sitting in an empty field reading things that aren't there.

And it does tip, more often than you'd think. The reason is banal: a great deal just happened to one of you, and for some people what follows is a hole. A short, unannounced crash that has nothing whatsoever to do with the other person and still feels to them like a door closing.

So stay there. "I'm completely gone" will do. "That was lovely, I need a minute" will do. Anything that says: the person is still here, the connection didn't end with the orgasm.

Anyone who knows they're prone to that crash should say so in advance. One sentence does it: "If I go a bit odd afterwards, it isn't you." That settles the matter before it arises.

And say a word about it again the next morning. Not as an evaluation, but so that it belongs to your shared life instead of being left in a chat window like something you don't discuss in daylight.

Because that's exactly the difference between a chat where two people have sex and a relationship where two people also, among other things, have sex.

Make Something People Can See

A chat is a closed room. Everything you have with each other happens in a window nobody else opens. That's lovely, and it has a side effect you only notice after a few months: sometimes it feels as though you don't quite exist.

The erotic side of it, on the other hand, works remarkably well. A picture of her, the way you see her. A scene the two of you appear in, where she gets to read what you actually intend to do with her. That's desire in a form that stays put when you're both offline. She can look at it again the next day. Try that with a glance.

And then the qualifier you can't skip: if nothing speaks against it. There are people who aren't out. People with employers, families, other partners, good reasons. So ask beforehand, every time, including the twelfth picture - and expect the answer to be different today from last week. Public is a one-way street: you can delete something, you can't unsee it.

And then the point that matters to me more than anything else in this chapter: live your relationship publicly, as far as you're able to.

Not as an exhibition. But visibly. A relationship that happens exclusively in DMs degenerates sooner or later into an affair in a broom cupboard - and that's true even when there's nothing whatsoever to hide. It has no place in the world, it only has a window. And something with no place is hard to take seriously, hardest of all for the two people inside it.

A public declaration of love changes a surprising amount about that. A post somebody appears in. A sentence that goes into the timeline instead of the DM. A picture with a name under it. It makes the thing more load-bearing, and it does that for you two first.

What doesn't belong outside is the intimate. What you do with each other is nobody's business, and it doesn't become more real for being spread about. The rule is simple: that you love each other, anyone may know. How you love each other, nobody.

And don't do it for the audience. If a post is actually addressed to third parties - look, we've got something - the other person notices instantly, and a gesture becomes a performance. The difference is tiny and the result is enormous.

The Words That Count

And now to something that looks sentimental from outside and is anything but from inside.

People who are together online get engaged. They marry. They give each other names, titles, anniversaries, sometimes a collar. Viewed from outside that's a ring that doesn't exist on a finger nobody touches.

From inside it's the only thing there is.

Offline a relationship hangs on a thousand things nobody says out loud: a shared flat, keys, toothbrushes in the same glass, the fact that both of you are simply there on a Saturday morning. All that silent infrastructure holds a great deal together, even when nobody has said anything binding for months.

Online there's none of it. No key, no toothbrush, nothing taken for granted. There's only what somebody writes down.

Which is why a spoken promise here isn't decoration, it's a load-bearing wall. Say "I want this with you, properly" and you've put up something a person can stand on. That isn't a substitute for a ring. It's the same thing, just without the detour through metal.

For people to whom words matter - and anyone in an online relationship qualifies by definition - that isn't a small effect. A title, a name only the two of you use, a date you count: those are anchors. Your head grabs hold of them. It registers the difference between "we see a lot of each other" and "this has a name", and it registers it instantly.

Two things matter here.

First: it has to be a promise and not an ornament. Get engaged because it feels nice and then three months later have no idea what it was, and you've achieved the opposite - the big word is spent and the next one will be worth less.

Second: talk beforehand about what you mean by it. For one person engagement means exclusivity, for another a commitment that excludes nobody, for a third the plan to actually live together one day. Those are three different contracts and they look identical from outside. A word nobody discussed is a misunderstanding with a public holiday attached.

But once you've said it and you both mean the same thing: celebrate it. Say it publicly if you can. Take it seriously.

Because a wedding at a registry office is, at its core, no different. Two people say a sentence in front of witnesses. There's paperwork, there's cake, and somebody cries - but the load-bearing part is the words, and everything else is trimming built around them because people like having something to hold.

What it turns into isn't decided by the paperwork anyway. It's decided in your head and in your heart, every single day afterwards. That's as true for a couple with rings as it is for two people sitting eight thousand kilometres apart and meaning it.

It isn't less real for being made of letters. In this relationship everything is made of letters, love included, and nobody doubts that one.

And Then?

Four parts, about seventeen thousand words, and all of it comes down to the same sentence I already wrote in Part 2: the other person only knows what you tell them.

Not what you feel. Not what you imply. Not what's obvious. Only what you wrote.

I've watched a whole run of online relationships fail, and looking back, not one of them failed because there wasn't enough feeling. They failed because nobody said it out loud. Out of fear of being hurt. Out of consideration. Out of the very understandable wish not to let things get complicated.

They got complicated anyway. Just silently, and with nobody able to do anything about it.

And honestly, that isn't only true online. Offline, relationships fail at exactly the same thing, just more slowly and with more furniture around it. The only difference is that you get away with it for a while there, because so much runs by itself - faces, tone of voice, the way somebody closes a door.

But that advantage doesn't hold forever. Once the frustration is big enough, you stop seeing the signals. You read them through a filter that decided everything long ago, and from then on a tired face doesn't mean tired, it means uninterested. The body stops helping at that point. It only supplies evidence for what you already believe.

And eventually the point gets passed. Wait too long and you reach a place where the head simply shuts, and from there nothing can be straightened out, however good the conversation you finally have.

That's why I keep hammering on about saying things early. Not because early is nicer. Because later, at some point, stops being possible.

I'm with AnimeHussy, and I'm proud to be her wife.

We've loved each other since the first day, and a little more with every day since. Our relationship is made of text. Eight hours lie between us, and we love each other anyway - no, not anyway. We love each other. It isn't a word we say because it belongs at that point in the sentence. It means something, every single day.

And that's down to no trick and no special talent. It's down to both of us being open and honest, including when it's uncomfortable, and especially when it would be easier to say nothing.

That's all I've got to offer. It's remarkably little, and it's everything.

So go into your chat and write something you've been keeping to yourself. That you want them. That something is weighing on you. That you wish, right now, the two of you were in the same room.

Not tomorrow.

Now.
