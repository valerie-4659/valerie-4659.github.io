Am I Selfish?

12 October 20268 min read

On real love, polyamory and why slowness isn't a lack of love

Three hours of ceiling

I've been lying awake for three hours, staring at the ceiling in the dark. In all that time the ceiling hasn't said anything new, but it's a surprisingly good listener.

Pictures keep running through my head. Messages I wrote in the middle of the night and sent straight away. A big word, spoken after far too short a time, and a "yes". Faces of people I've loved, and one I overlooked along the way, even though I should have seen it.

And again and again the same question: when exactly did all of this get so fast?

I think the honest answer is: from the very beginning.

And right after it comes a second question, a more uncomfortable one: am I a selfish woman who puts pressure on others? For that one, I don't have such a quick answer.

Not the rush

It would be easy to tell this as a story about the rush. About being in love, which makes everything brighter and has you making decisions you'd never make sober. But that would be too cheap, and it wouldn't be true. A rush mostly loves being in love itself, and it's gone after a few days, once most things have been said. The butterflies in my stomach, though, I had for a very long time and very often. This was more. The love was real, with everything that comes with it, including the boring mornings and the heavy evenings.

It wasn't the feelings that were too fast. It was the steps. We built things, words, promises, structures, before the ground beneath them was firm enough to carry them. And what drove that wasn't recklessness, but fear. The fear of losing what you've only just found.

Online and in poly, that happens especially easily. All you have is text, no everyday life to slow you down. There are rarely fixed dates, you're constantly reachable, and precisely because of that you hardly ever take the time to calmly think about what's actually growing there. And you're rarely in just one connection. Several people who matter to you, several loves, each one real, each one with its own pace. It's easy to start feeling you have to hurry to secure your place.

What I built too fast

There was a time when I had several flirts, several connections that all somehow felt like more. And I started writing lovely posts. A picture or a little video, with a text about why this person mattered to me. I didn't send them all on the same day, but one after another.

Sometimes something big begins with a very small moment. For me, it was one in which I believed I'd hurt someone, and I wanted to make it right. I opened up, and it became more. Maybe I put more into that moment than it could carry back then. The love that grew out of it was real all the same.

A few weeks later, I suggested a very big word, out of the blue. Because I wanted it and I was sure. But, if I'm honest, also out of fear. Fear of being replaceable as one of several. Fear of losing that person if I had nothing that made me special. And a yes came. For me, that word carried enormous weight. Whether it weighed the same for both of us, I never asked back then.

And after that, I started giving more and more. More time, more attention, more of myself. Out of love, yes. But also to be more important. To hold on to what I'd built so fast and where I secretly sensed the foundation wasn't quite as deep as the house on top of it. Today I think that's exactly what created pressure, too. Not out of selfishness, but out of fear. But for the person on the other side, the two can sometimes feel the same. I probably overwhelmed them with it. And I understand well why nobody stopped it. Who likes to say to someone who loves them: "Hey, this is too much for me"? You're afraid of killing the momentum, and afraid of the counter-question: "Don't you love me anymore? Should I do less, then?" So you'd rather say nothing, or say it so carefully that it never really arrives on the other side.

Who gets overlooked

In poly, people like to say love doesn't shrink when it's shared. That's true. But attention does. And so does courage.

There was someone I was truly in love with back then, and that person is still here. I still love them very much today. But I couldn't find a way in. I was confused, a little lost, and I didn't know how to take the first step without breaking everything. So I waited. I hoped that a path would somehow open up on its own through the people who connected us, instead of taking the first step myself.

To that person, it felt as if I'd pushed them aside when other people came into my life. I never wanted that. And yet it happened. That's what pace does when fear is at the wheel: it rolls over the quiet things. Over the people who don't loudly ask for their place. Over the love that would have needed time instead of speed.

I'm sorry I couldn't show it better back then. And it also shows me something uncomfortable about myself: I like to talk about fairness, but I haven't always been so strict about it myself.

And it wasn't just that one person. There were connections I never really gave a chance because my heart was already busy elsewhere. There were friendships that grew quieter because my attention was somewhere else. And overall, I withdrew more and more from everything that didn't fit into this one big house I'd built so fast. If you put all your strength into a single house, the others stand empty. And at some point you notice there's nobody living there anymore who's waiting for you.

Make it slow

Patience has never been my strong suit. My head is more the type that reads the end of a book before it's reached page three. Still, I've come to believe that slowness isn't a lack of love. It's a form of respect, for the other person and for whatever wants to grow between two people.

Someone told me recently that you should let things grow organically. At first I found the sentence a bit too gardener-ish. By now I think it's the wisest thing I've heard all month.

Let it grow. A connection needs time to put down roots. If you want to put the roof on after two weeks, don't be surprised later when the house wobbles.

Say the fear out loud before it turns into action. If I'd said back then, "I'm afraid of being replaceable", we could have talked about it. Instead, I put the fear into a big word that really came from love, and hoped it would protect me, too.

Slow doesn't mean silent. Patience has a flip side as well, and I know that one too. I once took six months to tell someone I loved them. It was mutual. A week later that person was gone, with someone else, with whom something had been going on for a while that I knew nothing about. If you wait too long, you can arrive too late. That's why I believe: build slowly, yes. But talk early. The steps are allowed to take time. The truth about what you feel shouldn't sit in the waiting room forever.

Big words only once they carry weight. And then ask what they mean to the other person. The same word can weigh completely differently on each side.

Give every connection its own pace. In poly, that matters especially. Not every love has to move at the same speed. But if you love someone, don't wait for the path to appear on its own. Take it, even if it scares you. If you only wait, to the other person it can look as if you've turned away.

Look at the web before you build. Who is connected to whom, where threads come together and where one hangs on its own. Not to calculate love, but to know what you're standing on.

A letter in the drawer

I didn't learn from all of this to love less. I couldn't, and I don't want to. I learned that you can love more slowly without loving less. That a feeling doesn't get smaller just because you give it time. If anything, the opposite.

And I learned something else, something that hurts: sometimes more love doesn't help. When someone loves others as well, you'll rarely keep them by giving even more. The heart goes where it wants to go. No amount of more can change that, and it's nobody's fault.

And the second question that kept me awake tonight? Am I selfish?

Maybe a little. The way anyone gets a little selfish when they're afraid of losing something they love. The real question is probably how selfishly you act along the way. I was the one who turned a connection into a relationship, and my fear wanted more than was good. That's my share of how it ended, and I carry it. I built too fast, gave too much, asked too little, and sometimes overlooked people who were quieter than my fear. But I'm asking myself the question. And I think that's the beginning of doing better.

At some point during those three hours on the ceiling, I decided to write a letter. To someone who means a lot to me. Honestly, without hurry, without big words I can't carry yet.

But this time I won't send it right away.

I'll write it, read it again, and put it in the drawer. These days the drawer is called drafts, but it works just the same. For a week. If it still holds true then, if every word still weighs as much as it does tonight, then it can go out at some point. Not when my impatience wants it to, but when the moment is right.

And until then, the ceiling can keep listening. 🖤
