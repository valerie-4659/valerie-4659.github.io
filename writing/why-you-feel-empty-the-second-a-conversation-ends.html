It's always the same moment.

We've talked for two hours. Someone dropped a meme in the DMs that I looked at three times, because it got better on the third. Under one of my posts, five of us drifted completely off topic and ended up arguing about which animal looks the most unpleasant. I'm sitting there with that warm, full feeling you get from two hours of simply being included.

Then somebody types "right, bed for me", with a little heart after it, and then there's nothing.

And the emptiness is already in the room. As if it had been waiting outside the whole time, checking its watch and sighing.

Not slowly. Not gradually. Instantly.

There are three versions of this, and I know all of them. The group chat with 340 unread messages, where you leave an emoji and nobody responds - a room full of people in which you're invisible. The stream you put on so that somebody is talking, which ends with "love you all, see you tomorrow", except she didn't mean you and couldn't possibly have. And the birthday party where you had real fun, and on the way home exactly the same hole opens up anyway.

The last one matters most. Because it proves this has nothing to do with being online.

Why This Happens

Before we get to strategies, something I consider more important than any tip. Because the worst part of this feeling isn't the feeling. It's the suspicion that you're the only one having it.

You are very, very not alone in this. A third of all adults between 21 and 54 report feeling at least somewhat lonely - 16 percent strongly, 18 percent moderately. Among 21 to 30 year olds, 42 percent report feelings of loneliness, and one in five in that age group feels strongly lonely. And those numbers have barely moved since 2022. This isn't a pandemic aftershock anymore. This is the normal state of things.

In every group chat where you feel invisible, there are statistically several people feeling exactly the same.

And here's what kills the cliché: a third of young people living with family feel lonely despite their social surroundings, and among those in shared flats it's 41 percent. Having people around you is clearly not insurance.

It isn't a shortage of people. The standard advice is "you should get out more". Well meant, and about as useful as "just eat something" when you're nauseous. Because loneliness is a shortage of felt connection, not of attendance. The feeling comes from the gap between the relationships you have and the ones you'd like, whether that gap is about number or about quality. The psychologist John Cacioppo spent most of his life on this, and for him the measure was never the count but the sense that someone genuinely knows you and would be there if it mattered.

That's why physical closeness doesn't automatically solve anything. Someone can be sitting right beside you and you're still alone.

The feeling has a purpose. This is the finding that matters most to me, and I wish someone had told me sooner.

Loneliness isn't a defect. It's a signal, and it works exactly like hunger. We are a deeply social species, and we need feedback when that need isn't being met. Low blood sugar makes us hungry, and hunger sends us to find food. If we ignore that signal long enough because we've decided it's embarrassing, we eventually get too depleted to go looking for food at all. Loneliness works the same way, except it doesn't sustain us with calories, it sustains us with connections.

Hunger warns of harm to the body, pain warns of tissue damage - and loneliness is the warning system for the social body.

So your head isn't broken. It's doing precisely what it was built to do, with all the tact and timing of a smoke alarm at three in the morning. What hits you when the window closes is a notification, not a verdict.

It reinforces itself. Now the uncomfortable part, which probably explains more about your own behaviour than you'd like.

The signal doesn't only push us toward other people. Under the evolutionary model, loneliness also flags that mutual aid and protection are missing, which turns up self-preservation. That pull in both directions is measurable: lonely people prefer larger interpersonal distances within intimate space.

Translated: loneliness makes you needy and wary at the same time. You want to get closer and you take a step back anyway. If you've ever wondered why you didn't send the message you badly needed to send - that isn't a character flaw, that's the signal pulling both ways.

On top of that: lonely people increase the loneliness of those they interact with, and reducing perceived negativity can break that cycle. Your own baseline mood colours the conversations you have, and they come back to you accordingly.

It's a loop. And loops can be opened in more than one place.

Online, what's missing is continuity. Online friendships aren't worth less - anyone claiming otherwise has never had someone answer them at three in the morning. 💛 The difference is something else: online, the contact stops existing the moment nobody's typing. Your colleague stays in the office even when you're not talking. Online, one click separates "is here" from "does not exist".

It isn't closeness that's missing. It's continuity. And that's good news, because continuity is a craft.

Numbers don't help. One long-term study followed nearly 7,000 people for nine years. The result: not only passive scrolling was linked to rising loneliness, but active posting and engaging too. The researchers describe a feedback loop - lonely people turn to the platforms to deal with the feeling, and the use may simply fan the flames.

Against that, here's the finding I'd hang above my desk: what goes with less loneliness isn't having many followers, it's following many friends. Your reach doesn't count. Who you read does.

For a while I tried to compensate for inner loneliness with metrics. It works, briefly: a post does well, and for one evening it feels like belonging. The next day you need another one. You can have twenty thousand followers and be as lonely as someone with one, because a number is only a number, and no number has ever once asked how your day was.

And being in love doesn't help either. Flirting works instantly and almost always - right up until the window closes, after which it's often worse than before.

The reason is unromantic. Passionate love, the thing people mean when they say they're in love, is typically strongest when a relationship is new - or doesn't exist yet. The feeling burns brightest when there's least there. Companionate love, by contrast, is experienced less intensely but combines emotional intimacy and commitment with a deep attachment to the other person. Less fireworks, more foundation.

In Sternberg's well-known triangle of intimacy, passion and commitment, romantic love is usually the one missing commitment while passion and intimacy run high - which is why it gets compared to Romeo and Juliet. And commitment is precisely the part that reaches past the closed window.

The neurology tells the same story: during the attraction phase the brain's reward centre runs up to two and a half times more active than in calm periods, a level compared to the intensity of addiction. In long-term loving couples, the oxytocin and vasopressin networks responsible for security are highly active alongside it.

Dopamine gets you high when someone writes. Oxytocin carries you when nobody does.

What to Do After the Window Closes

Not all of this fits everyone. Pick two.

Leave traces. A conversation that disappears was never there - that's how it feels, and unfortunately your head believes that arithmetic. So keep something: a line in a notes app, a picture, a screenshot of the laughing. I have a file containing nothing but things people have said to me, and on bad evenings it's worth more than any good advice. Evidence against your own head.

The first ten minutes. The emptiness doesn't turn up eventually, it turns up immediately after you close the window. If you know what you do in those ten minutes - make tea, put music on, write three lines, bother the cat - you don't fall into the hole, you fall into something. It sounds ridiculously small. It works anyway, because you're not fighting the feeling, you're fighting the empty space it spreads into.

Have something waiting for you. A book, a series, a half-written text, a jigsaw on the table. Not as distraction, but as a second appointment - one with yourself. The hole after logging off is always also a hole in the structure of the evening.

Rituals instead of chance. If contact only happens by accident, every ending is a goodbye into the unknown. So make plans. Sunday evening, half an hour, always. The smallest thing works: a "good morning" that comes every morning. It isn't the quantity that counts, it's that it comes back.

Write asynchronously. Voice notes, letters, long emails. They don't need a shared moment - nobody has to be online for something to exist. A voice note in the morning echoes into the afternoon. A chat at midnight lasts until midnight plus two minutes.

Narrow contact beats broad contact. A chat where things are constantly happening feels like company, but input isn't connection. The test: if you hadn't been there, would anyone have noticed? If not, you weren't in company, you were in the audience. One direct message to one person is worth more than three hours of group dynamics. There has to be someone who knows they were meant.

Use streams as a source of warmth, not as connection. They make the flat less silent, and there's nothing shameful about that. But you can't be missed by someone who doesn't know you exist. So always put something next to it that can write back. A stream and a person together are far more than two streams.

Make the circle smaller, not bigger. The reflex is to look for more people. Usually the opposite is right. Three people who know you beat thirty who can read your name.

What to Do Inside the Conversation

An exchange that leaves nothing behind leaves nothing behind when it's over. Most of it is decided not afterwards but in the middle.

Build in a door. Leave something open at the end: a question, an unfinished thought, a "I'll tell you that properly another time". A conversation that's completely finished is closed. One with an open door is only paused.

Announce the sequel. "I'm off to bed, but tomorrow I'll tell you about the delivery guy." Two seconds of work, and the conversation doesn't end, it pauses. Someone with a sequel sits differently in the silence.

Introduce yourself. Not by name - by you. Say what you like, what annoys you, what you did at the weekend even if it was nothing special. People can only attach to something they can see. Anyone who only reacts and never offers anything stays a friendly surface - and then wonders why nobody thinks about them.

Look for what connects you. Talk about cooking, not about the weather. Weather fills time and builds no bridge. But if you discover you both spend Sundays reducing sauces, you now have a shared subject - and that exists even when nobody's typing. Depth isn't the same as heaviness here. It isn't about the big wounds, it's about something that genuinely concerns you both.

Take the initiative. The most important point and the most uncomfortable. Anyone waiting for the other person to carry on may be waiting a very long time, because the other person is doing exactly the same. Two people both waiting don't make a conversation, they make two lonely evenings. So write first. Ask. Pick up something from last week. The worst that can happen is a late reply.

Don't let it stress you out. Communication is supposed to be fun. If writing starts to feel like a task - the reply you still owe, the read receipt, the guilt about the three days - then it's working against you. Nobody has to answer immediately. Nobody has to be everywhere. A conversation you look forward to carries you; one you process empties you out further.

What Actually Connects on Social Media

Everything here has one thing in common: it runs in both directions.

Show who you like, and why. Not just a like. Say publicly what you think is good about someone, with reasons. It costs nothing and turns a number into a person, on both sides.

Weave other people into your work. This is the one that worked most clearly for me: take other creators' characters, ideas or worlds into your own content - with their permission, obviously. The moment someone's character appears in your story, you're no longer two accounts side by side, you're two people with something in common. Connection grows out of that almost by itself.

Write together. Shared stories, joint projects, a subject you're both working on. Something shared keeps existing when nobody's online - exactly the continuity that's otherwise missing.

Break the island thinking. The reflex on platforms is to treat your account like a small kingdom to be defended against everyone else. That makes you lonely and, incidentally, makes worse content. You're part of a community, not its competitor. People who think that way stop counting and start building.

Listen to people. Honestly, attentively, without immediately parking your own thing next to theirs. The most underrated move there is, because everyone wants it and almost nobody does it. Pleasant side effect: people who listen get talked to.

Ask again a week later. "So how did the thing with your dad turn out?" Nothing says "you're present with me" as clearly as a callback.

Keep looking. This isn't a complete list and couldn't be one. Try things. Anything that leaves something shared at the end beats anything that leaves a number.

What You Can Do for Other People

Loneliness doesn't sit evenly distributed across networks. It clusters at the edges, and over time it pushes people further out. Whoever is hanging on at the edge drifts further.

So if there's someone in your circle who's always standing slightly off to one side: pull them in. But not out of pity - everyone can smell that from a mile away, and it humiliates more than it helps.

What works is concrete. Give the person a role - something that doesn't happen without them. Address them directly, not into the room. Refer back to something they said last week. And invite them before they have to ask, because having to ask is the highest hurdle there is for a lot of people.

That isn't charity. That's self-defence for everyone.

Even With Love in Your Luggage

Love is the strongest thing there is against this feeling. But it doesn't do what you expect it to.

The window still closes. The evening is still quiet. Anyone who believes the right person means no more quiet moments will hit the first one and wonder whether something's wrong.

Something is right, actually - just not what you thought. Love doesn't fill the silence, it holds what happens inside it. "Nobody is here" becomes "she isn't here right now", and that's not cosmetic, that's a different state entirely. One of them has no floor. The other one does. What's left is missing someone - and missing isn't emptiness. It has a direction, a name, a reunion at the other end.

That's why almost everything gets easier with real love. Not overnight, but noticeably. The ten minutes after logging off lose their teeth. The flat isn't empty anymore, just unused. And when a bad evening does come, you know there's someone who'll take it on with you tomorrow.

What's left is the missing. And I don't want to be rid of that. Missing someone means there's someone there.

And If Nothing Helps at All

Some evenings are just like that. Some weeks too. Then the emptiness isn't a problem waiting to be solved, it's a state you sit through, the way you sit through rain. The only thing that holds then is knowing it passes. Not today. But it passes.

If the emptiness stops arriving only after the window closes, though, and simply stays. If it's already there in the morning. If nothing on this list makes the slightest difference even though you've honestly tried - then it isn't loneliness anymore, it's something else, and there's no article and no strategy for that.

For that, there are people who do it professionally.

I know how high that hurdle feels. Asking for help feels like admitting you can't manage on your own. But it's the same as eating when you're hungry. Responding to a signal isn't failure - it's the entire point of having one.

Call a helpline. Talk to your doctor. Write to someone you trust, even if it's only one sentence.

You don't have to carry this alone. That's the whole idea.
