What grief is, and why it hurts so much

Foreword

I'm processing a lot right now. Behind me lies a breakup without blame, and that doesn't make it any easier. When someone has done something wrong, there's at least a place to put your anger down. When nobody is to blame, all you're left with is grief.

Grief happens in your head, and everyone has to go through it at some point. How long it takes is different for everyone. You can't speed it up, you can't take a shortcut, and you can't organise it away. It takes as long as it takes.

But maybe it helps to understand yourself a little better during times like these. To know that you're not crazy and not too sensitive, that this is simply how our brain works. This text is for me, and for everyone who's sitting in the same place right now.

Three in the morning

I wake up at three. No alarm; that's set for five and has barely had a chance in all this time. My body got used to it, the way you get used to anything that happens every day. Get up, coffee, and then the first glance at my phone. On the other side of the ocean it was evening, and someone there was waiting for me to put them to bed. How was your day, what did you eat, did you sleep well. Trivial sentences. The most beautiful ones of the whole day.

For a few days now, I've still been waking up at three. My body still gets up, still reaches for the phone, still opens the window where nobody writes anymore. My head knows it's over. It read the message, it understood it, it has even written texts about it. The rest of me apparently hasn't caught up yet.

I always wanted to know what grief actually is. Why it hurts so much, even though nobody has done anything physical to you. Why your throat tightens and your heart ties itself in knots. And why you sometimes cry over people you haven't cared about in years. So I read up on it. Here's what I found, and what it did to me.

The map in your head

The psychologist Mary-Frances O'Connor spent years looking into the brains of grieving people. Her explanation is surprisingly sober, and that's exactly why it hit me: grief is a form of learning.

For every person who matters to us, our brain creates a kind of inner map. It doesn't just record who this person is, but also something that's never said out loud: this person is there. Somewhere, reachable, close enough. When I get up at three in the morning, I don't have to think about who to message first. The map knows.

And then the person is gone. Through death, through a breakup, through one sentence on the wrong evening. The brain receives the information, but the map doesn't update itself. It's a bit like a satnav that reliably takes you, every morning, to an address where nobody lives anymore. You stand at the door, ring the bell, and only then do you remember.

Grief is this relearning. Not once, but hundreds of times. Every morning, every "I have to tell them about this", every song, every smell is a new lesson: the address isn't right anymore. The brain learns it only slowly, because it stored this person for a good reason. Attachment is vital to our survival. You don't delete something like that with a single click.

Why it hurts physically

The crazy thing is: nobody hit me. And yet there's something sitting in my chest that feels exactly like it. That's not imagination, and it's not exaggeration.

A little over twenty years ago, the researcher Naomi Eisenberger had people play a harmless ball-tossing game on a computer. At some point, the participants simply stopped getting the ball. A virtual ball, a handful of pixels, nothing more. And still, areas of the brain lit up that are also active during physical pain. Being excluded hurts, quite literally.

From an evolutionary point of view, that makes sense. For a herd animal, falling out of the group was life-threatening. Whoever was alone got eaten. So nature made sure that loss and exclusion feel so awful that we'll do anything to avoid them. The pain is an alarm system meant to drive us back to the herd.

And there's something else. The anthropologist Helen Fisher studied people who had just been left. In their brains, areas linked to reward and craving were active, similar to withdrawal. That explains why you keep looking at your phone, rereading old messages, opening their profile, even though you know perfectly well it doesn't help. That isn't weakness. That's a brain waiting for its usual dose.

Anyone with RSD knows this alarm system especially well, because ours is set more sensitively. It goes off at a single late reply. With a real loss, it doesn't just go off, it howls all night long. At least now I know why.

Grieving the ones we stopped caring about

And then there's this other, stranger kind of grief. You hear that someone has died whom you haven't seen in years. An old school friend, a former flame, a relative you never really warmed to. Actually, this person didn't matter to you anymore. And yet suddenly you're sitting there with a lump in your throat. For a long time I wondered whether that wasn't a bit dishonest. Today I think there are several reasons for it, and none of them is dishonest.

Not caring is rarely zero. A bond that once existed isn't deleted. It gets moved to the archive. My head does this completely automatically: when someone shows up less often, they slide further back, but never entirely out of sight. Death pulls these people out of the archive in one go, and with them everything that once was.

You grieve the maybe. As long as someone is alive, there's a possibility, even if you'd never use it. A talk, an apology, a chance meeting at a bus stop. With death, that door closes forever. You don't grieve what was, but everything that now can never be.

You grieve a piece of yourself. Everyone who was once close to us is a witness to a chapter of our life. The person I was at fifteen only exists in the memories of a handful of people. When one of them dies, a small piece of that version of me dies with them.

And you grieve your own finiteness. Every death is a reminder that everything ends, you included, and the people you love right now. That's often the biggest and quietest part of grief, especially for those who were actually far away. It doesn't knock. It just sits down next to you.

At some point over the last few days, I asked myself whether love is a kind of energy. Something you produce, a little every day, and that doesn't simply stop just because the person on the other side has left.

I think it is. Love isn't a tap you turn off, and then there's peace. It grew over weeks and months, in habits, in anticipation, in small rituals at three in the morning. When the person leaves, the love is still there. It just doesn't have an address anymore. It stands on the street with its suitcase and doesn't know where to go.

That's grief. Love that no longer has a place to go.

And like energy in physics, it doesn't simply disappear, it transforms. Part of it turns into pain, the part that keeps you awake at night. Part of it turns into memory, into the archive you'll one day be able to take something from without it hurting right away. Sometimes part of it turns into something new, in my case a friendship, because we decided not to treat each other like strangers. And part of it flows into whatever you make of it. Over the last few days I've written a song, a story and several texts. That's quite a lot of transformed energy for someone who's barely slept.

None of it is lost. It just finds a new path.

What is actually missing

If I'm honest, it isn't the physical side I miss. I even think you forget the sensations of sex surprisingly quickly, the same way you forget faces. What remains is something else. Love is sharing, being there for each other, a togetherness. Something that happens almost entirely in feeling.

The small talk in the morning never really carried information. How I'd slept didn't matter. What mattered was that someone asked. Every one of those little questions was really saying: I'm here, and you matter enough to me for something trivial. Those conversations warmed my day, because I didn't have to feel alone.

The relationship researcher John Gottman calls moments like these "bids", small offers to turn towards each other. A "look at this", a "how was it?", a heart for no particular reason. He watched couples for years and found that relationships are carried less by big gestures than by whether you respond to these tiny offers. Couples who often turn towards each other stay together. Couples who turn away lose each other, usually very quietly.

That's why it's the trivial things, of all things, that hurt so much. You don't miss the information. You miss being turned towards.

And that's exactly why I don't believe there's a big difference between love online and love offline. In both cases we store fragments of the other person. We fill part of our brain with them, with their quirks, their favourite words, their daily routine, with all the trivial things. Whether that happens at a kitchen table or in a chat window, the brain doesn't much care. In the end, a part of that person lives on inside us. And it's exactly that part that heads for the old address at three in the morning.

What grief needs

If grief is learning, then it needs what all learning needs: repetition and time. You can't skip it, just as you can't learn a language by flicking through the dictionary really fast.

Many people know the model with the five stages, from denial to acceptance, neatly one after the other. Reality is messier. Grief comes in waves. One day you're doing surprisingly well, and the next a song in the supermarket is enough, and you're standing between the pasta and the tinned tomatoes trying to hold it together. That isn't a step backwards. It's one more lesson for the map in your head.

What helps me is not pushing the waves away. Every wave you let through teaches the map something. Every one you swallow comes back later, usually at three in the morning.

Grief also needs a body that someone looks after. Sleep, eat, get some fresh air, even when you don't feel like it. That sounds banal, but an exhausted brain learns badly, and it makes everything louder, especially if you already have a sensitive alarm system.

It needs company, not a cure. The people who check in can't fill the gap, and they don't have to. It's enough if they sit with you for a while.

And it doesn't need a replacement. The temptation is strong to fill the empty spot quickly so it stops hurting. But if you send a new person to an old address, you won't really see them there. New love deserves a place of its own, not an inherited one.

If it doesn't get easier after a few weeks, but harder, getting help isn't a defeat. Some maps are so big that while you redraw them, you need someone to help hold the pen.

Tomorrow at three

I'll keep waking up at three; I don't get a say in that anyway. Three in the morning was my time long before it became ours, and it will stay mine afterwards too.

Tomorrow my body will get up again and reach for the phone, and my head will have to explain to it once more that the address isn't right anymore. That will go on for a while. Every one of these small disappointments is a lesson, and at some point the map will have learned it.

Until then, I'll do something different with those first few minutes. I'll make coffee, sit down by the window and ask myself the question someone else used to ask me.

How did you sleep?

Badly, I write. But better than yesterday.

And I hope that someday I'll find someone again who asks me the same thing, and it simply feels good. People do check in now too, and I'm grateful for that. But it's never the same feeling as with someone you feel deeply connected to. 🖤
