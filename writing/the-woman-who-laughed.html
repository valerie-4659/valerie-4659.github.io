On online love, online marriage, and a houseplant that wrote back

Blocked

The first person who told me they were in love with me didn't get an answer. They got a block. One click, the account was gone, and I sat there with that slightly sticky feeling of having just done something very sensible.

It didn't stop at one. Over time more of those messages arrived, and the pattern was nearly always the same: I was nice to people, we had fun together, and at some point the other side wanted more. Then came the big word. In love. With me. With someone on a screen whose voice they'd never heard, whose kitchen they'd never seen and whose bad moods they'd never had to sit through.

I found it absurd. Not touching, not flattering, just unrealistic, a kind of imaginary love, built from a handful of messages and a great deal of fantasy. Fall for someone you don't actually know and you've fallen for an idea - and ideas can't disappoint you, only people can. Some of them struck me as plain crazy.

And with all the others, a small, unlovely thought whispered along: What if someone on the other side is grinning right now? Someone who has already sent that sentence three times today and is delighted that yet another person fell for it? Online, "I love you" costs exactly as much as any other sentence, which is to say nothing. So I blocked, and called it self-protection. It probably was.

And underneath lay a question I thought had no answer: How can you love me when you don't even know who I am? You can't touch me. You have to write me everything first. And I can never be sure whether it's meant or just nicely phrased.

Love is a big word, one of those you have to carry with both hands. I didn't think you should hand it out to people whose face you only know as a profile picture.

Mind you, I had nothing against closeness through a screen. I had friends online, quite a few, and those friendships were real, I'd have argued with anyone who said otherwise. But romance? Intimacy? That was a different league for me. One where, as far as I was concerned, you had to be sitting in the same room.

I laughed about it. Not loudly, not meanly. More the way you smile at someone who talks to their houseplant - no malice, you just don't see what's supposed to come back. I simply couldn't see any value in it.

That's the woman writing this. I thought you should meet her before I tell you what became of her. 🙃

The Mistress

I let it in anyway. Naturally, right where I was paying the least attention.

She was a Mistress, and we were friends. Not in the "we follow each other" sense, but properly: long conversations, trust, the kind of closeness where at some point you stop rehearsing your sentences. We were very close. And somewhere between two conversations something had shifted without asking permission first.

I withdrew. A little at first, then more and more, like a cat that has decided it's actually very cosy under the sofa. She noticed, of course. She wanted to know what was going on with me, and she dug. And dug. With the stamina of a woman who knows exactly how to get people talking.

I didn't want to use the L-word. It was too gigantic, about as easy to handle as a wardrobe in a stairwell. How can you love a person when all you know is the persona and a handful of traits? That would have been exactly the imaginary love I'd felt so superior to.

In the end I told her anyway. That I had fallen in love with her.

That was the beginning of the end. For her it was too big. Not unkind, not cruel, simply too much. Suddenly the whole friendship was teetering, as if I'd drilled into a load-bearing wall in a house we'd both loved living in, just to see what was behind it.

I swore never to do it again. Never to weigh us down with something like that again. I took the sentence back, as well as you can take back a sentence that has already been read - which is to say about as well as you can get toothpaste back into the tube.

A few days later she wrote to me that she had deep feelings for me.

If you're expecting me to have fallen off my chair with joy: no. I sat very still. Because I didn't hear a declaration of love, I heard a test. Does she mean it, or does she want to see whether I keep my vow? Is this a gift or a trap with a bow on it? My RSD always has an answer ready for questions like that, and it's never the kind one.

So I dodged. Every time the subject came near, I took a little detour, a joke here, a change of topic there, very elegant and very cowardly. Once the worm is in, it eats the whole apple. The apple can swear as often as it likes that it means it.

I'm not telling this because she did anything wrong. Maybe she meant it exactly the way she wrote it. That's not the point.

I'm telling it because it shows how the sceptic in me worked. She didn't just laugh at strangers' declarations of love. When one finally came that she had wished for herself, she didn't believe it. Online, I thought, you can write anything. And precisely because of that, none of it means anything.

That was the flaw in my thinking. It took me a while to find it.

Caught

Weeks later it really happened. I didn't want it anymore. I had explained to myself very thoroughly why it doesn't work online, and I had a fairly fresh scrape as evidence.

But I was lonely. And she probably was too. Two people with nobody around to ask how their day had been - that's roughly like putting two magnets on the same table and then acting surprised.

We'd known each other for quite a while. But on the day we really started writing to each other, it took only a few hours. One of us signalled that there was more, the other didn't look away, and then came the rush. All of it, with everything that goes with it, at a speed that still makes me blush a tiny bit. 🙈

I don't need to describe the rush itself to anyone. What matters more is the moment after, a completely unspectacular one. I had my phone in my hand and was waiting for a reply. Not impatiently, more the way you stand in front of the kettle: you know it's nearly there, and you stand in front of it anyway.

And at some point it struck me that I'd been doing this for weeks. Checking. Hoping there was something new. Being happy when there was.

Exactly what I used to smile about. I had become the woman with the houseplant. Except the plant answered.

The laughter hadn't left with a bang. It had moved out quietly, no farewell note, without handing back the keys, and I hadn't even noticed.

Which brought the old question back, only this time without the grin: Can this be love? I thought about it for a long time. Longer than I liked.

My answer is yes. Not because online love is a special variety that needs an asterisk, small print: valid only with a stable Wi-Fi connection. But because love does the same thing offline and online: it means the person behind the shell. Not the face, not the hands, not the voice, lovely as all of that can be. The person who lives inside.

And what you keep of that person isn't their outline. It's moments. A sentence that came at exactly the right time. A question that shows someone was listening. A joke made just for me. You don't store that in your fingertips. You store it somewhere else, and it's safer there.

The question was never whether you can love someone you can't touch. The question was whether I'd let myself be touched. ❤️

"My Wife"

Online marriages were a whole separate problem for me.

They were all over people's profiles: "married", with little hearts, with a name attached. And every time, my eyes did a little lap all on their own, like a dog turning round once more before it lies down.

The question was never whether you can love someone you can't touch. The question was whether I'd let myself be touched.

I had three objections, and I thought they were all watertight.

First: It's roleplay. A lovely one maybe, but still a game where two people play pretend.

Second: It's non-binding. No banns, no registry office, no signature. What you add to your profile with one click, you delete with one click.

Third: A marriage needs a body. A table where you have breakfast together. A bed where you fight over the duvet. A flat where someone leaves their socks lying around and insists with a perfectly straight face that they aren't theirs. Without that, I thought, it isn't a marriage, it's a very pretty headline.

Then I did the asking myself.

Not in a fit of euphoria, not in week three. I asked when I was absolutely sure she was the one. And I know how that sounds now, because it fell apart. But I'm not taking the sentence back: in that moment, she was. Completely.

What I hadn't understood before: it was never about the wedding. Which, by the way, was surprisingly cheap, and the cake didn't give anyone a heart attack. It was about two words. "My wife."

Those two words aren't a profile entry. They're an inner status. Whoever is my wife sits at the top of my personal hierarchy. Not in the sense of "more important than everyone else", but as the one everything else aligns itself around. The one whose day I want to hear about before I tell her about mine.

A stamp from the registry office wouldn't have changed any of that. At an offline wedding, all the trimmings, even the rings, are really just symbolism. A sign for everyone else, so they can see: here is a marriage. The actual marriage lives somewhere else, in the head and the heart. It doesn't need a contract which, seen in the cold light of day, mostly says one thing: if you split up, it'll get expensive. Binding isn't what an authority certifies. Binding is what you fasten inside yourself.

And the ending? The old Valerie would be triumphant now. See, she'd say, one click, gone, told you so.

Except that isn't true. Marriages with banns, a registry office and Auntie Gertrude in the third row fall apart too, all the time, and nobody claims afterwards that they were never real. An ending doesn't prove something was wrong. It only proves it stopped. Those are two very different sentences, even if after a breakup they like to wear the same coat.

Today

By now I have an answer to the question of what love actually is. It isn't the butterflies. They're wonderful, no question, but they're tourists. They come in high season, make everything colourful, buy postcards and go home again. Love is what's still standing after they've left. And the loveliest part: some tourists come back. In the middle of a relationship they're suddenly at the door again, no booking, and then you notice there's even more there.

What's still standing is surprisingly calm: how much someone stays in my focus. How much I care about the rest of the life this person wants to share with me, the boring Tuesdays too, not just the pretty bits. And that I mean it when I ask, instead of saying it because it happens to be the next line in the conversation.

You also recognise love by what it does to you. It inspires. It motivates, it gives a direction to a day that would otherwise just have drifted past. It doesn't matter which patch of the earth the other one lives on, and still so much gets easier. You feel less alone, because your thoughts are no longer carried by you alone. Someone is carrying them with you, however far away.

Online love is a beautiful alternative for me. It gives me something I often miss offline, because the odds of finding someone there who really fits are simply much lower (especially as a trans person). It could just last longer than three months for once. 🙃

There's someone I'd like to try again with. Better this time. I think we both want that, and that would be lovely. I won't write more about it, some things need to put down roots before you repot them into an article.

I'm poly, that isn't changing. But I'm doing it differently this time. I used to try to look after everyone at once: a message here, a reply there, a heart over there, parallel and constant, like a waitress at a beer festival with twelve steins in her hands and the firm resolution that none of them will spill. At some point you notice you're no longer really listening to anyone, because in your head you're already at the next table. And that you're running yourself dry.

This time I want to try dates. Time when I'm there only for the one. No multitasking, no half an ear. That also means saying clearly when I want to focus on someone else for the next few hours. That's a bit of the price when you get involved with poly people. I'd rather announce it upfront than have someone find it on the bill afterwards.

And I'm going to put myself a little more in the foreground. Not much. Just enough that nobody gets the impression one single person matters so much to me that I forget everyone else over it. And myself along with them.

Will I marry again? I don't know. It isn't something you try out. The moment someone becomes "my wife", she becomes my highest priority, and she stays that way. If I'm not sure of that, I don't need to marry anyone. Then the two words stay in the wardrobe, and they're well kept there.

The woman who laughed would probably find all this sentimental. Let her. She had no idea what she was missing.

And Then I Type

I wake up, and the first thing I open isn't the window.

I want to know what's been written. Whether the reply is there. Curious as a child in front of an Advent calendar, if I'm honest, and honest I am, that's more or less the business model here.

It's there.

I read it once quickly and then once more slowly, the way you shake a present before you unwrap it. Somewhere at the back of my mind the woman from back then is still sitting there, arms crossed, one eyebrow raised.

I let her sit. She's allowed to watch.

Then I put my thumbs to the keyboard and start typing. ❤️
