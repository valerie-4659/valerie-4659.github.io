It's a little past one in the morning, I'm sitting alone in a dark room, and I'm laughing so hard I have to clap a hand over my own mouth, because there are people asleep in this house. There are eleven words on the screen. The person who wrote them is several hundred kilometres away, I have never once touched her, and I still know exactly what her face looked like while she typed it.

Whenever I tell someone about this, the same question turns up: "Yes, but is it something real?"

And that's where the trouble starts. Not with the question - with the answer most people quietly give themselves. Because if you believe it isn't real, you treat it accordingly. People handle an online relationship the way they handle a TV series: as long as it entertains, they stick with it, and when an episode drags, they go do something else. Nobody works on a series. Nobody says "we need to talk" to a series.

I want to argue the opposite here. Not that an online relationship is the same as a relationship with a shared washing machine. It isn't, and we'll get to that at length. But that it is its own thing. Something that can hold weight. Something that steadies you when you live alone. Something that gives you what's missing elsewhere. And that it deserves exactly as much work as anything else that holds you up.

And yes, it can hurt like hell. We'll get to that too, don't worry. 🖤

There is a person sitting there

Before anything else applies, this does:

There is a real human being on the other side. Always. Even when there's a persona standing in front of her.

That sounds like stating the obvious, and sadly it isn't, or people wouldn't behave the way they do online. The screen does something to us. It briskly files whoever we're talking to into the same drawer as TV characters, banner ads and people you shout at on Twitter. All just symbols. All just content.

Except it isn't. Behind the avatar with the drawn-on face sits somebody with back pain, unpaid bills and a load of laundry that's been quietly rotting in the machine since Tuesday. Behind the name that doesn't exist is a person who has to be up in the morning. The character is allowed to be invented. The person writing her never is. She had a day before she wrote to you, and she'll have a day after you're gone.

Which is why "it was only online, though" doesn't work as a sentence. That sentence describes the channel. It says nothing about what actually arrived.

If you hurt someone online, you hurt a human being. If you love someone online, you love a human being. There is no in-between setting, no mode in which it all counts a bit less because it was typed.

Everything that follows comes out of that one point.

Don't compare flirting to a relationship

Let's sort out what we're even talking about.

A flirt is fireworks. Loud, colourful, everyone looks up, and after twelve minutes it's over and smells faintly of sulphur. Flirting is wonderful. Flirting is also no achievement whatsoever, because both sides only need their best three lines and then get to go home. I have excellent best-three-lines. You cannot build a single thing with them.

A relationship is a campfire. It photographs terribly. It impresses nobody. But you can warm your hands at it, and the moment things get unpleasant outside, that turns out to be the only thing that matters.

A campfire does want wood, though. Regularly. And almost always precisely when you've just got comfortable. It's the third Tuesday evening in a row where nothing special happens and you're there anyway. It's the question of whether you ask why someone has been writing oddly for two days - even though you suspect the answer will be awkward and you're honestly quite tired.

The most common mistake I see online: people hold their campfire up next to fireworks and are disappointed that it doesn't bang. So they move on to the next fireworks. And the next. And then wonder why, two years later, they're still cold.

No substitute. And real anyway.

Let's say it plainly so it's been said: an online relationship does not replace a relationship where someone is in the room.

It can't hold you while you cry. It can't bring you tea. It can't sit next to you and say nothing, which is honestly one of the most underrated human skills there is. It won't notice you've looked awful for four days unless you write it down yourself. Anyone claiming all of this is equivalent is selling you something.

And still: it can be deeply fulfilling. It can be the place where, for the first time in years, you say exactly what you mean, because nobody interrupts you and nobody is emptying the dishwasher at the same time. It brings together people who would never have met offline - not as a sad fallback, but because you found brains that fit yours, and brains are distributed geographically in a truly miserable fashion. It can steady you when you live alone. It can give you something your relationship at home is missing, and that's not shameful, it's a very, very old human arrangement.

But it has a built-in design flaw, and you need to know about it: it is far too easy to end.

Offline, quite a lot holds you together that has nothing to do with love. The lease. The car. The friends who will ask. The cat that only one of you can look after, and who knows it. That's not a romantic foundation, but it is a foundation, and it's the reason you sit out a difficult week instead of ending it.

Online there is none of that. There's a button.

That's why online relationships end fast. Not because they're worth less, but because the exit is right next to the sofa and permanently open. Anyone who works on something online is doing it one hundred percent voluntarily. There is not one single external reason to stay.

Which, if you turn it round, makes staying rather a big deal. 💛

All you have is text

Now for the craft, because this is a skill and not a talent, and that's the good news.

Everything a body normally handles - gesture, expression, the eye-roll, the tears, the tone, the hesitation, the warmth - has to squeeze through about forty characters a line. It's rather like being asked to play an orchestra on a recorder. It can be done. But only if you don't treat the recorder as a toy.

The toolbox

You have more to work with than you think.

Asterisks for action. Lowercase that sounds tired. The sentence that simply breaks off because - The paragraph that's two words long, because it doesn't land otherwise. The written-out sigh. The typo you leave in because it's more honest than the corrected version. The pause you place deliberately, which speaks exactly the way a pause speaks out loud.

They read everything. Truly everything.

You need to know this, ideally before someone throws it at you.

There are people - considerably more of them than you'd hope - who read every character. Which emojis you use and which have suddenly vanished. Whether there's an exclamation mark where there wasn't one. Whether the full stop at the end is new. Whether "hey" has turned into "hello". Whether the pet name is missing.

You're allowed to find that silly. Unfortunately it isn't silly, it's simply logical: if someone can't see your face, they'll analyse whatever is available. And what's available is punctuation. Humans are pattern-recognising animals, and when you take a pattern away from us, we just go and find a smaller one.

I'm one of them. I once spent half an hour interpreting a missing heart. It was the battery. 🙃

Response time talks too

The classic. Someone who normally replies in three minutes suddenly takes six hours, and in those six hours the other person's brain writes an entire novel. With subplots, flashbacks and a distinctly unpleasant ending.

Usually it was the washing machine. Sometimes it wasn't. The point stands: the time between your messages is part of your communication whether you like it or not. "Out tonight, I'll write tomorrow" costs you four seconds and saves a human being half a day.

Different brains, different countries

This is where most misunderstandings are born, and almost never out of malice.

Online gathers people who would never be in the same room offline. Different countries, different languages, different brains. That's the entire appeal of the thing. It's also the fault line.

Neurodivergence plays a big part here, and in both directions. Some people read every character and calculate what the missing full stop means. Some people mean exactly what's written and cannot fathom why anyone is offended. Both are right. Both are wrong. And neither of them wanted to break anything.

Add cultural difference to that. Directness that's perfectly ordinary in one country reads as an attack in another. Politeness that means respect over there comes across as cold and dismissive over here. Irony rarely survives a border crossing, and in a second language it dies on send.

Which means: things you consider harmless can acquire enormous weight on the other end. A sentence you typed in four seconds can sit with her for three days and get heavy as wet laundry.

You won't be able to predict this. That isn't the standard. The standard is what you do when it happens - and the right answer is not "well, that's not how I meant it" and straight on with the conversation. The right answer is asking what actually arrived.

And the other way round: if something lands badly, ask before you spend three days writing your novel.

Asking is not weakness online. It's the replacement for the face you can't see.

You invent part of the person

This has to be said out loud, even though it's uncomfortable and I'd rather not write it.

Text has gaps, and your brain is an animal that cannot tolerate gaps. It fills them. And since it has no data, it fills them with the best thing it knows: the ideal person you've always wanted anyway.

So part of what you love, you wrote yourself. That's no disgrace and it doesn't make the feeling false. But you only notice it when the actual person contradicts your draft - when she says something that doesn't fit, and you find yourself, astonishingly, experiencing it as betrayal. She hasn't betrayed anything. She just never agreed to be your invention.

The archive

And then there's the thing that simply doesn't exist offline: nothing between you is fleeting.

Every argument is re-readable. Every promise documented, with a timestamp. The sentence you wrote seven months ago on a bad night is still retrievable, verbatim, which is outrageously unfair, given that human beings remember their own sentences considerably more generously than the transcript does.

It's a blessing. You can go back and read how the whole thing started, and sometimes that's the loveliest thing you have. It's also a curse. And it's an excellent reason to phrase things more carefully online than you would at the kitchen table.

Be what you are

Now the part that matters most to me.

A relationship is not ERP. You don't crank everything up to eleven so that both of you sit there fizzing at the screen. A relationship reflects who you are - and the closer you stay to yourself, the more real it feels. Not because authenticity is a virtue you get gold stars for, but because everything else is labour, and nobody sustains labour for years.

Which gives you the only test question you actually need before you write yourself into anything:

Would I live this for real, and stand by it?

Not: do I find this hot. Not: does this sound good. But: would I actually want to hang there tied up for seven hours, when after twenty minutes everything aches, my foot's gone numb and I'm hungry? Could I genuinely handle someone else taking priority on an ordinary Tuesday? Would I want to carry responsibility for a person I can't catch when she falls?

Our heads find plenty of things hot. That's entirely fine, and trying them out mentally is fine too - that's what the head is for, it's the only place you can test things without breaking an arm or ruining the carpet.

The difference lies elsewhere: RP is RP, and a relationship is a relationship. Unless everything is RP - in which case that's clean too, as long as both of you know it and both of you want it that way.

What breaks things is the blur. You write yourself into something because it's working nicely and the other person responds so beautifully. And at some point there's an actual human being with actual expectations standing there, and you realise you never intended to live any of it.

Then you didn't play. You lied.

So: make the seam visible. Have a signal for "this is me now, not the character". That doesn't break anything - on the contrary, it's the precondition for the game being free at all.

And the hardest point, which applies well beyond any play: be honest about how much you love. Never say something just to please. Never say something because it happens to suit the scene. "I love you" is not a building block for improving the mood. It's a statement about your condition. And if it isn't true, you cause damage you cannot go back and collect.

But it works in the other direction too, and that's the part almost everybody skips. Say what you feel. All of it that concerns the other person.

Also when you're sad. Also when you're low and can't quite say why. Also when you're frightened. Also - and especially - when you feel neglected.

Because offline someone sees it. Offline, a person sitting next to you notices you haven't properly laughed in three days, and eventually asks. Online they notice nothing. Online you're just a little more monosyllabic than usual, and that doesn't read as sadness, it reads as disinterest. Your bad week arrives at her end as "she doesn't like me any more". Then you both sit in the same silence and draw two completely different, equally wrong conclusions.

So say it. It doesn't have to be elegant.

"I miss you" is a complete sentence. "I'm not doing well right now, it has nothing to do with you" spares a person a week of rumination. "I feel like I'm getting the short end" isn't an accusation, it's information - and information you can actually do something with. The other person can accept it, or explain it, or change it. But only if it reaches them at all.

Unspoken feelings don't disappear, you see. They just relocate. They settle into your messages, make them shorter, cooler and somehow pointed, and then both of you wonder where the lovely mood went. 💛

Making more of it

Up to here it's been rather a lot about what to avoid. Now the nice part: what you can actually do.

Because depth doesn't happen by itself online. Offline it accumulates half by accident - through shared time, shared rooms, shared shopping, through the sheer volume of small rubbish you experience together without ever planning it. Online there is no small rubbish. Online there is only what you actively write down. Everything else simply doesn't exist.

That sounds like work, and it is. But it comes with an unbeatable advantage: you get to choose what you build.

Be curious. Especially about the boring parts.

Ask about the ordinary day. How was work. What nice thing did you do today. What was that appointment you mentioned last week actually about. Has the neighbour kicked off again.

It sounds banal, which is exactly why so few people do it. Everyone wants to be meaningful together, everyone wants depth, big conversations, soulmate-hood with subtitles. But meaningfulness isn't what holds people together. What holds you together is knowing what the other person's life actually looks like: which bus she takes, what her boss is called, which mug is the right mug, why she hates Thursdays.

Anyone who only exchanges highlights online ends up knowing a summary, not a human being.

And then the sentence that probably does more work than anything else in this article: "So how did the appointment go?" Because it doesn't only prove you listened. It proves you carried it with you, that it went on living in your head while she wasn't there. That is by far the cheapest way to show a person they count.

Small talk is life talk

Don't be afraid of small talk. It has a dreadful reputation, because we associate it with lifts, work parties and dentists' waiting rooms.

But with someone you love, small talk isn't small talk. It's life talk. The conversation about the weather is really a conversation about what her day feels like. The conversation about the new series is really about what makes her cry. Nobody has ever genuinely talked about the weather.

Find topics you're both interested in and get properly stuck into them. Argue about trivia. Send each other things that reminded you of the other - that's the online version of "saw this and thought of you", and it works just as well.

Routine is good. Fatigue isn't.

Routine also has an undeserved bad name. Rituals are the thing that carries an online relationship at all: the morning message, the fixed evening, the goodnight that always comes. Reliability is worth more online than excitement, because it's the only thing that can stand in for the missing everyday.

It only gets tiring when the routine is the entire content. When you fill in the same form every evening: "Evening." - "Hey, how was your day?" - "Fine, yours?" - "Same." And then you both stare at the cursor.

So break it open now and then. Not with grand gestures, with different questions. What was the best thing about your week. What are you most afraid of right now. What would you do if a free day fell out of the sky tomorrow. Tell me something about you I don't know yet - and yes, people go quiet for a moment after that, but then they start, and then it gets lovely.

Do things together, not just alongside each other

Talking isn't the only way to share time.

Watch the same film at the same time and comment your way through it. Play something together. Read the same book, each in your own bed. Cook the same recipe on Saturday and send each other the results, including the parts that went wrong. Just leave the call open in the evening even when nobody's talking - that's the nearest thing to sitting next to each other and saying nothing.

Send each other photos of the unspectacular. And I explicitly don't mean pictures of yourselves - I mean pictures of what you're looking at. Today's sky. The chaotic desk. The coffee, the cat on the laundry basket, the roadworks you have to walk past every morning. That's closeness with no face involved at all: for a few seconds you're looking out of the same eyes.

Someone who can see the other person's everyday life doesn't have to invent it - which, incidentally, is the best antidote there is to the projection problem from earlier.

And yes, the erotic side belongs here too

Let's not pretend this is a footnote. 😄

Do ERP. Do sexting. Throw pictures and videos at each other and wind each other up. If you fancy it, buy toys you can control remotely - the technology has got surprisingly good at ignoring distance.

But the actual point is a different one, and it's the one nearly everybody leaves out: let the other person in while you're doing it.

Say what you're actually doing while your characters are busy with each other. How you're lying. What you're feeling right now. Where your hands are. That's the difference between two people taking turns sending paragraphs, and two people in bed together who happen to be a few hundred kilometres apart.

Close your eyes and let it be her hand touching you, while she sits opposite you and watches. That isn't a gimmick. That's precisely what text is capable of and what it was built for: it makes pictures inside heads, and heads are famously the organ where everything happens anyway.

Ask each other afterwards how it was. Not as a feedback round, but because "that was lovely, but I'd like more of X" is a sentence a great many people never manage offline - and manage online quite easily, because you don't have to look at each other while typing. Use that. It's one of the few genuine advantages you've got.

For anything longer, a dedicated place is worth it. A chat kept open for that alone, where a story can run on for weeks without "did you do the washing" landing in the middle of it. That protects both sides: the scene stays a scene, and your everyday stays everyday.

Two more things, then I'll stop wagging my finger.

First: all of it is voluntary. Always, every single time, in both directions. And it must never turn into that particular piece of arithmetic - "I showed you this and that of me, and you're showing me nothing". That isn't fairness, that's pressure with tidy bookkeeping. Not everyone wants to send pictures of themselves, and the reasons are nobody's business. There are lives in which a picture like that is a genuine risk. There are people who don't like looking at their own body. And there are people who simply don't feel like it, which is an entirely sufficient reason on its own.

Anyone who pushes anyway might get their picture. But they'll get it from someone who felt talked into it, and in doing so they've destroyed the exact thing they were after.

Second: remember the archive. Pictures don't expire either. Only send what you'd still want to have sent on a day when the two of you aren't good any more.

But not only that

And now the brakes, because otherwise I'll be misread.

ERP and sexting are not meant to replace your everyday. "Hey, how are you, I'm horny, let's have sex" is completely fine as an occasional opener and, frankly, quite charming. As the only mode, day after day, it's the most reliable way I know of wearing an online relationship out.

Because then two people turn into a vending machine. You know what's coming the moment you open the window, you know the running order, and eventually it feels less like closeness and more like an appointment to get something done. And a person who feels processed isn't closer to you afterwards. They're further away.

The erotic side lives off everything else. It gets better the more you know about each other - more everyday, more trust, more small rubbish. It's dessert. Dessert is glorious. Dessert is also the one thing you've genuinely had enough of after three days.

So that you look forward to her coming online

Which is what all of this is really aiming at.

Anticipation is what makes joy, and anticipation needs something to point at. So give each other something. Save stories for later instead of firing them off immediately. Have a thing that belongs only to you - a word, a joke nobody else understands, a name for something that doesn't need a name. Celebrate anniversaries, including daft ones. The day you first wrote to each other is a real date and is allowed to be celebrated.

And plan something. Anything. However small: a book you'll both read in the autumn. A series starting Friday. A goal you're heading towards together.

Because a relationship that only has a present always feels slightly provisional. The moment there's a shared later - even if it's only next Tuesday - a nice chat turns into something with a direction.

When there's more than one of you

Tell the people you love when you have time for them.

Yes, that sounds like management. Like calendars, like project plans, like everything that's supposedly got nothing to do with love.

It isn't, though. It's just more honest than what most people do.

Offline nobody thinks about it, because the body handles it. If you're cuddling Susan today, you cannot simultaneously be in bed with Nadine. That's not an agreement, that's physics.

And the disappointing sort of physics at that. An electron is allowed to be in several places at once as long as nobody's looking - it's called superposition and it's deeply unjust, given that this ability was handed to particles who have absolutely no use for it. You, unfortunately, are about a sextillion times too large. You are always exactly where you are, and nowhere else.

(Unless you're lucky and Susan and Nadine live with and love each other. Then you get to lie in the middle, which is the only case I know of where a human comes genuinely close to being in two places at once. You'll still have problems, obviously, but not scheduling ones. 😄)

Online, however, you suddenly are an electron after all. You can have three windows open side by side, and as long as nobody's looking, you're with all three at once. The boundary your body normally draws is simply gone. Which is why the agreement has to replace what the room used to handle.

And out of that comes the genuinely lovely part: someone who knows that Tuesday is her evening doesn't have to check every single evening whether she still matters.

The second part is focus. Don't try to entertain everyone in parallel. You don't enjoy it either when the person you love starts a conversation with you and keeps glancing at her phone. Online the phone is merely invisible. The effect is identical, and most people notice it considerably sooner than you're hoping: in the length of your replies, in the pace, in the fact that you didn't respond to the second half of her message at all.

And then there's the question of the number. If you live poly, please think honestly about how many people you actually have capacity for. Not how many you can feel affection for - affection is cheap and effectively unlimited, the warm feeling stretches comfortably to fill a stadium. Capacity is something else. Capacity is time, attention, energy, and the ability to still be there for someone on a rotten day.

And it doesn't scale nicely. With two people you have two relationships. With three you have three relationships plus the question of how those three feel about each other. The more there are, the more complicated it gets - not along a friendly straight line, but along a curve that eventually bends upwards in a rather unpleasant way.

What's left at the end is a "hi". For everyone. A friendly, fast, utterly meaningless "hi", because twenty other people are due a hello and there's something longer running with two of them on the side. And not everyone is content to be a hi. Most people aren't. They just don't say so, because they don't want to feel demanding.

For myself, I worked out at some point that three is my limit. Not on principle, but because I tried it and noticed that beyond that somebody always gets short-changed - and reliably the one who's currently asking for the least. And if one of the three is genuinely intense, it's more like two. That number doesn't have to be yours, yours might be higher or lower. But it's worth knowing it at all, instead of overestimating it fresh every time.

And if you're unsure, just turn it around.

Imagine being with someone who lives poly - and barely has time for you. Who gets in touch every few days, always friendly, always brief, always in passing between two other windows. And who then writes "I love you".

Would you believe it? Or would you sit there wondering what that word still means coming from him, if it costs him so little?

That's the test question. Not "am I allowed to do this". But: "would I want to put up with this?"

And then there are the people who are already there. The partner, the children, the life that doesn't happen on a screen. Who knows what? And more to the point: did you actually ask, or did you assume it would be fine? Consent isn't something you can quietly issue to yourself.

Time is exactly as finite online as it is offline, it just doesn't feel that way. There's no journey there and no journey back, no getting up, no moment where you notice: oh, it's got late. Which is why this thing will eat you if you let it. Notification, small hit of dopamine, three in the morning, and by the next day your own life is one window smaller.

Look after yourself. Not because any of it is disgraceful, but because a person who has given up their own life becomes an alarmingly exhausting partner alarmingly fast.

The hard parts

Crisis at a distance

At some point the other person falls apart, and you're sitting eight hundred kilometres away with a keyboard.

That, I think, is the hardest experience in online relationships, and almost nobody talks about it honestly. You can't drive over. You can't hold a hand. You can't even be certain she's still there when nothing comes through for two hours.

What you can do is still not nothing: you can stay. You can write that you're staying. You can stop offering solutions and instead send the message that only says "I'm here". It's unspectacular and it looks like far too little. It isn't.

What you can't do is save the person. You need to know that before it happens - not so you can extract yourself, but so you don't break over having failed at something you were never able to do. If someone needs professional help, that's the right answer, and walking them towards it is also love. Just a less romantic variety.

Ending it cleanly

It will end. Maybe not this one, maybe not soon, but almost everything ends eventually, and that's exactly where it shows what sort of person you are.

Is your interest fading? Do you have less time? Has your life shifted? Say so. Give the other person a chance to adjust. And don't expect a crystal ball - nobody can see what's going on with you if you don't write it down. Human beings are remarkably bad at telepathy, despite all of us behaving as though it came as standard.

And if you've lost interest: be honest. An honest ending hurts for two weeks. A dishonest one hurts for years, because the other person never finds out what exactly they're supposed to be recovering from.

Ghosting, and what it does

And now the part where I'm not going to pretend I'm looking at this from the outside.

Ghosting is the worst thing you can do to a person online, and simultaneously the default solution, because it's so gloriously convenient. No conversation, no accusation, no tears. Just a window that doesn't open any more.

For the one left behind, it's grief without a funeral. You don't know whether it was boredom or a hospital. Whether you were rejected or whether somebody died. You can't mourn, because you don't know if there's anything to mourn. And you can't move on either, because there's that door, which might still open.

And then the brain turns up and does what brains always do with an information gap. It fills it. Usually with yourself.

For the one left behind, it's grief without a funeral. You don't know whether it was boredom or a hospital.

I have RDS - Rejection Dysphoria Syndrome, the thing where rejection doesn't hurt so much as detonate. For me that means the self-reference in anything difficult in a relationship can get genuinely dramatic. When someone leaves and doesn't leave a reason behind, the reason is me within seconds. Not as a suspicion. As a fact, with supporting evidence, at full volume, applied retroactively to everything I have ever written. Was it that sentence? Was I too much? Was I not enough?

For people built this way - and online there are very, very many of us - silence is not a neutral state. Silence is a verdict. And because it comes with no reasoning, you can't even argue against it.

So, quite simply: explain why it's over. It doesn't have to be long. Or flattering. Or even particularly well phrased. It just has to exist, so that the other person has something to hold on to when their own head interrogates them at half past three in the morning.

A broken heart heals. A broken heart without an explanation doesn't even get started.

What all this is for

I've written a great deal now about what goes wrong, and that was deliberate, because hardly anyone talks about it and most people stumble into this thing as though it were a pleasant way to pass the time.

But I'm not doing it because I think it's dangerous. I'm doing it because I think it's valuable.

It's a little past one in the morning, I'm sitting alone in a dark room, and I'm laughing. Eleven words. Somebody took the trouble to build something out of nothing but letters that reached me - across several hundred kilometres, a few server farms and a connection that has clearly seen better days. That's not a fallback. That's a small miracle that happens millions of times a day and for which nobody has yet found a decent word.

You can make something lasting out of this. Something lovely, for two people or for more. Something that steadies you when you live alone. Something that gives you what's missing elsewhere.

It only costs a little. Honesty about who you really are. Care in what you write. The willingness to ask instead of writing your own novel about it. And the nerve to take it seriously even though it could be over at any moment.

Not because it'll last. Because it's worth it while it's here.

So write back. Today. And live your life with someone you love; be part of a whole and make the most of your time. Don’t just focus on the good parts - see a person in all their facets. And that applies not only online, but offline as well. If you’re in a relationship, the rules are similar, just slightly different. Work together every day on the bigger picture that the two of you form.💛
