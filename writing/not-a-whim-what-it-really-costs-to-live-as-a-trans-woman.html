Not a Whim: What It Really Costs to Live as a Trans Woman

9 October 20267 min read

When I take her hand, she starts to cry.

Not because I did something wrong. But because almost no one touches her anymore.

We met through an initiative meant to bring older people back into society a little. I was one of the ones helping. She was one of the ones taking part. In the city where I live, trans isn't headline news for anyone anymore, and so we got talking, the way you get talking when you recognize a little of yourself in someone. The talking turned into a friendship.

She was forty when she made her decision. That was a good forty years ago, and the world was a different place back then. Her wife cast her out. Her children cast her out. The rest of the relatives followed suit. Families are remarkably well organized about these things.

Since then she has lived more or less alone. Today she lives in a care home. The other residents don't know what to do with her, and out in the corridor the carers whisper behind their hands. You hear whispering very well in a care home. There isn't much other noise.

She says she doesn't regret her decision. It was what she had always wanted, and if she hadn't done it, it would have destroyed her. Before, she was depressed, for years, because her head knew that something wasn't right, and knew it a little louder every year. I know that getting louder.

She regrets nothing. But just imagine it: forty years of living on the outside. For something you can't help.

Not a Lifestyle Choice

There are people who think trans is a trend. A whim. Like a tattoo after the third cocktail that you regret the next morning.

If it were a choice, it would be the stupidest one you could make. You trade a life where you're unhappy but belong for one where you can be happier but have to prove every single day that you're allowed to exist. Nobody signs up for that on a whim. You sign up because the alternative is slowly disappearing inside yourself.

And the nasty part is: it gets harder with every step, not easier. The first step happens in your head, where no one sees it. Then the first people you tell. The clothes. The name. The hormones. Eventually the street, work, the family at the dinner table.

Every step makes you more visible, and every step makes the world around you a little smaller. A few people leave. A few doors close, quietly, without anyone slamming them. The quiet ones are worse.

That's why many never go very far.

The Bill

At some point you reach a place where you start doing the math. Not with numbers, with people. Do I give in to who I am and risk being alone? Or can I live with not being me, and at least have someone?

That's not a choice between two lifestyles. It's a choice between two kinds of loneliness.

Why people stop at this point is something a large survey in the US looked into. Those who paused or reversed their transition mostly name reasons from outside. Pressure from parents. The stigma, the way the people around them looked at them. Not finding a job. Pressure from a partner. And surprisingly often, simply: it was too hard.

The most important thing isn't in the answers, it's next to them. The only people surveyed were those who still call themselves trans. They weren't wrong about themselves. They stopped because the bill got too high. And anyone who never sets out at all, or stops and stays stopped, shows up in no survey. How many that is, nobody knows.

At its core, we're punished twice. First your own head locks you in, for years, with the knowledge that something isn't right. And when you've finally fought your way out, society is waiting outside with the bill. You can't help either one. You pay anyway.

For Sex, Yes

In real life, most people simply don't want us. For sex, yes. For more, it often isn't enough.

There are nights that go surprisingly well, and mornings when you wake up alone because someone looked for their shoes in the dark and quietly slipped out of the house. It isn't enough for breakfast. Certainly not for a walk in daylight.

Women and men have both left. Both find their shoes in the dark remarkably fast. But a woman is more likely to see me as a person than a man is. That's why I mostly date women, I feel safer there.

There are two kinds. The first lack the courage. They might want more, but what would their friends say, their family, the people at the office. To the others, we're not people but a category. A fantasy you open when you're in the mood and close when you're done.

Before someone writes it under this article: yes, I like making people horny. I own that, I've never hidden it. Being desired isn't the problem. Only being desired is.

And then there are the chats. There we're desired, and if we're lucky, we find love there too. Because online you're read first and seen second. Because there it's about character, not category.

And when that turns into love, it isn't a substitute, and it isn't something that only feels like it. It's love. And it's more than the morning after ever gave.

And Yet

Anyone who's read this far might think I'm unhappy. I'm not.

Though I was, for a long time. I was severely depressed because I couldn't understand why I kept getting sadder. At some point the helplessness was so great that I wanted to do something final.

Then I learned a lot about myself. And since I've been open about it, my character has turned around. A withdrawn man who only hid and was lonely became a woman who is more open, warmer, happier. One who walks toward people instead of turning them away. One who believes in chances more than in the pointless. That strange feeling of having to act differently from who I am is gone. I'm simply myself.

The bill is high, that's true. But what you get for it is yourself. The woman in the care home says the same after forty years: she regrets nothing. That shouldn't be skimmed over.

What She Wishes For

She has one wish, and it isn't for herself.

She wishes that things get better for everyone who comes after her. That society truly takes trans people in. Not in the form of a gender-neutral toilet that gets planned into a new building and then considered done. But with a broad acceptance you can't put up a sign for.

How much that's needed is shown by a recent study from Switzerland: almost a third of queer people in care homes feel they have to keep an essential part of their own life quiet. And many older trans people see a doctor late or not at all, out of fear of being treated badly. These are the ones who were here before us. The ones who pried open the doors we walk through today.

What acceptance looks like isn't complicated at all. Someone who sits down next to her over coffee. Carers who say her name without that tiny pause first. Families who might even celebrate it. A society that asks how to address you instead of whispering. People who look instead of looking away in embarrassment. People who don't quietly pull the door shut behind them in the morning. And nobody trying to explain to me that there are only two genders.

On my next visit I'll take her hand again. She'll cry, and I won't let go.

And if you have trans people around you, in your family, at work, in your neighborhood: treat them with respect. If it's your father, your mother, maybe your child, then swallow your pride. You may feel betrayed. But that person has betrayed themselves their whole life until now. And after all, you loved them or liked them before, too. A little sensitivity is enough. It costs you almost nothing, and we've already paid enough.
