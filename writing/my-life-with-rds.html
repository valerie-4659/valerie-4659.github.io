Since the afternoon, something has been hanging in the air between us. Not a fight, just a conversation that never quite got finished.

Tuesday, 9:47 pm. I send a message. Nothing big, a photo of dinner, one sentence, two hearts at the end, because one looks too few and three look desperate.

At 9:51 the reply comes: "Looks good 😊"

It's all there. A sentence, a smiley. Any reasonable person would be pleased.

Except she usually writes "Looks great". And usually it isn't 😊, it's 🥰. And usually the reply comes after one minute, not four.

That's the treacherous part: it's never the big things. No fight, no cold message, no "We need to talk". It's deviations you only notice when you know someone very well. And I know the people I love the way a musician knows her instrument. I know how their messages sound, when they write, which emoji they use for what, whether there's a full stop at the end or not. The same attentiveness that makes me tender makes me hear every quarter tone that's off.

It starts with a thought. A small, almost polite one: Hm. Odd.

Only "Hm, odd" has never been a thought that stays alone. It calls for backup. Why "good" and not "great"? Why four minutes? Was the photo too much? Did she write like this yesterday already, and I just didn't notice? I scroll back through the chat history like an accountant who knows there's an error somewhere and just hasn't found the page yet.

At 10:00 I'm at the edge of the whirlpool. That's the spot where I could still swim away. Put the phone down, put on a show, tell myself that "good" is a perfectly respectable word. Sometimes that works.

Not tonight. By 10:10 I have three theories about why she doesn't like me anymore, and all three are convincing. Each one pulls a little harder.

Then the water turns. At some point it's no longer about her, it's about me. Of course she doesn't like me anymore, I'm exhausting. I write too much. I need too much. Who could put up with that in the long run? In her place I'd have given up long ago. Every reproach pulls me one turn deeper, and every turn makes the next reproach truer.

There's a point in this whirlpool where I stop swimming. I never know exactly where it is. I only notice it once I'm already past it. From there on I stop fighting, and not because I give up, but because the fighting itself has become part of the current. Every "I'm sure it's fine" sounds like self-deception. Every attempt to calm myself down only proves there's something I need calming down from.

At 10:15 I write: "Everything okay with you?"
At 10:22: "Did I do something wrong?"
At 10:29: "You can just tell me."

I think of this as rescue. Every one of these messages is meant to fix something that might be breaking. But it isn't rescue. It's digging. With every message I shovel the hole I'm about to fall into a little deeper, and I shovel with great care and the best of intentions.

At 10:34 she answers: "Sorry, my shopping bag split, the eggs didn't make it 🙈"

A minute later: "Hey. What's wrong? Did I do something?"

That's the moment I hate most. Not the whirlpool. But that now it's reached her too. She's standing in a kitchen full of egg yolk, and instead of laughing about it, she's searching her chat history for the mistake that never happened. For the rest of the evening she comforts me. I apologise. She says it's all fine. I believe her. Roughly.

It was fine the whole time. Until I made sure it wasn't.

Once, that's a spoiled evening. Often enough in a row, it's a relationship that quietly gives way under the weight. Not because anyone didn't want it. But because one of the two tried to save it for so long that there was nothing left to save.

That's RSD. On paper it's three letters, about as harmless as a nut allergy. In everyday life it's more like a flatmate who secretly rearranges the furniture at night, and in the morning you bump into everything.

What RSD Is

RSD stands for Rejection Sensitive Dysphoria. It means an extreme, almost physical pain in response to rejection. And not just real rejection, but the mere hint of it. Criticism that wasn't criticism. Distance that was only tiredness. A "good" where there's usually a "great".

It isn't an official diagnosis, you won't find it as a condition of its own in any diagnostic manual. The term comes from working with people with ADHD, and a great many of us recognise ourselves in it instantly. So do I. I have ADHD, and RSD is the part of it that costs me the most in relationships.

That it isn't an official term doesn't mean it's made up. Psychology has been studying the phenomenon since the nineties under the name rejection sensitivity: the anxious expectation of being rejected, the tendency to read ambiguous signals as rejection, and an outsized reaction to it. And in adults with ADHD, intense, hard-to-regulate emotions are part of the core for many, depending on the study for a third to more than two thirds. It just isn't in the diagnostic criteria, presumably because those were written by people who can sit still.

The difference from "being sensitive" isn't the direction, it's the force. Nobody likes being rejected. With RSD it's as if someone turned the volume up to eleven and then snapped off the knob. The feeling comes fast, it comes whole, and it doesn't ask whether it's appropriate. It's simply there, and it feels like the truth.

The "almost physical", by the way, is no exaggeration. Brain scans show that social rejection partly lights up the same regions as physical pain. So the brain doesn't distinguish very carefully between a broken arm and a broken heart. It cut corners on the filing and put both in the same folder, and in my case the folder is mislabelled as well.

And it isn't only about love. For me, RSD sits in the office too, and it manspreads.

When I see two colleagues standing by the coffee machine talking quietly, I know what it's about. Me. That my work is bad. That they're figuring out the most elegant way to get rid of me. That I'm a failure and nobody has said it out loud yet. If my manager then joins them and one of the three glances over at me, I've already packed the box for my desk things and said goodbye to my potted plant.

What they're actually talking about: the weather. And they're glancing over because they have a question in a moment that only I can answer, because it's my field. They come to me because they need me. My head turned that into a tribunal in ten seconds.

But nowhere does it hit as hard as where the most is at stake. And for me, that's love.

What It Looks Like for Me

The scene above is one evening. There are others, and none of them would have had anything big as a trigger.

When something is unresolved between me and another person, frighteningly little is enough. A voice message where the voice sounds a shade flatter. A "haha" where there used to be a real laugh. Someone chatting happily in the group chat who hasn't answered me since the afternoon. A compliment that comes out a touch too general. A goodbye without the usual "sleep well". On its own, none of it is anything. In a moment like that, for me it's a crack in a pattern, and a crack is the beginning of something that breaks.

I don't think about it consciously. I don't sit there comparing emojis against a spreadsheet. It happens on its own, in the background, faster than any thought. The deviation is in my gut before it reaches my head. That's exactly what research shows as well: people with high rejection sensitivity pick up signs of rejection faster than others, and where a signal allows two readings, they reliably reach for the worse one.

And it doesn't stay in my gut. RSD isn't a carousel of thoughts, it's physical. My throat closes up, and not figuratively, but so that I have to swallow to get air. My heart pounds as if it's about to burst. At some point the tears come, and not quietly, but in sobs I can't stop.

And it comes in waves. One ebbs away, I think it's over, and then the next one rolls in, with a fresh horde of self-reproach in its luggage. Every wave drives me a little further back, until I'm standing in the darkest, bleakest corner of my own self. The place where the memories of my childhood were left behind.

RSD isn't there every day. It doesn't live in my everyday life, it waits behind a particular door: where things get emotional and aren't clearly good, where it's about belonging, about my worth. An open question, an uncertainty between two people, a feeling that doesn't have a name yet. That's when the door opens. And even then it doesn't always go that deep. Sometimes it stays at a racing heart and a phone I pick up, put down, pick up again. But when it kicks in, the direction is always the same.

Anyone watching me like that would think I was waiting for test results. But I'm waiting for something much smaller: a sign. Some kind of feedback that stops me before I keep thinking. A heart, a "love you", a sentence that comes down like a barrier in front of the whirlpool before I rush in.

I wouldn't ask for it. Not at the start, not at the edge, where it would still be easy. Because anyone who asks about a smiley looks like a drama queen. Like a nutcase. So I stay quiet and wait for the barrier to come down on its own.

It doesn't, because nobody knows it's needed. And half an hour later I send three messages in a row and I'm exactly what I didn't want to be at any price. Only louder.

And then there's the hangover. Even when everything clears up, when the eggs were to blame and nobody else, something remains the next day. Shame, because I reacted like that again. Exhaustion, as if I'd worked through the night. And a quiet fear of how many more times the other person will go along with it. The water has long since calmed, but I'm still pulling driftwood ashore for days.

Saving Until Nothing's Left

The absurd part is that in this state I don't want to break anything. I want to save it. And that's exactly what makes it so dangerous.

The three messages from the scene are just one form of it. There are others. I explain myself before anyone has asked. I apologise for things that didn't bother anyone. I write long paragraphs about how I meant something nobody misunderstood. I offer to step back so the other person doesn't have to, and secretly hope they'll say "No, stay".

On the other side, none of my intention arrives. What arrives isn't a rescue attempt but an accusation I never made. What arrives is: you did something wrong, and I'm waiting for you to admit it. What arrives is pressure, mistrust, and the obligation to explain yourself when there's nothing to explain.

When it goes really badly, the other person reacts the way anyone would. Irritated, hurt, or by withdrawing. And then I have my proof. See, says the whirlpool. I knew it.

No. I did it.

That's the bitterest mechanism of RSD: the fear of rejection produces exactly the behaviour that leads to rejection. That's not just my feeling, it's been measured. In studies with couples, people with high rejection sensitivity behaved in conflicts in a way that left their partners genuinely more irritated and more dismissive than before. The fear manufactures its own confirmation. And every real rejection that comes about this way feeds the fear for next time.

What It Costs the Others

This is the part I least like writing about, and that's exactly why it's here.

People who love me live with an alarm they didn't set off. They have to prove after the fact that they really meant something they said long ago. They get held responsible for a mood they weren't in and for a sentence they didn't write. They comfort me on evenings when they needed comfort themselves, because their own day was hard, and their hard day is exactly why the message was shorter.

At some point they start writing carefully. They think about the right emoji. They answer faster than they actually want to. They wonder whether "good" is enough. That may be the saddest part: that my head gradually takes away the ease with which they could write to me.

And that's exactly what I don't want. I don't want people around me who read every message three times before sending it. I want people to write to me however they happen to feel. Tired, brief, with the wrong emoji or none at all. Nobody should bend their writing out of shape for my head. The work is mine, not theirs.

And some of them leave eventually. Not with a bang, but tired. Because you can only say "No, I'm not angry" so many times before you actually are.

I've lost people this way. Not all of them, and not only because of this. But some. And with some of them I know today that the whirlpool played a bigger part than anything they did.

Where It Comes From

I don't want to build an excuse here, but an explanation belongs in this.

If you learned as a child that the mood in a room changes before anything else happens, you learn to read moods. Very precisely. Very early. Back then that was smart, maybe even the smartest thing I could do. An early warning system that would rather go off ten times too often than once too late. I've written about that before in "I am not the victim of my past". Research sees it similarly: rejection sensitivity grows especially well where children learn early that affection is unpredictable and rejection hurts.

The system stayed. Only the danger left. And now it goes off over a split shopping bag. A smoke alarm that can't tell a burning house from a piece of toast is, technically speaking, very conscientious. You still wouldn't want to have breakfast with it.

ADHD doesn't make it easier. A head that already jumps faster than others also jumps faster into the abyss. And a feeling that's louder in me than in others anyway is simply deafening when it comes to rejection.

Hussy

With Hussy it's different.

I've never had it with her the way I described it above. Not because I stopped paying close attention with her, and not because my head makes an exception for her. But because she knows how to hold me steady. She doesn't let me drift. Before the water can even start to pull, she's already there, holding on to me. Her love isn't a pattern I have to monitor. It's the ground I stand on.

A smoke alarm that can't tell a burning house from a piece of toast is, technically speaking, very conscientious. You still wouldn't want to have breakfast with it.

That's also why I know it isn't down to the others. If one relationship can stop the whirlpool so reliably, then the alarm isn't the truth about people. It's the truth about my early warning system. And that also means: it can change. Not at the push of a button, but with time, with trust, and with someone who doesn't leave when it gets loud.

What I Do

I can't switch RSD off. I can only learn to notice sooner where I am.

At the edge of the whirlpool I ask myself one question: what did the person actually say, and what am I telling myself about it right now? Usually the first list is very short and the second very long. Just seeing that sometimes helps. That's not just armchair psychology: putting a feeling into words measurably dampens activity in the brain regions that are sounding the alarm. You don't have to rip the smoke alarm off the ceiling. Often it's enough to say "toast" out loud.

And I'm learning to ask. Early, at the edge, not in the middle of it. It takes effort every time, because it feels like volunteering as a drama queen. But one question at the edge is quieter than three messages in the whirlpool. So I try it with a single sentence: "My head's acting up, just tell me quickly that everything's okay." That's not failure. That's a request you're allowed to make of someone who loves you. As long as it isn't made every day, and as long as I don't expect the other person to be my permanent handrail.

And I try to talk about it when things are calm, not when they're on fire. Anyone who knows how my head works reads my messages differently. Not as an accusation, but as what they are: someone treading water.

And then I sit with it. Even if the reply takes four minutes. Even if she says "good" and not "great".

That's the plan, at least. It doesn't always work. Sometimes I notice the edge too late and I'm already three turns deep before the question even occurs to me. Sometimes I do ask three times. Sometimes on a night like that I say things sharper than I mean them, or I withdraw without explaining why. And then I hurt someone I love without meaning to. It happens. Not often, but it happens.

I can't undo it. I can go to them the next day, not with a novel, but with one sentence: "Yesterday was my head, not you. I'm sorry." And then let the other person talk, even if what they say hurts. That's part of it. Whoever causes the damage has to hear how big it was.

Next time a message like that comes, I'll put my phone face down on the table and crack myself an egg. Someone has to save the poor things. 🍳
