On the one-way streets of love — and why affection that hides isn't affection at all

There's a pattern running through the lives of many trans people. It isn't coincidence, it isn't an isolated case, and it isn't oversensitivity. It has a name in the research literature, it's measurable, and it makes people ill. It's time we talked about it.

The Pattern

It almost always starts the same way. A message — late at night, private, somewhere no one else is reading. The words are warm, sometimes passionate. "I like you." "You're special." "I have feelings for you." And you believe it. Why wouldn't you? Words carry weight when you've learned that affection isn't something you can take for granted.

But then something strange happens: those words exist only in the dark. In public you're an acquaintance. A friend, maybe. But never the person someone wants to be seen with. Never the one they introduce to their friends, the one they stand up for, the one they claim when somebody asks.

You don't notice right away. You explain it away — she's shy, he needs time, it's complicated. But at some point you count the times you were publicly meant, and the number is: zero. And then you understand.

Two Faces of the Same Coin

This pattern has two faces, and they resemble each other disturbingly closely.

The first face belongs to some women who identify as lesbian and who seek intimacy with a trans woman — but only behind closed doors. In public, the "real" partner is a cis woman. The trans woman doesn't exist there. She's what you use and then put away. Feelings get shared openly with the girlfriend who fits the picture — while the trans woman stays a secret.

The second face belongs to some heterosexual cis men — the so-called "chasers." They desire trans women, often with an intensity that's flattering at first. But it becomes clear quickly: it isn't about the person. It's about the body. About the thrill of the supposedly forbidden. About a kink you indulge at night and deny in the morning. You swipe right at two in the morning, but you'd never introduce her to your friends.

The word "some" matters here. Not every lesbian woman, not every cis man behaves this way. There are people who stand by their partner openly and without drama. But the pattern is common enough that research can describe it, measure it, and name it.

What the Science Says

Fetishization Isn't Imagined

In 2021, Annalisa Anzani, Louis Lindley, Giacomo Tognasso, M. Paz Galupo, and Antonio Prunas published a study in Archives of Sexual Behavior whose title is a direct quote from a participant: "Being Talked to Like I Was a Sex Toy, Like Being Transgender Was Simply for the Enjoyment of Someone Else."

In it, 142 trans and non-binary people described their experiences with fetishization. The researchers identified three broad themes: the context in which fetishization occurs, negative experiences of it — and, to be honest about the findings, positive or ambiguous ones too. Not every form of sexual attention is a violation; some participants experienced desire as something wanted.

But the majority described something else: being reduced to a body, to genitals, to a category. Not seen as a person, but as an object of curiosity. The authors frame this theoretically as the intersection of sexual objectification and minority stress — as a form of dehumanization.

The difference between desire and fetishization lies exactly here: desire is directed at a person who happens to be trans. Fetishization is directed at the transness, and the person is incidental.

Objectification Gets Under the Skin

In 2020, Comiskey, Parent, and Tebbe examined in Psychology of Women Quarterly how objectification affects trans women — with 173 participants and a statistical model.

Their finding: those who internalize cultural appearance standards monitor their own bodies more closely. That constant self-surveillance leads to body shame. And in their model, body shame was measurably linked to disordered eating and the intention to obtain silicone injections — to real, sometimes dangerous interventions on one's own body.

That's the quiet bill someone eventually pays: when a person is treated only as a body for long enough, they eventually start seeing themselves that way.

Hiding Damages Relationships — Demonstrably

In June 2025, a team led by Elliot Schuler and Erin Wilson published a study in Frontiers in Global Women's Health involving 89 trans women in committed partnerships.

One result stands out: women who felt they had to hide their relationship reported satisfaction with closeness and intimacy dramatically less often. A lack of family acceptance had a similar effect. The sample is small and the study is a snapshot in time — but the association is clear: the hiding itself damages the relationship.

Kristi Gamarel and colleagues had already shown this in 2019 in the Journal of Social and Personal Relationships, studying 191 couples — trans women with cis male partners. The more frequently they experienced interpersonal stigma, the higher their psychological distress. And that held true for both partners. Notably: a high degree of commitment buffered this effect for the trans women — but not for the men.

Translated: someone who is ashamed of their partner doesn't only harm her. He harms himself. And someone who commits to her openly protects her measurably.

Secrets Come at a Price

That secrecy hollows out relationships isn't specific to trans partnerships. Justin Lehmiller, then at Colorado State University, showed in 2009 in the Personality and Social Psychology Bulletin: the more secret a relationship, the lower the commitment, the lower the self-esteem, the more physical symptoms reported.

The mechanism behind it: secrecy reduces psychological closeness between partners and generates negative emotional states — nervousness, fear. So the secrecy isn't merely a symptom. It is itself the damage.

Minority Stress — The Invisible Constant Load

In 2003, Ilan Meyer described the minority stress model in Psychological Bulletin — originally for lesbian, gay, and bisexual people; it was extended to trans populations later, notably by Hendricks and Testa (2012). The core idea: chronic stressors such as discrimination, anticipated rejection, and the need to conceal parts of one's identity have direct effects on mental health.

Concealment is explicitly one of those stressors. A daily diary study by Scheer and colleagues, published in 2025 in the Journal of Counseling Psychology, followed 57 people over 14 days. The result: on roughly 36 percent of all days, participants concealed their identity — and days with more experiences of discrimination were days with more concealment.

And here the circle closes: someone who is hidden by their partner doesn't carry that stress only out in the world. They carry it into their most intimate relationship. The place that should offer shelter becomes a source of stigma itself.

The Drawer

There's an image that captures how this feels: the drawer.

You become the object lying inside it. It gets opened when you're needed — at night, when the loneliness presses in, when curiosity calls, when the kink speaks up. You get used. Then the drawer closes and the person returns to their "normal" life. The drawer doesn't get mentioned. The drawer doesn't exist.

And if the person inside complains? If they say, "I don't want to lie in a drawer"? Then they're the one who's overreacting. Who wants too much. Who doesn't understand how "complicated" it all is.

But it isn't complicated. It's cowardly.

What Love Is Not

Love is not: having feelings that are only allowed to exist in a dark corner.

Love is not: desiring a person while being ashamed of that desire.

Love is not an instrument you use to open doors — and set down again once you've walked through.

Love is not something that has to wait because someone currently lacks the energy or the headspace for it. Love is mutual, something both people work at — not just the one side waiting while the other reaches out whenever it suits them.

Someone who loves a trans person but can't show it perhaps only loves the idea of them. The version that fits inside their secret. The version no one is allowed to see. But that's a composite sketch, not a person.

What Love Is

Love is visible. Not always loud, not always dramatic — but visible. It's the hand you hold in public. It's the name you say out loud when someone asks. It's the matter-of-factness of saying: "Yes, she's with me." Anyone who can't do that shouldn't be talking about love.

Love is not a one-way street. It doesn't flow in one direction only — from patience toward convenience, from an open heart into a closed drawer.

And love doesn't live only in a dark corner where no one is looking.

Behind Every Trans Person Stands a Human Being

A person who had to climb over more obstacles on their path than most people can imagine. Rejection, violence, exclusion, the daily fight for recognition — often against their own family, often against institutions, sometimes against everyone around them. That path deserves respect, not reduction to a fantasy.

Trans people are not a porn category. Not the ultimate kink. Not the MILF of the 2000s. Not a box on a bucket list you tick and forget.

To everyone out there who desires trans people: if you love them, then stand by it — with everything that comes with it. Not perfectly. Not every day. But once. One single damn time in public.

Anything else is cherry-picking at no cost: you take the closeness, the intimacy, the desire — but not the part that actually costs something. Not the acknowledgment. Not the willingness to withstand other people's stares. If you only want the pleasant half, be honest enough to call it what it is. That isn't love — it's a service nobody paid for.

Because no human being is a trophy you polish in secret. No human being is an object you keep in a drawer.

Be clear about what you're getting into. Not "a trans." A human being. With a history, with scars, with strength, with feelings. And that's how this person should be treated — not as a category, not as a body, not as a secret.

Because no human being is a trophy you polish in secret. No human being is an object you keep in a drawer.

Trans people are people. And they deserve love that doesn't have to hide.
