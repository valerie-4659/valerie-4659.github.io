Red flags for people who feel with their heart first

The moment you explain away

It's just after ten. The reply is shorter than usual, no heart, no "sleep well", just "ok, talk tomorrow". You read it three times, as if a heart might write itself in on the third read.

Then the familiar part kicks in. You're too sensitive. They probably had a long day. You're making a mountain out of a molehill, and then a small mountain range out of the mountain. In the end you write "Sleep well ❤️" and pretend nothing happened.

Most of the time, nothing has. And sometimes it's the first wisp of smoke.

I have RSD, and my head is a gifted storyteller that can build a complete breakup out of three dots in a chat window, with dialogue and rainy weather. I learned to distrust it. Only later did I learn that it doesn't always lie.

This text is for people who feel with their heart first and do the maths afterwards. Who have learned to apologise for their instincts. And who love online, where there is only text, and with it plenty of room for both: real warning signs and ghosts.

The smoke detector

Sensitive people have a smoke detector that's set more sensitively than most. It goes off when the toast is just a little too dark. That's exhausting, and at some point you're tempted to simply take the battery out. Please don't.

A sensitive smoke detector isn't broken. You just have to get up and check every time. Usually it's the toast. But if you take the battery out, you only notice the real fire when the curtains are ablaze.

For me, checking means two questions. The first: what do I see, rather than what do I feel? "I'm being avoided" is a feeling. "My question from Tuesday still hasn't been answered" is an observation. The second: a moment or a pattern? One short reply is a moment. Short replies for three weeks, while the same person bubbles over like a fountain everywhere else, are a pattern.

Between delicate flower and undertow

Anyone who knows RSD grows careful over time, above all with themselves. You deliberately give the inner drama less room. That's hard-won. But it has a flip side: you don't want to be the panicky delicate flower who can hear a pin drop in a thunderstorm, so you stay silent even when something really has fallen.

I never said out loud that I was afraid of becoming the third wheel when new connections formed around me that I wasn't part of. I thought the sentence was RSD and swallowed it. It wasn't RSD. It was an observation.

That's one way to fail at RSD: you push the real signals down along with everything else. The other is the one we fear, and rightly so. When control slips, an undertow forms. Every short reply becomes evidence, you ask questions that are really accusations, and you tug at the very relationship you want to hold on to. That undertow hurts people who have done nothing wrong.

The path in between is narrow: put the observation calmly on the table and keep the drama to yourself. "I'm feeling a bit like the third wheel right now, I know my head is making it louder, but I wanted to tell you anyway" isn't being a delicate flower. It's the opposite of the undertow. It's honesty with the seatbelt on.

Where it really burns

None of these signs is proof on its own. But when several come together, or one turns into a pattern, it's no longer just the toast.

Big words with little weight. Online, everything moves faster, and sometimes the word "soulmate" comes up after just two weeks. That alone isn't the problem. I believed in soulmates, and I still believe it's something very special. Big words are beautiful when you also give them big meaning. The warning sign is when the same word weighs differently on each side: a promise for one person, a nice feeling on a nice evening for the other. Usually you only notice when you try to lean on it. That's why it's worth asking early about the big words: what does this mean to you?

Words and actions drift apart. Feeling love through text is hard, but it's possible. It shows in how you treat each other. Does it only happen in the DMs, or also out in the open, where others can see it? Does the other person only react to what you send, or does something come of its own accord: a question about how that difficult appointment went, a small surprise, a thought of you that nobody prompted? Online, these small gestures are the actions. When the words grow and the gestures shrink, believe the gestures. And if you have doubts: they rarely come from nowhere. That's your gut, and it often reads between the lines before your head has caught up.

"Nothing will change." A sentence that changes the moment it's spoken, like "I'll be right there" or "This won't hurt a bit". It becomes a warning sign when it's meant to end a conversation rather than start one.

Rumours without names. "People are talking about you, but I can't tell you who." That gives you nothing you could clear up, only distrust of the people around you. Someone who means well tells you what it's about, or says nothing. A secret told by halves isn't care. It's a leash.

There's always something. Crises are real, that's not the point. The point is the timing: when they always arrive just as you need something, and the exhaustion only applies to you, while the same person happily posts away everywhere else. A reason that only ever applies to you isn't a reason. It's a ranking.

Care that sounds like goodbye. "Think of yourself too." "Don't make me so big." "Look around for other people, I want you to be happy." Imagine that in a monogamous relationship: "Darling, please go and find yourself an affair." Nobody says that. Even in poly, where other connections naturally belong, there's a difference between someone being glad about your happiness and someone nudging you towards the door. Sure, you shouldn't bet everything on one single person. But love online is often just a few hours a day, and of course you want to make the most of them. When these sentences pile up while closeness is fading, an exit is often being prepared, one with a clear conscience. I've heard sentences like these more than once, shortly before an ending.

Decisions are made without you. Openness was promised, but what concerns you gets discussed elsewhere and served to you finished. Often it starts exactly where a new person comes in. Nobody owns another person, not in poly either, especially not in poly. But anyone who enters a relationship takes on responsibility, and it's greatest when old and new love are going to cross paths. The fair thing is to say it beforehand: "I've fallen in love with someone, and I'd like to have that love alongside ours, because it matters to me too." Then you have the choice whether that works for you, or whether you'd rather leave before it hurts. Just doing it and saying "I'm a free person" afterwards is unfair. Freedom doesn't mean the others carry the consequences alone. And anyone who doesn't ask you about decisions like that usually won't ask you about the really big one either. That one comes on an ordinary evening, without warning.

Where we hold the door open ourselves

Not every danger comes from outside. Some patterns we bring along ourselves, neatly gift-wrapped.

Wanting to be uncomplicated. Anyone who has often been "too much" eventually tries to take up as little room as possible. But uncomplicated also means: easy to overlook. And when things get tight, the quietest person is the one who can be let go most quietly.

Harmony at any price. Swallow one small thing, then another. From the outside it looks like harmony; from the inside it's silence. And silence builds up until it either explodes or simply leaves.

The hunger to be seen. Anyone who hasn't felt seen for a long time reacts strongly to someone who suddenly sees them intensely. That's nothing to be ashamed of. But that's exactly where big words slip in most easily. A full stomach doesn't buy every loaf.

Effort out of fear. When a new person came into my circle and our conversations became more routine, my head started asking questions. What does this person have that I don't? Have I become boring? Is something missing in bed? So I fought, not against the other person, but against my fear. Getting up in the middle of the night, anticipating every wish, always being there. Only: the harder you try out of fear, the smaller you make yourself. Love you have to earn anew every day isn't a safe place. What you give should be a gift, not an entry ticket.

Not looking at the constellation. Love doesn't shrink when it's shared; time and attention do. Look honestly every now and then: where do several threads come together, and where does one hang on its own? That's not a question of blame. It's a question of what's load-bearing.

What can help

I'm not writing this as someone who got it right. By now, the third relationship has ended that I didn't end myself, and every time I was quieter and smaller by the end than I should have been. These are the things I wish, looking back, I had known sooner.

Ask early where you stand. When something shifts, not half a year later. An uncomfortable answer today is cheaper than a surprising one in three months.

Two columns. On the left, what you feel. On the right, what you see. The left one is almost always longer. But sometimes there's something on the right that can't be argued away.

Visible, not loud. "I'm missing something right now" isn't drama. "That hurt me" isn't an attack. You can stay quiet. You shouldn't become invisible.

Don't shrink to stay. Anyone who makes themselves fit so there's no trouble disappears piece by piece from their own relationship. And whoever can no longer be seen won't be missed when things get tight.

An outside view. Someone who is neither in your head nor in the relationship, and who tells you what your right-hand column looks like from outside.

Know what you need. Most of us know exactly what the other person needs, and surprisingly little about ourselves. Without that yardstick, all you measure is whether you're giving enough.

Leaving without proof. You don't have to prove anyone is playing games before you step back. "This isn't enough for me" is a complete sentence.

The next time it beeps

You're not broken and you're not too much. You just hear sooner what others only hear when it's already burning. And most short replies really are just tired evenings. This text isn't meant to make you more suspicious, but more secure.

For a long time my fear of loss was so great that I avoided human closeness almost entirely. If you don't let anyone near you, you can't lose anyone. The equation wasn't wrong, just imprecise. Yes, much of it will end. But what matters is the time in between.

So, if tonight the reply is shorter than usual and something in your chest starts to beep: don't take the battery out. Get up. Check what you see, not just what you feel.

And then decide whether to fetch the butter or the fire extinguisher. 🦋
