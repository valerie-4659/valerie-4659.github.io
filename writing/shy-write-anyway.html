The Thumb Over the Button

Your thumb hovers over the send button. Above it sits a message you've been building for a quarter of an hour now. It's not a long message. It's three sentences. But they're three sentences that have already been through five drafts, one with an emoji, one without, one with a joke you deleted straight away, because it was funny in your head and on the screen suddenly looked like a cry for help.

The message is for someone you admire. Maybe this person creates images that make you forget to breathe for a moment, every single time. Maybe they write texts that explain a thought you've been carrying around for years. Maybe it's simply someone who always says exactly the right thing in the comments, so casually, as if the right thing were just lying around out there on the street and all you had to do was pick it up.

And all you want to say is: I liked that. Nothing more.

You don't press send. You delete. You leave a heart under the post ❤️, because a heart can't do anything wrong. A heart has never embarrassed anyone. Then you put the phone down, a little relieved and a little disappointed in yourself, and that's a mixture that tastes roughly like cold coffee - you drink it anyway, but you're not proud of it.

If that sounds familiar: Hello. Lovely to have you here. You're in good company, and a very large one at that. Most of us write far more messages than we ever send. Somewhere there must be a graveyard for unsent messages, and it's probably bigger than the internet itself.

This text is about shyness. The everyday kind and, a little later, the heart-pounding kind too. It's about feeling small next to someone who seems big. About feeling intimidated. About people we put on a pedestal so high we have to tip our heads back just to see them - and about why it's so hard to chat with someone up there.

And in the end, I promise, it's about how to send that message after all.

The Committee in Your Head

Before a message is allowed to leave your phone, it has to pass through an authority. Everyone has one. It meets inside your head, it has no office hours, and it consists of a committee that never agrees on anything.

There's the member who asks: Does this sound too excited? Does it come across as pushy? The second asks: Does it sound too cold? Will they think I don't mean it? The third has a question about a comma. The fourth reminds you that three years ago, when the waiter said "Enjoy your meal", you answered "You too", and that this incident has not yet officially been closed.

And the fifth member says nothing at all. It just sits there and looks at you. That's the worst one.

The committee doesn't mean any harm. Honestly, it doesn't. It wants to protect you. Its job is to prevent embarrassing moments, and since the most reliable way to prevent an embarrassing moment is to do nothing at all, the committee is very pleased with its record. Zero embarrassments this year. Unfortunately, also zero conversations.

The crazy part: People who deliberate this long usually don't have too little to say, they have too much respect for their own words. Shy people know that words carry weight. They know a sentence can land wrong. That's actually a good quality. It's just set a little wrong, like a radiator running on full in the middle of summer.

Psychology has a lovely name for this: the spotlight effect. In a well-known experiment, students had to walk into a room full of people wearing a rather embarrassing T-shirt. Afterwards, they were asked to estimate how many people had noticed the shirt. They guessed about half. In reality, it was roughly a quarter. We believe a spotlight follows us through life. In truth, it's more like a torch with a weak battery, and most people are far too busy shaking their own.

The person you want to write to won't read your message under a magnifying glass. They'll read it, maybe smile, and then carry on with their day, which almost certainly also contains laundry and an unpaid bill. The committee can take a break. It has earned one.

The Pedestal

Now to the real problem. Because the committee works especially thoroughly when a message is meant to go upwards.

We humans have a habit of putting others on pedestals. It happens very quietly. Someone does something we admire - beautiful images, clever texts, lots of followers, a way of moving through life as if they'd never once pushed a glass door marked "Pull". And whoosh, this person is standing half a meter higher than us. Then a meter. And eventually so high that from below, all you can see are the soles of their shoes.

Follower counts are especially treacherous here. They work like stilts. A number with lots of zeros says nothing about whether someone is kind, whether they listen, or whether they happen to be lonely right now. But it lifts a person up, and we automatically look up.

The pedestal has a catch, and it's built in: When you put someone higher, you put yourself lower. That's pure geometry. Suddenly every message feels like a petition to the royal court. You're no longer thinking about what you want to say, but whether you're allowed to say anything at all. Whether it's important enough. Whether you're important enough.

And you can't make small talk with someone standing that high up. Try asking a statue how its weekend was. It won't answer. Not because it's rude, but because it's made of marble, and you're the one who made it out of marble.

I know this because sometimes I'm on the other side. There are people who look at me rather intimidated. I notice it in the way they write, when they write at all: very carefully, very politely, with an apology up front, as if they were taking their shoes off before stepping into my inbox. Others don't write at all. They leave hearts, for months, faithful as a church clock, and I wonder who's actually behind them.

Why that is, I can only guess. Maybe they make me more important than I am. Maybe they don't know what to say. Maybe they think they're somehow worse, less interesting, less something. Maybe it's all of the above. The committee does meet with full attendance, after all.

And every time, it makes me a little sad. Not for me, but for the conversation that never happens.

Because here's the little secret about pedestals that rarely makes it down to the ground: It's drafty up there. And rather lonely. You stand there, get looked at, get hearts, and at some point you notice that everyone keeps a respectful distance, as if you were a museum piece behind a velvet rope. Nobody asks how your day was. Nobody tells you about their dog. You're admired, but you're never visited.

So the pedestal protects no one. It just makes two people lonelier who could have had a wonderful conversation.

We All Cook with Water

So let me take the pedestal apart a little. Board by board.

I'm not perfect. That sounds like something you say out of modesty, but I mean it quite practically. I forget messages. I read something, I'm delighted, I plan to reply, and then my head is already three thoughts further and the reply lies there like an umbrella left behind on a train. I write sentences I later find embarrassing. I type messages and delete them again, just like you. And yes, I'm sometimes shy too. There are people I spend ages wondering whether to get in touch with, because for me they're standing on a pedestal. My committee just meets on different days.

That's not the exception. That's the norm. Every person you admire has a fridge with something forgotten way at the back. Everyone has said "You too" to a waiter. Everyone has days when they feel small, look at other people's profiles and think: They've got it all so together.

Because we always compare our insides to other people's outsides. Our mess to their tidy photos. Our doubts to their finished texts. That's about as fair as comparing your own kitchen after cooking to a picture in a furniture catalogue. Nobody cooked in the catalogue either. Nobody has ever cooked there.

There are two findings from research that I find very comforting.

The first is called the pratfall effect. Back in the sixties, researchers found that competent people come across as more likeable when they have a small mishap. In the experiment, it was a spilled cup of coffee. Perfection creates distance. A stain on the shirt creates closeness. Which also means: The people on your pedestal would probably be far more approachable with a stain. And most of them have one, you just can't see it from below.

The second is called the liking gap. Researchers had strangers talk to each other and then asked them how much they thought the other person had liked them. Almost everyone underestimated it, and by quite a lot. We walk out of conversations thinking "Oh god, that was weird", while the other person is thinking "Oh, that was nice". Two people who like each other and are both convinced they made fools of themselves. If that's not material for a comedy, I don't know what is.

So: You're probably far more pleasant than your committee tells you. And the people up on the pedestal are probably far more ordinary than you think. In German we say everyone cooks with water - nobody has a secret magic ingredient. Some of us also regularly let it boil over.

Small Talk for People Who Hate Small Talk

Right. Let's assume the pedestal has been taken down, the statue is a person again, and now you really want to write something. But what, exactly?

Small talk has a bad reputation. Many shy people think of it as an art you either have or you don't, like juggling or rolling your tongue. But small talk is just a handshake made of words. Nobody expects a handshake to be profound. It's only meant to say: I'm here, I'm friendly, I don't bite.

A few things have worked for me, from both sides of the inbox.

One detail beats the whole. "Your images are amazing" is sweet, but the only possible reply is "Thank you!", and then the conversation is over before it's begun. "The light on the windowsill in your last image reminded me of my grandma's kitchen" is a sentence people want to answer. It shows you really looked. And it tells them something about you along the way.

A question beats a compliment. Praise is lovely, but it's an endpoint. A question is a door. "How long did that take you?" "Where did the idea come from?" "Is the cat real?" People love talking about the things they've made, and even more about their cats.

Everyday life is allowed. Many people think you can only talk to someone who does something great about the great thing. As if they had no life outside their work. But they do. If someone posts that it's raining and they're out of coffee, you're allowed to reply. "No coffee? That's an emergency" is a perfectly legitimate way to start a conversation, no matter how many people follow them. Especially then, actually. It might be the first time in weeks that someone asks about the person rather than the product.

The first sentence doesn't have to be clever. This is important, so I'll say it twice: The first sentence doesn't have to be clever. Not funny, not original, not perfectly phrased. "That made me laugh" is completely enough. The cleverness comes later, usually all by itself, once you've warmed up a little. No conversation has to start at its peak. Even operas begin with tuning up.

Give something of yourself. A message made only of admiration is a bit like a gift without a sender. Lovely, but you don't know whom to thank. Half a sentence about you helps them see you. "I generate a bit myself, but hands drive me to despair every single time." Now they've got a thread to pull on.

You're allowed to admit you're nervous. Sounds strange, but it works surprisingly well. "I've wanted to write to you for ages and didn't dare" is one of the most disarming sentences there is. It takes the pressure off immediately, because you've said the awkward part out loud yourself before anyone else could notice it. And I promise you: Most people don't find that embarrassing. They find it sweet. Probably also because they're a little nervous themselves and recognize themselves in it with relief. 🌸

Public before private. Not every first encounter has to happen in the inbox. A comment under a post is the smaller step, a kind of front garden. If you've shown up there once or twice in a friendly way, your first private message isn't going to a stranger anymore, but to someone who's seen your name before. That's the difference between a cold shower and a lukewarm one, and lukewarm is exactly the right temperature for shy people.

From the other side of the inbox, it looks exactly the same, by the way. When someone comments on my posts or shares something, I often take a curious look at their profile. I remember good comments. And when a message eventually arrives, it's not from a stranger, but from someone I already know a little. Even better, I've got a hook right away: "You really liked that one image the other day, your comment on it was so sweet. Did you have something specific in mind when you saw it?" So yesterday's comment isn't just a front garden. It's a garden gate that opens from both sides.

Leave the conversation open. Many conversations don't die because nobody likes each other, but because someone put a full stop. "Haha, yes" is a dead end. "Haha, yes - and how did it turn out?" is a door. A conversation needs a little hook at the end of every message, where the other person can hang their coat.

More than "Fine, thanks". When someone asks how you are, "Fine, thanks" is the polite way of ending the conversation before it's started. A handshake without a hand. A real little detail plus a question back is plenty: "Tired, but the coffee is fighting bravely. And you?" Suddenly you both know a bit more about each other, and nobody had to turn their soul inside out.

Conversations are allowed to be serial novels. Nothing has to be finished in one evening. Online, a conversation can last for days, with breaks in between where both of you live, sleep and hang up laundry. And when you write the next day "I kept thinking about what you said yesterday", that's one of the loveliest sentences there is. It says: You stayed on my mind.

You're allowed to end a conversation, too. Many people don't dare to start because they're afraid of getting stuck, forever, in a conversation about houseplants. "I have to go, this was lovely" is a complete sentence. Nobody is offended. On the contrary: People who know how to stop get written to again.

Make the goal smaller. The goal isn't a big friendship. The goal isn't for them to think you're amazing either. The goal is one kind message. Sent means won, everything after that is a bonus.

And if nothing at all comes to mind, "Hey, I've been reading your stuff for a while and just wanted to say hello" is still a hundred times better than the prettiest heart. A heart is a wave across the street. A hello is crossing over.

What Helps When Your Heart Races

That leaves the nerves. They don't go away just because you've read an article about them, that would be far too easy. But you can tame them a little.

Clumsy beats silent. Silence is almost always misread: as disinterest, as coldness, sometimes even as arrogance. A clumsy sentence, on the other hand, is read as what it is, namely interest on wobbly knees. Nobody has ever received a kind message and complained about the word order. Well, almost nobody.

A heart is a wave across the street. A hello is crossing over.

Rename it. Nervousness and excitement feel almost the same in the body. Pounding heart, damp hands, the feeling that your stomach is falling down a small flight of stairs. In one study, people were asked to say either "I am calm" or "I am excited" before a nerve-racking task. The excited ones did significantly better. Calming down doesn't work well when your heart is already racing. Reframing it does. Your heart isn't pounding out of fear. It just got the wrong ticket.

Be curious instead of watchful. If you keep thinking "How do I come across right now?" while writing, you're guarding yourself like a security service, and that makes you more nervous. If you ask yourself instead "What is this person about to tell me?", you forget yourself a little. That's exactly what helps. Curiosity is the best sedative you can get without a prescription.

Collect evidence. Take a screenshot of every kind reply and make a folder for them. It'll grow, faster than you think. On days when the committee is in session again, claiming you're only bothering everyone, put the folder on the table. Committees love files. There's very little they can say against files.

And it gets easier. The tenth message is easier than the first, the fiftieth is almost routine. Nerves wear off if you ignore them often enough. The committee gets lazy over time. Eventually it falls asleep during meetings, and you just write.

When Your Heart Joins In

And then there's the extra-spicy version. The one where not only the committee is in session, but your heart is knocking on the door too, wanting a say.

You don't just find someone great, you find them attractive. Maybe even quite. And suddenly a harmless message turns into a minefield. Is "You look amazing" too direct? Is a winking emoji already a proposal? Will they think I only want one thing? And do I maybe want that one thing a little? At this point, the committee gets reinforcements, namely a member who keeps blushing.

The pedestal grows twice as fast here. Beautiful people seem unreachable. Beautiful people who are also kind seem like a glitch in the matrix. And the more desirable someone seems, the more certain we are that there's already a queue up there and we'd have to join the back, with a ticket number and a folding chair.

Here's the good news, and I know it from my own inbox: Attractive people get surprisingly few nice messages. Lots of messages, sure. But most of them are crude, demanding, or consist of a single word and a photo nobody ordered. A message that is friendly, a little playful and asks for nothing stands out in an inbox like that like a flower in a rubbish bin.

Flirting isn't a promise or a contract. It's a light tap on the shoulder: I see you, do you see me too? A compliment about something specific isn't overstepping. "That color looks outrageously good on you" says more and pushes less than ten flame emojis. And if you pay attention to what comes back, you can hardly go wrong. If lightness comes back, it can stay light and keep going. If only a polite thank you comes back, that's an answer too, and not a bad one.

What shyness fears most here isn't even the no. It's looking ridiculous. As if you'd presumed to play in a league you don't belong in. But there are no leagues. There are only people who like each other or don't, and you never know beforehand.

What comes after, when flirting turns into more and at some point you'd have to say what you feel, is another story. For the beginning, being friendly and a little brave is enough. Nobody asks for more. Except maybe the committee, but nobody's asking the committee.

When No Reply Comes

Now for the chapter the committee fears most. You wrote. You sent it. And then... nothing.

I won't sugarcoat it. That feels rotten. Anyone sensitive to rejection knows how quickly a missing reply turns into a whole story, with chapters, cross-references and a very sad ending. I know that well. My head is extremely industrious in that department.

But a missing reply is almost never a verdict. Most of the time it's a lack of time. People with lots of messages lose track. They read something on the go, they're happy about it, they want to reply properly later, and that "later" is a place very few messages ever return from. Sometimes your message is simply in the wrong folder, between advertising and someone who urgently wants to sell you cryptocurrency.

I've found messages weeks later myself and been terribly ashamed. The people who wrote them probably thought in the meantime that I wanted nothing to do with them. When really, I just had a brain that likes to misplace things.

And even if the reply turns out short: A "Thank you!" with a heart isn't a rejection. It's a person who had a little time between dinner and laundry and gave it to you. That counts.

What you don't need to conclude: that you're never allowed to try again. One silent message is no proof that you're bothering anyone. It's a message. Nothing more. With the next post, you're allowed to say something again, without referring back, without "I know you didn't reply, but...". Just fresh. Conversations rarely grow from a single message. They grow from lots of small ones that eventually find each other, like mushrooms after rain.

Just Being There for a While

And now something just as important as everything before it: You don't have to.

Shyness isn't a defect you have to fix before you're allowed to belong. It's a different way of moving through the world. A little slower, a little more attentive. Shy people are often the ones who really listen while everyone else is just waiting for their turn. That's a gift, not a weakness, even if it's easy to overlook in all the noise.

We live in a time when loud and important have become almost the same word. Those who post a lot get seen, those who are quiet get overlooked. That creates pressure that's good for nobody, least of all those who already weigh every word three times.

But presence doesn't need a performance. You're allowed to simply be there. Read along, feel along, get a sense of how people tick before you say anything yourself. That's not cowardly. It's like standing in the kitchen at a party for a while, watching, before you dare go into the living room. And let's be honest, the best conversations happen in the kitchen anyway. That's not scientifically proven, but everyone knows it.

Trust grows quietly, in the background. You only notice it once it's already there. At some point you read something and no longer think "Am I allowed?", but simply "Oh, I've got something to say about that". And then you write it. Not because you have to, but because it suddenly feels completely natural. That's how real self-confidence grows. Not with one big leap, but with many small steps where at some point you forget they're steps.

And the quiet counts too. A private message often moves more than a public comment. In private, there's room for "maybe" and "I'm not quite sure". You don't need finished answers. You're allowed to ask questions. Some of the most beautiful friendships I know didn't start with a big entrance, but with a quiet message to someone who was just as unsure.

Send It

So, back to the beginning. To the thumb over the button.

The person you want to write to is a human being. They have good days and bad ones, a full laundry basket and some song they secretly love and never tell anyone about. They're as happy about kind words as you are. Probably even more than you think, because as I said, it's drafty on pedestals.

You're not too small to write to them. Not too boring, not too unimportant, not too anything. You're exactly as tall as everyone else, namely normal-sized, with normal-sized doubts and a committee in your head that's had a bit too much coffee.

Maybe you've got someone in mind right now. The account where you've been leaving hearts for months. The person you'd love to get to know and thought you weren't entitled to.

Then open the message. Write one sentence. Just one. It doesn't have to be clever, or funny, or perfect. "I just wanted to say hello" is enough. Don't read it five times. Don't let the committee vote.

And then press send. ❤️
