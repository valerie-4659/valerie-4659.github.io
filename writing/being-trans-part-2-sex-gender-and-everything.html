I'm quite open about who I am, and that naturally draws attention. Sometimes it's genuine curiosity, sometimes it's comments like "Plz mommy!", and you briefly wonder whether humanity has given up on the concept of a full sentence again.

I'm not here to judge anyone, quite the opposite. If someone is genuinely curious or has questions, they're welcome to ask, and I'm happy to take the time for that. But in those conversations I keep noticing how much gets mixed up. Whether that means I'm into men. Whether it's some kind of femboy thing, just more committed. Whether you only become a "real" woman after surgery. The questions are rarely meant badly, but they show how few people realize how broad and complex this topic actually is. That's why I'm writing this.

This article is mainly based on my own perspective as a trans woman. Still, it's important to me to say right at the start that there are many other identities, expressions, and paths, and each of them is valid and deserves respect.

Part 1 was my own story, how all of this started for me. This part is about everything around it: what the words actually mean, what biology has to say, and above all how to treat each other without it becoming exhausting for everyone involved.

Four Things That Constantly Get Mixed Up

Most of the confusion comes from throwing four very different things into one pot, giving it a good stir, and then wondering why the soup tastes odd.

First there's the body, biological sex: chromosomes, hormones, gonads, and everything you can or can't see from the outside.

Then there's identity, the knowledge of who you are. That's the layer nobody can read from the outside, because it sits inside.

The third layer is expression, how you show yourself. Clothes, hair, makeup, the way you sit, walk and speak.

And finally there's desire, who you love and who you find attractive.

These four are far less tied together than most people think. A trans woman can love men, women, both, or nobody. A man in a dress can be a man through and through who simply likes wearing dresses. A drag queen plays with femininity on stage and often takes the wig off afterwards very contentedly. A femboy lives a feminine expression without seeing himself as a woman. And a woman in a flannel shirt and work boots is still a woman, even if she looks like she could fix your car in ten minutes.

So knowing one of these layers tells you nothing about the others. It's a bit like trying to work out the driver's favorite food from the color of the car.

Biological Sex Is Not Binary

When it comes to the body, a lot of people still think in very simple categories, male or female, and that's the end of it. But biology itself doesn't play along.

The factors that make up biological sex don't always line up as neatly as a pair of socks fresh out of the packet. There isn't really such a thing as "100% male" or "100% female" across all dimensions. Variations exist in chromosomes, far beyond just XX and XY, in hormone levels, and in how a body develops.

A few examples, so this doesn't stay abstract.

Roughly one in 500 to 1,000 men doesn't have XY, but XXY. It's called Klinefelter syndrome, and up to two-thirds of them never find out. They live their whole lives as men, with one extra chromosome, and nobody notices.

On the other side, there are women with XY chromosomes. With complete androgen insensitivity, the body doesn't respond to testosterone and develops as female, and many only find out when their period doesn't come during puberty.

At the 1996 Olympics in Atlanta, 3,387 female athletes were genetically tested. Eight of them carried the gene that was supposed to mean "male", and seven of those had androgen insensitivity. All eight were allowed to compete, and a few years later the IOC dropped universal testing. Biology had simply refused to give a simple answer.

Intersex people show this especially clearly. How many there are depends on who you ask: estimates range from 0.018% to 1.7%, depending on what gets counted. When even science can't agree on a definition, that's a pretty strong hint that two boxes aren't enough. Germany, by the way, has had a third legal gender option, "divers", since the end of 2018, after its Constitutional Court required it.

So even at a purely physical level, sex is better understood as a spectrum of traits rather than a fixed binary.

Gender Is More Than the Body

Beyond biology, there is gender identity, how a person perceives themselves, and that's exactly the part that often gets overlooked.

Being a woman is not automatically defined by specific body parts. It's about how someone experiences themselves from the inside and how they want to exist in the world. I'm speaking from my own perspective as a trans woman, but the same applies to trans men, non-binary people, and many others. Every person defines for themselves how they want to be perceived, and that deserves respect.

Where this inner certainty comes from, nobody knows exactly, and I think it's more honest to say so. There are hints that predisposition plays a role. A large analysis of twin data in 2025 showed that identical twins are much more often both trans than fraternal twins. But far from always, so there's no single "trans gene", rather an interplay nobody fully understands yet.

What we do know fairly well: you can't talk someone out of it. Attempts to make people "unlearn" their gender identity or sexual orientation demonstrably cause harm, and in Germany such conversion treatments on minors have been banned since 2020, as has advertising them. When a state needs a law to protect people from attempts to rewire them, that says a lot about how deep this runs.

So a trans woman does not "become" a woman only after completing a full transition. She already is one the moment she perceives herself that way.

That doesn't mean everyone else has to instantly understand or fully share that perspective, but it does mean that the request should be respected. When someone says: "I am a woman, please address me and treat me as one," that's not a demand for agreement, it's a request for basic respect.

And that's where the difference lies: you don't have to fully relate to something in order to treat someone with respect. I don't understand why people put raisins in potato salad either, and I still greet them politely.

Garters Don't Make a Woman

There's one question I hear especially often, and sometimes I hear a quiet uncertainty in it too: what's actually the difference between being trans and crossdressing?

From the outside it can look the same. Someone goes through life as a man and wears garters, lace, high heels. But wearing garters doesn't automatically make you trans. Many crossdressers are perfectly happy being men. They enjoy the fabric, the forbidden, the thrill, playing with another side, and when the evening is over they contentedly put it all back in the drawer and are fully themselves again. There's nothing wrong with that, and it isn't a preliminary stage of anything.

With being trans, it's the other way round. The woman isn't the costume, she's what was underneath all along. And when a trans woman wears garters, it isn't dressing up, it's an amplifier. The feeling gets louder because the fabric on your skin confirms something that's been true inside for a long time.

How you notice that in yourself, nobody can answer with a test, and I distrust anyone who tries. But there are a few things many trans women eventually recognize in themselves.

The feeling stays when the excitement is gone. The thrill can fade, the lingerie can lie in the wardrobe, and still there's this pull, while doing the dishes, in the supermarket, on a perfectly ordinary Tuesday. It's no longer about the special evening, it's about everyday life.

Taking it off hurts. Not because the lovely moment is over, but because it feels like slipping back into something that doesn't fit. For many crossdressers it's exactly the other way round: the man is home, and the lingerie is the day trip.

You don't just want to be dressed, you want to be seen. Not as a man in pretty things, but as a woman. A "she" from someone else, a feminine name used just like that, and suddenly everything inside goes quiet and warm. There's a word for that feeling, by the way: gender euphoria.

And at some point you're standing in front of the mirror. Maybe freshly made up, maybe in a dress, maybe just with damp hair after the bath. And for a moment it isn't a man looking back who has put something on. A woman is looking back, and the strange thing is that it doesn't feel like a surprise, it feels like a reunion. You're not startled, you recognize her. Sometimes it only takes a few seconds before your head says "but" again. But you don't forget those seconds, and they come back, a little longer each time.

That isn't proof and it isn't a checklist. Some feel it at five, some at fifty, some only after years of thinking it was just crossdressing. That isn't a wrong turn, it's often simply the road on which you slowly approach yourself until you're ready to look.

Even Magnus Hirschfeld, whom we'll get to in a moment, didn't keep the two apart at first. His 1910 book was called "Die Transvestiten", and in it crossdressers and trans people still ended up in the same box. It took decades for science to understand that two people in the same dress can be experiencing something completely different.

None of This Is New

Sometimes I hear that this is all just a trend of the last few years. That always makes me smile a little, because Berlin of all places was further along more than a hundred years ago than some comment sections are today.

From 1909, the Berlin police issued so-called "transvestite passes", at the urging of the physician Magnus Hirschfeld. Anyone with one of those papers in their handbag could walk the streets in the clothes of another gender without being arrested. In 1919, Hirschfeld founded the Institute for Sexual Science in Tiergarten, and trans people worked there not only as patients but also as staff, which was possible almost nowhere else at the time.

One of them was Dora Richter. She had come to Berlin around 1920, had been arrested several times for wearing women's clothing, and found a position in the institute's household. At the institute she was affectionately called "Dörchen". In 1931 she had surgery and is considered the first documented trans woman to undergo a vaginoplasty.

Two years later, it was all over. On 6 May 1933, students looted the institute, and four days later its books and files burned on the Opernplatz. If you know the famous photos of the Nazi book burnings: part of what's going up in flames there is knowledge about people like me.

And that's just Germany. In South Asia, hijras have lived as a gender of their own for centuries, and India's Supreme Court officially recognized them as a third gender in 2014. In Samoa, fa'afafine have been a natural part of family life for generations. So what's new isn't that we exist. What's new, at most, is that we can talk about it again.

The Path of Transition

Transition is not a fixed process with a defined set of steps that everyone has to work through in order. For some, it's mainly social transition, a new name, different pronouns, a different presentation. For others, it also includes medical steps like hormone therapy or surgery. And others consciously choose not to take certain steps, because they don't need them, can't tolerate them, or can't afford them.

There is no fixed endpoint you need to reach to be "truly" a woman, a man, or anything in between. Every path is individual and every decision personal, and above all, no one is less "real" just because they don't take every possible step.

That also goes for so-called passing, whether strangers read you at first glance as who you are. Some blend in seamlessly after a few years, some never quite do, and that often depends more on bone structure, voice and luck than on anything you can control. A woman who isn't immediately recognized as a woman on the street is still one. She just has a harder street to walk.

A Word Like a Name

Language is where respect costs the least and shows the most.

"Trans" is a describing word, like "tall" or "red-haired". That's why it's "trans woman", two words, just like "tall woman". A trans woman is a woman who happens to be trans, not a separate kind of person that needs to be welded into one word.

Then there's the old name, which some call a deadname. For many, it's the name under which they had to be someone they weren't for years. If you use it by accident, correct yourself briefly and move on. Used on purpose, it's a slap with an announcement.

And with pronouns, mistakes happen, especially at the beginning, especially with people who've known someone for a long time. That's human. A quick "sorry, she" is completely enough. Please no ten-minute speech about how hard all of this is for you, because then the trans person ends up doing the comforting, and that's a bit like stepping on someone's foot and then complaining about your sore shoe.

Just Ask

Not long ago someone asked me, honestly and a little helplessly: "How do I even address her?"

My answer was short: just ask her.

That sounds almost too simple, but it's the whole trick. Asking isn't an admission of ignorance, it's a sign of respect. It says: I want to address you correctly, and I consider my guess less important than your answer. Guessing says the opposite: I've already decided what you are.

How do you ask? Briefly, and to the person herself. "How would you like to be addressed?" That's all it takes. No preamble, no three-minute introduction about how you've got nothing against anyone. One on one is kinder than in front of a whole room, and once is plenty.

What not to ask is just as easy to remember: anything you wouldn't ask a female colleague. What's in her underwear. What's been operated on and what hasn't. What the old name was. Whether it might just be a phase. And the classic "So what does your family say about it?", which almost always hits exactly the wound that was not supposed to be the topic of the evening.

Compliments, by the way, are explicitly allowed. "You look great" is wonderful. "You can't even tell" is well meant and still lands like "you fake it well". Just drop the comparison and a real compliment is left over.

If a pronoun slips out wrong: correct it briefly, move on. You're also allowed to practice when the person isn't there, and saying a new name out loud while clearing the table helps more than you'd think.

And one thing that isn't a small matter: who someone is out to is that person's decision. Passing it on isn't gossip, it can cost someone their job, their family, or worse. If you know and others don't, then you simply know, and that's the whole job.

Being curious is fine. You may ask, nobody has to answer. When someone says "I don't talk about that", it isn't a retreat, it's a boundary, and boundaries are not an invitation to dig.

For friends and family there's one more thing: it's the same person. Same jokes, same mess in the car, same taste in terrible films. It's allowed to take time until the name and pronouns sit right, and it's allowed to feel unfamiliar. Just don't let the trans person be the one doing the comforting while you get used to it.

The shortest summary I have: ask, listen, then use what you heard.

Final Thoughts

Not everything fits into simple categories, not people, not gender, and not identity. If you think in rigid boxes and fixed norms, you're often mostly limiting yourself and making life more complicated than it needs to be.

Life is not black and white, it's an endless spectrum of colors, and the most beautiful part is often the transitions in between. See people for what they are, which is people, because in the end it's character that matters.

All of this is the part that happens in your head. But at some point it's Monday morning, and there's a person with a body that doesn't always play along, and a life that keeps going. That's what Part 3 is about.

Until then: when someone tells you their name, use exactly that one. It costs nothing. 💜
